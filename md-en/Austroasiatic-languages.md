The **Austroasiatic languages**[\[note 1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-1) ([/ˌɒstroʊ.eɪʒiˈætɪk, ˌɔː-/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*OSS-troh-ay-zhee-AT-ik, AWSS-*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key")) are a large [language family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_family "Language family") spoken throughout [Mainland Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainland_Southeast_Asia "Mainland Southeast Asia"), [South Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Asia "South Asia") and [East Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asia "East Asia"). These languages are natively spoken by the majority of the population in [Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam "Vietnam") and [Cambodia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambodia "Cambodia"), and by minority populations scattered throughout parts of [Thailand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thailand "Thailand"), [Laos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laos "Laos"), [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India "India"), [Myanmar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myanmar "Myanmar"), [Malaysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysia "Malaysia"), [Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh "Bangladesh"), [Nepal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nepal "Nepal"), and [southern China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_China "Southern China"). Approximately 117 million people speak an Austroasiatic language, of which more than two-thirds are [Vietnamese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_language "Vietnamese language") speakers.[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-2) Of the Austroasiatic languages, only [Vietnamese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_language "Vietnamese language"), [Khmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khmer_language "Khmer language"), and [Mon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mon_language "Mon language") have lengthy, established presences in the historical record. Only two are presently considered to be the [national languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_language "National language") of sovereign states: Vietnamese in Vietnam, and Khmer in Cambodia. The Mon language is a recognized indigenous language in Myanmar and Thailand, while the [Wa language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wa_language "Wa language") is a "recognized national language" in the de facto autonomous [Wa State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wa_State "Wa State") within Myanmar. [Santali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santali_language "Santali language") is one of [the 22 scheduled languages of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eighth_Schedule_to_the_Constitution_of_India "Eighth Schedule to the Constitution of India"). The remainder of the family's languages are spoken by minority groups and have no official status.

*[Ethnologue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnologue "Ethnologue")* identifies 168 Austroasiatic languages. These form thirteen established families (plus perhaps [Shompen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shompen_language "Shompen language"), which is poorly attested, as a fourteenth), which have traditionally been grouped into two, as Mon–Khmer,[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-3) and [Munda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munda_languages "Munda languages"). However, one recent classification posits three groups (Munda, Mon-Khmer, and [Khasi–Khmuic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khasi%E2%80%93Palaungic_languages "Khasi–Palaungic languages")),[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-4) while another has abandoned **Mon–Khmer** as a taxon altogether, making it synonymous with the larger family.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-5)

Austroasiatic languages appear to be the extant [autochthonous languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_language "Indigenous language") in mainland Southeast Asia, with the neighboring [Kra–Dai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kra%E2%80%93Dai_languages "Kra–Dai languages"), [Hmong-Mien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hmong%E2%80%93Mien_languages "Hmong–Mien languages"), [Austronesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austronesian_languages "Austronesian languages"), and [Sino-Tibetan languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Tibetan_languages "Sino-Tibetan languages") having arrived via later migrations.[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-SidwellBlench2011-6)

## Etymology

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Austroasiatic_languages\&action=edit\&section=1 "Edit section: Etymology")]

The name *Austroasiatic* was coined by [Wilhelm Schmidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Schmidt_\(linguist\) "Wilhelm Schmidt (linguist)") ([German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language "German language"): *austroasiatisch*) based on *auster*, the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") word for "South" (but idiosyncratically used by Schmidt to refer to the southeast), and "Asia".[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-7) Despite the literal meaning of its name, only three Austroasiatic branches are actually spoken in South Asia: [Khasic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khasic_languages "Khasic languages"), [Munda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munda_languages "Munda languages"), and [Nicobarese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicobarese_languages "Nicobarese languages").

## Typology

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Austroasiatic_languages\&action=edit\&section=2 "Edit section: Typology")]

Regarding word structure, Austroasiatic languages are well known for having an iambic ["sesquisyllabic"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sesquisyllable "Sesquisyllable") pattern, with basic nouns and verbs consisting of an initial, unstressed, reduced [minor syllable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_syllable "Minor syllable") followed by a stressed, full syllable.[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAlves2014524-8) This reduction of presyllables has led to a variety of phonological shapes of the same original Proto-Austroasiatic prefixes, such as the causative prefix, ranging from CVC syllables to consonant clusters to single consonants among the modern languages.[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAlves2014526-9) As for word formation, most Austroasiatic languages have a variety of derivational prefixes, many have [infixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infix "Infix"), but suffixes are almost completely non-existent in most branches except Munda, and a few specialized exceptions in other Austroasiatic branches.[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-10)

The Austroasiatic languages are further characterized as having unusually large vowel inventories and employing some sort of [register](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Register_\(phonology\) "Register (phonology)") contrast, either between [modal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_voice "Modal voice") (normal) voice and [breathy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breathy_voice "Breathy voice") (lax) voice or between modal voice and [creaky voice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creaky_voice "Creaky voice").[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-11) Languages in the Pearic branch and some in the Vietic branch can have a three- or even four-way voicing contrast.

However, some Austroasiatic languages have lost the register contrast by evolving more diphthongs or in a few cases, such as Vietnamese, [tonogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonogenesis "Tonogenesis"). Vietnamese has been so heavily influenced by Chinese that its original Austroasiatic phonological quality is obscured and now resembles that of South Chinese languages, whereas Khmer, which had more influence from Sanskrit, has retained a more typically Austroasiatic structure.

## Internal classification

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Austroasiatic_languages\&action=edit\&section=4 "Edit section: Internal classification")]

Linguists traditionally recognize two primary divisions of Austroasiatic: the [Mon–Khmer languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mon%E2%80%93Khmer_languages "Mon–Khmer languages") of [Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Asia "Southeast Asia"), [Northeast India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North-East_India "North-East India") and the [Nicobar Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicobar_Islands "Nicobar Islands"), and the [Munda languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munda_languages "Munda languages") of [East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_India "East India") and [Central India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_India "Central India") and parts of [Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh "Bangladesh") and [Nepal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nepal "Nepal"). However, no evidence for this classification has ever been published.

Each of the families that is written in boldface type below is accepted as a valid clade.\[*[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*] By contrast, the relationships *between* these families within Austroasiatic are debated. In addition to the traditional classification, two recent proposals are given, neither of which accepts traditional "Mon–Khmer" as a valid unit. However, little of the data used for competing classifications has ever been published, and therefore cannot be evaluated by peer review.

In addition, there are suggestions that additional branches of Austroasiatic might be preserved in substrata of [Acehnese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acehnese_language "Acehnese language") in Sumatra (Diffloth), the [Chamic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamic_languages "Chamic languages") of Vietnam, and the [Land Dayak languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_Dayak_languages "Land Dayak languages") of Borneo (Adelaar 1995).[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-13)

### Sidwell (2009–2015)

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Austroasiatic_languages\&action=edit\&section=8 "Edit section: Sidwell (2009–2015)")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7c/Mekong_river_basin.png/400px-Mekong_river_basin.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Mekong_river_basin.png)

[Paul Sidwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Sidwell "Paul Sidwell") and [Roger Blench](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Blench "Roger Blench") propose that the Austroasiatic phylum dispersed via the [Mekong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mekong "Mekong") River [drainage basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_basin "Drainage basin").

[Paul Sidwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Sidwell "Paul Sidwell") (2009), in a [lexicostatistical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexicostatistical "Lexicostatistical") comparison of 36 languages which are well known enough to exclude loanwords, finds little evidence for internal branching, though he did find an area of increased contact between the Bahnaric and Katuic languages, such that languages of all branches apart from the geographically distant [Munda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munda_languages "Munda languages") and Nicobarese show greater similarity to Bahnaric and Katuic the closer they are to those branches, without any noticeable innovations common to Bahnaric and Katuic.

He therefore takes the conservative view that the thirteen branches of Austroasiatic should be treated as equidistant on current evidence. Sidwell & [Blench](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Blench "Roger Blench") (2011) discuss this proposal in more detail, and note that there is good evidence for a Khasi–Palaungic node, which could also possibly be closely related to Khmuic.[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-SidwellBlench2011-6)

If this would the case, Sidwell & Blench suggest that Khasic may have been an early offshoot of Palaungic that had spread westward. Sidwell & Blench (2011) suggest [Shompen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shompen_language "Shompen language") as an additional branch, and believe that a Vieto-Katuic connection is worth investigating. In general, however, the family is thought to have diversified too quickly for a deeply nested structure to have developed, since Proto-Austroasiatic speakers are believed by Sidwell to have radiated out from the central [Mekong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mekong "Mekong") river valley relatively quickly.

Subsequently, Sidwell (2015a: 179)[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-15) proposed that [Nicobarese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicobarese_languages "Nicobarese languages") subgroups with [Aslian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aslian_languages "Aslian languages"), just as how Khasian and Palaungic subgroup with each other.

A subsequent computational phylogenetic analysis (Sidwell 2015b)[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-17) suggests that Austroasiatic branches may have a loosely nested structure rather than a completely rake-like structure, with an east–west division (consisting of Munda, Khasic, Palaungic, and Khmuic forming a western group as opposed to all of the other branches) occurring possibly as early as 7,000 years before present. However, he still considers the subbranching dubious.

Integrating computational phylogenetic linguistics with recent archaeological findings, Paul Sidwell (2015c)[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Sidwell2015-18) further expanded his Mekong riverine hypothesis by proposing that Austroasiatic had ultimately expanded into [Indochina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indochina "Indochina") from the [Lingnan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingnan "Lingnan") area of [southern China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_China "Southern China"), with the subsequent Mekong riverine dispersal taking place after the initial arrival of Neolithic farmers from southern China.

Sidwell (2015c) tentatively suggests that Austroasiatic may have begun to split up 5,000 years B.P. during the [Neolithic transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_transition "Neolithic transition") era of [mainland Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainland_Southeast_Asia "Mainland Southeast Asia"), with all the major branches of Austroasiatic formed by 4,000 B.P. Austroasiatic would have had two possible dispersal routes from the western periphery of the [Pearl River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_River_\(China\) "Pearl River (China)") watershed of [Lingnan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingnan "Lingnan"), which would have been either a coastal route down the coast of Vietnam, or downstream through the [Mekong River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mekong_River "Mekong River") via [Yunnan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yunnan "Yunnan").[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Sidwell2015-18) Both the reconstructed lexicon of Proto-Austroasiatic and the archaeological record clearly show that early Austroasiatic speakers around 4,000 B.P. cultivated rice and [millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millet "Millet"), kept livestock such as dogs, pigs, and chickens, and thrived mostly in estuarine rather than coastal environments.[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Sidwell2015-18)

At 4,500 B.P., this "Neolithic package" suddenly arrived in Indochina from the Lingnan area without cereal grains and displaced the earlier pre-Neolithic hunter-gatherer cultures, with grain husks found in northern Indochina by 4,100 B.P. and in southern Indochina by 3,800 B.P.[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Sidwell2015-18) However, Sidwell (2015c) found that iron is not reconstructable in Proto-Austroasiatic, since each Austroasiatic branch has different terms for iron that had been borrowed relatively lately from Tai, Chinese, Tibetan, Malay, and other languages.

During the [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age "Iron Age") about 2,500 B.P., relatively young Austroasiatic branches in Indochina such as [Vietic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietic_languages "Vietic languages"), [Katuic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katuic_languages "Katuic languages"), [Pearic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearic_languages "Pearic languages"), and [Khmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khmer_language "Khmer language") were formed, while the more internally diverse [Bahnaric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahnaric_languages "Bahnaric languages") branch (dating to about 3,000 B.P.) underwent more extensive internal diversification.[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Sidwell2015-18) By the Iron Age, all of the Austroasiatic branches were more or less in their present-day locations, with most of the diversification within Austroasiatic taking place during the Iron Age.[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Sidwell2015-18)

Paul Sidwell (2018)[\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-19) considers the Austroasiatic language family to have rapidly diversified around 4,000 years B.P. during the arrival of rice agriculture in Indochina, but notes that the origin of Proto-Austroasiatic itself is older than that date. The lexicon of Proto-Austroasiatic can be divided into an early and late stratum. The early stratum consists of basic lexicon including body parts, animal names, natural features, and pronouns, while the names of cultural items (agriculture terms and words for cultural artifacts, which are reconstructible in Proto-Austroasiatic) form part of the later stratum.

[Roger Blench](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Blench "Roger Blench") (2017)[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Blench2017-20) suggests that vocabulary related to aquatic subsistence strategies (such as boats, waterways, river fauna, and fish capture techniques) can be reconstructed for Proto-Austroasiatic. Blench (2017) finds widespread Austroasiatic roots for 'river, valley', 'boat', 'fish', 'catfish sp.', 'eel', 'prawn', 'shrimp' (Central Austroasiatic), 'crab', 'tortoise', 'turtle', 'otter', 'crocodile', 'heron, fishing bird', and 'fish trap'. Archaeological evidence for the presence of agriculture in northern [Indochina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indochina "Indochina") (northern Vietnam, Laos, and other nearby areas) dates back to only about 4,000 years ago (2,000 BC), with agriculture ultimately being introduced from further up to the north in the Yangtze valley where it has been dated to 6,000 B.P.[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Blench2017-20)

Sidwell (2022)[\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Sidwell_JSEALS_Special_8-21)[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Sidwell2021-22) proposes that the locus of Proto-Austroasiatic was in the [Red River Delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_River_Delta "Red River Delta") area about 4,000-4,500 years before present, instead of the Middle Mekong as he had previously proposed. Austroasiatic dispersed coastal maritime routes and also upstream through river valleys. Khmuic, Palaungic, and Khasic resulted from a westward dispersal that ultimately came from the Red River valley. Based on their current distributions, about half of all Austroasiatic branches (including Nicobaric and Munda) can be traced to coastal maritime dispersals.

Hence, this points to a relatively late riverine dispersal of Austroasiatic as compared to [Sino-Tibetan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Tibetan_languages "Sino-Tibetan languages"), whose speakers had a distinct non-riverine culture. In addition to living an aquatic-based lifestyle, early Austroasiatic speakers would have also had access to livestock, crops, and newer types of watercraft. As early Austroasiatic speakers dispersed rapidly via waterways, they would have encountered speakers of older language families who were already settled in the area, such as Sino-Tibetan.[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Blench2017-20)

### Possible extinct branches

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Austroasiatic_languages\&action=edit\&section=10 "Edit section: Possible extinct branches")]

[Roger Blench](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Blench "Roger Blench") (2009)[\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-25) also proposes that there might have been other primary branches of Austroasiatic that are now extinct, based on [substrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratum_\(linguistics\) "Stratum (linguistics)") evidence in modern-day languages.

- **Pre-[Chamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamic_language "Chamic language") languages** (the languages of coastal Vietnam before the Chamic migrations). Chamic has various Austroasiatic loanwords that cannot be clearly traced to existing Austroasiatic branches (Sidwell 2006, 2007).[\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Sidwell2006-26)[\[25\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Sidwell2007-27) Larish (1999)[\[26\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Larish1999-28) also notes that [Moklenic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moklenic_languages "Moklenic languages") contain many Austroasiatic loanwords, some of which are similar to the ones found in Chamic.
- **[Acehnese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acehnese_language "Acehnese language") substratum** (Sidwell 2006).[\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Sidwell2006-26) Acehnese has many basic words that are of Austroasiatic origin, suggesting that either Austronesian speakers have absorbed earlier Austroasiatic residents in northern Sumatra, or that words might have been borrowed from Austroasiatic languages in southern Vietnam – or perhaps a combination of both. Sidwell (2006) argues that Acehnese and Chamic had often borrowed Austroasiatic words independently of each other, while some Austroasiatic words can be traced back to Proto-Aceh-Chamic. Sidwell (2006) accepts that Acehnese and Chamic are related, but that they had separated from each other before Chamic had borrowed most of its Austroasiatic lexicon.
- **[Bornean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bornean_languages "Bornean languages") substrate languages** (Blench 2010).[\[27\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-29) Blench cites Austroasiatic-origin words in modern-day Bornean branches such as [Land Dayak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_Dayak_languages "Land Dayak languages") ([Bidayuh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bidayuh_languages "Bidayuh languages"), [Dayak Bakatiq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakati%CA%BC_language "Bakatiʼ language"), etc.), [Dusunic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dusunic_languages "Dusunic languages") ([Central Dusun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dusun_language "Dusun language"), [Visayan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunei_Bisaya_language "Brunei Bisaya language"), etc.), [Kayan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kayan%E2%80%93Murik_languages "Kayan–Murik languages"), and [Kenyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenyah_languages "Kenyah languages"), noting especially resemblances with [Aslian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aslian_languages "Aslian languages"). As further evidence for his proposal, Blench also cites ethnographic evidence such as musical instruments in Borneo shared in common with Austroasiatic-speaking groups in mainland Southeast Asia. Adelaar (1995)[\[28\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-30) has also noticed phonological and lexical similarities between [Land Dayak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_Dayak_languages "Land Dayak languages") and [Aslian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aslian_languages "Aslian languages"). Kaufman (2018) presents dozens of lexical comparisons showing similarities between various Bornean and Austroasiatic languages.[\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-31)
- **[Lepcha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepcha_language "Lepcha language") substratum** ("***Rongic***").[\[30\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-32) Many words of Austroasiatic origin have been noticed in [Lepcha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepcha_language "Lepcha language"), suggesting a [Sino-Tibetan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Tibetan_languages "Sino-Tibetan languages") superstrate laid over an Austroasiatic substrate. Blench (2013) calls this branch "*Rongic*" based on the Lepcha autonym *Róng*.

Other languages with proposed Austroasiatic substrata are:

- **[Jiamao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiamao_language "Jiamao language")**, based on evidence from the register system of Jiamao, a [Hlai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hlai_languages "Hlai languages") language (Thurgood 1992).[\[31\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-33) Jiamao is known for its highly aberrant vocabulary in relation to other [Hlai languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hlai_languages "Hlai languages").
- **[Kerinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerinci_language "Kerinci language")**: van Reijn (1974)[\[32\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-34) notes that Kerinci, a [Malayic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malayic_languages "Malayic languages") language of central [Sumatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumatra "Sumatra"), shares many phonological similarities with Austroasiatic languages, such as [sesquisyllabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sesquisyllabic "Sesquisyllabic") word structure and vowel inventory.

John Peterson (2017)[\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-35) suggests that "pre-[Munda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munda_languages "Munda languages")" (early languages related to Proto-Munda) languages may have once dominated the eastern [Indo-Gangetic Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Gangetic_Plain "Indo-Gangetic Plain"), and were then absorbed by Indo-Aryan languages at an early date as Indo-Aryan spread east. Peterson notes that eastern [Indo-Aryan languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Aryan_languages "Indo-Aryan languages") display many morphosyntactic features similar to those of Munda languages, while western Indo-Aryan languages do not.

## Writing systems

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Austroasiatic_languages\&action=edit\&section=11 "Edit section: Writing systems")]

Other than Latin-based alphabets, many Austroasiatic languages are written with the [Khmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khmer_alphabet "Khmer alphabet"), [Thai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thai_alphabet "Thai alphabet"), [Lao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lao_alphabet "Lao alphabet"), and [Burmese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burmese_alphabet "Burmese alphabet") alphabets. Vietnamese divergently had an indigenous script based on Chinese logographic writing. This has since been supplanted by the Latin alphabet in the 20th century. The following are examples of past-used alphabets or current alphabets of Austroasiatic languages.

- [Chữ Nôm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%E1%BB%AF_N%C3%B4m "Chữ Nôm")[\[34\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-36)
- [Khmer alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khmer_alphabet "Khmer alphabet")[\[35\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-37)
- [Khom script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khom_script_\(Ong_Kommadam\) "Khom script (Ong Kommadam)") (used for a short period in the early 20th century for indigenous languages in Laos)
- [Old Mon script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Mon_script "Old Mon script")
- [Mon script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mon_language#Alphabet "Mon language")
- [Pahawh Hmong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pahawh_Hmong "Pahawh Hmong") was once used to write [Khmu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khmu_language "Khmu language"), under the name "Pahawh Khmu"
- [Tai Le](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tai_Le_script "Tai Le script") ([Palaung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palaung_language "Palaung language"), [Blang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blang_language "Blang language"))
- [Tai Tham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tai_Tham "Tai Tham") ([Blang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blang_language "Blang language"))
- [Ol Chiki alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ol_Chiki_alphabet "Ol Chiki alphabet") ([Santali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santali_language "Santali language") alphabet)[\[36\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-38)

## External relations

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Austroasiatic_languages\&action=edit\&section=12 "Edit section: External relations")]

### Austric languages

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Austroasiatic_languages\&action=edit\&section=13 "Edit section: Austric languages")]

Main article: [Austric languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austric_languages "Austric languages")

Austroasiatic is an integral part of the controversial [Austric hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austric_languages "Austric languages"), which also includes the [Austronesian languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austronesian_languages "Austronesian languages"), and in some proposals also the [Kra–Dai languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kra%E2%80%93Dai_languages "Kra–Dai languages") and the [Hmong–Mien languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hmong%E2%80%93Mien_languages "Hmong–Mien languages").[\[39\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-41)

### Hmong-Mien

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Austroasiatic_languages\&action=edit\&section=14 "Edit section: Hmong-Mien")]

[Several lexical resemblances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Hmong%E2%80%93Mien_language#Austroasiatic "Proto-Hmong–Mien language") are found between the Hmong-Mien and Austroasiatic language families (Ratliff 2010), some of which had earlier been proposed by [Haudricourt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andr%C3%A9-Georges_Haudricourt "André-Georges Haudricourt") (1951). This could imply a relation or early language contact along the [Yangtze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yangtze "Yangtze").[\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-42)

According to Cai (et al. 2011), [Hmong–Mien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hmong%E2%80%93Mien_languages "Hmong–Mien languages") people are *genetically* related to Austroasiatic speakers, and their languages were heavily influenced by [Sino-Tibetan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Tibetan_languages "Sino-Tibetan languages"), especially [Tibeto-Burman languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibeto-Burman_languages "Tibeto-Burman languages").[\[41\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-43)

### Indo-Aryan languages

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Austroasiatic_languages\&action=edit\&section=15 "Edit section: Indo-Aryan languages")]

It is suggested that the Austroasiatic languages have some influence on [Indo-Aryan languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Aryan_languages "Indo-Aryan languages") including [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit "Sanskrit") and middle Indo-Aryan languages. Indian linguist [Suniti Kumar Chatterji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suniti_Kumar_Chatterji "Suniti Kumar Chatterji") pointed that a specific number of substantives in languages such as [Hindi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindi "Hindi"), [Punjabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punjabi_language "Punjabi language") and [Bengali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengali_language "Bengali language") were borrowed from [Munda languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munda_languages "Munda languages"). Additionally, French linguist [Jean Przyluski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Przyluski "Jean Przyluski") suggested a similarity between the tales from the Austroasiatic realm and the Indian mythological stories of [Matsyagandha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matsyagandha "Matsyagandha") (Satyavati from *[Mahabharata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahabharata "Mahabharata")*) and the [Nāgas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N%C4%81ga "Nāga").[\[42\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-44)

## Austroasiatic migrations and archaeogenetics

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Austroasiatic_languages\&action=edit\&section=16 "Edit section: Austroasiatic migrations and archaeogenetics")]

See also: [Peopling of Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peopling_of_Southeast_Asia "Peopling of Southeast Asia") and [Ancient Southern East Asian § Neolithic expansion into Southeast Asia and Oceania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Southern_East_Asian#Neolithic_expansion_into_Southeast_Asia_and_Oceania "Ancient Southern East Asian")

[Mitsuru Sakitani](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%B4%8E%E8%B0%B7%E6%BA%80 "ja:崎谷満") suggests that [Haplogroup O1b1](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Haplogroup_O-M95\&action=edit\&redlink=1 "Haplogroup O-M95 (page does not exist)"), which is common in Austroasiatic people and some other ethnic groups in [southern China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_China "Southern China"), and haplogroup O1b2, which is common in today's [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_people "Japanese people") and [Koreans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koreans "Koreans"), are the carriers of early rice agriculture from southern China.[\[43\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-45) Another study suggests that the haplogroup O1b1 is the major Austroasiatic paternal lineage and O1b2 the "para-Austroasiatic" lineage of the [Koreans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koreans "Koreans") and [Yayoi people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yayoi_people "Yayoi people").[\[44\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-46)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/57/The_proposed_route_of_Austroasiatic_and_Austronesian_migration_into_Indonesia_and_the_geographic_distribution_of_sites_that_have_produced_red-slipped_and_cord-marked_pottery.png/220px-thumbnail.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:The_proposed_route_of_Austroasiatic_and_Austronesian_migration_into_Indonesia_and_the_geographic_distribution_of_sites_that_have_produced_red-slipped_and_cord-marked_pottery.png)

The Austroasiatic migration route began earlier than the Austronesian expansion, but later migrations of Austronesians resulted in the assimilation of the pre-Austronesian Austroasiatic populations.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5b/Proto-Austroasiatic_migration.png/220px-Proto-Austroasiatic_migration.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Proto-Austroasiatic_migration.png)

Austroasiatic possible migration routes

A full genomic study by Lipson et al. (2018) identified a characteristic lineage that can be associated with the spread of Austroasiatic languages in Southeast Asia and which can be traced back to remains of Neolithic farmers from [Mán Bạc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A1n_B%E1%BA%A1c "Mán Bạc") (c. 2000 BCE) in the [Red River Delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_River_Delta "Red River Delta") in northern Vietnam, and to closely related [Ban Chiang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ban_Chiang "Ban Chiang") and Vat Komnou remains in [Thailand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thailand "Thailand") and [Cambodia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambodia "Cambodia") respectively. This Austroasiatic lineage can be modeled as a sister group of the [Austronesian peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austronesian_peoples "Austronesian peoples") with significant admixture (ca. 30%) from a deeply diverging eastern Eurasian source (modeled by the authors as sharing some genetic drift with the [Onge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onge_people "Onge people"), a modern [Andamanese hunter-gatherer group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andamanese_peoples "Andamanese peoples")) and which is ancestral to modern Austroasiatic-speaking groups of Southeast Asia such as the [Mlabri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mlabri_people "Mlabri people") and the [Nicobarese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicobarese_people "Nicobarese people"), and partially to the Austroasiatic Munda-speaking groups of South Asia (*e.g.* the [Juang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juang_people "Juang people")). Significant levels of Austroasiatic ancestry were also found in Austronesian-speaking groups of [Sumatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumatra "Sumatra"), [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java "Java"), and [Borneo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borneo "Borneo").[\[45\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-47)[\[note 3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-49) Austroasiatic-speaking groups in southern [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China "China") (such as the [Wa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wa_people "Wa people") and [Blang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blang_people "Blang people") in [Yunnan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yunnan "Yunnan")) predominatly carry the same Mainland Southeast Asian Neolithic farmer ancestry, but with additional geneflow from northern and southern East Asian lineages that can be associated with the spread of [Tibeto-Burman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibeto-Burman_languages "Tibeto-Burman languages") and [Kra-Dai languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kra-Dai_languages "Kra-Dai languages"), respectively.[\[47\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-50)

### Migration into India

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Austroasiatic_languages\&action=edit\&section=17 "Edit section: Migration into India")]

According to Chaubey et al., "Austro-Asiatic speakers in India today are derived from dispersal from [Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Asia "Southeast Asia"), followed by extensive sex-specific admixture with local Indian populations."[\[48\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChaubeyMetspaluChoiMagi20101013-51) According to Riccio et al., the [Munda peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munda_peoples "Munda peoples") are likely descended from Austroasiatic migrants from Southeast Asia.[\[49\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-52)

According to Zhang et al., Austroasiatic migrations from Southeast Asia into India took place after the [Last Glacial Maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Glacial_Maximum "Last Glacial Maximum"), circa 10,000 years ago.[\[50\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-FOOTNOTEZhang2015-53) Arunkumar et al., suggest Austroasiatic migrations from Southeast Asia occurred into Northeast India 5.2 ± 0.6 kya and into East India 4.3 ± 0.2 kya.[\[51\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-54)

According to Tagore et al., the ancestors of present-day Austroasiatic peoples lived in diverse territories, from central India to mainland Southeast Asia. Austroasiatic Indians and Malaysians also shared common ancestry until about 10.5 kya. Around 7 kya, East Asian farmers migrated to mainland Southeast Asia and intermixed with local Austroasiatic hunter-gatherers. Continuous migration eventually led to the fragmentation and isolation of the latter. East Asian ancestry was not introduced in Austroasiatic Indians, who intermixed with locals with [Ancestral South Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancestral_South_Indian "Ancestral South Indian") ancestry.[\[52\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-55)
