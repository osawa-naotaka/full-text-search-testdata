An **acid** is a [molecule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule "Molecule") or [ion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion "Ion") capable of either donating a [proton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton "Proton") (i.e. hydrogen ion, H+), known as a [Brønsted–Lowry acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Br%C3%B8nsted%E2%80%93Lowry_acid%E2%80%93base_theory "Brønsted–Lowry acid–base theory"), or forming a [covalent bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covalent_bond "Covalent bond") with an [electron pair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_pair "Electron pair"), known as a [Lewis acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_acid "Lewis acid").[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-IUPAC_acid-1)

The first category of acids are the proton donors, or [Brønsted–Lowry acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Br%C3%B8nsted%E2%80%93Lowry_acid%E2%80%93base_theory "Brønsted–Lowry acid–base theory"). In the special case of [aqueous solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueous_solution "Aqueous solution"), proton donors form the [hydronium ion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydronium_ion "Hydronium ion") H3O+ and are known as [Arrhenius acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid%E2%80%93base_reaction#Arrhenius_theory "Acid–base reaction"). [Brønsted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Nicolaus_Br%C3%B8nsted "Johannes Nicolaus Brønsted") and [Lowry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Martin_Lowry "Thomas Martin Lowry") generalized the Arrhenius theory to include non-aqueous [solvents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvent "Solvent"). A Brønsted or Arrhenius acid usually contains a hydrogen atom bonded to a chemical structure that is still energetically favorable after loss of H+.

Aqueous Arrhenius acids have characteristic properties that provide a practical description of an acid.[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-2) Acids form aqueous solutions with a sour taste, can turn blue [litmus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Litmus "Litmus") red, and react with [bases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_\(chemistry\) "Base (chemistry)") and certain metals (like [calcium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium "Calcium")) to form [salts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_\(chemistry\) "Salt (chemistry)"). The word *acid* is derived from the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") *acidus*, meaning 'sour'.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-3) An aqueous solution of an acid has a [pH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH "PH") less than 7 and is colloquially also referred to as "acid" (as in "dissolved in acid"), while the strict definition refers only to the [solute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solute "Solute").[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-IUPAC_acid-1) A lower pH means a higher **acidity**, and thus a higher concentration of [positive hydrogen ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydron_\(chemistry\) "Hydron (chemistry)") in the solution. Chemicals or substances having the property of an acid are said to be **acidic**.

Common aqueous acids include [hydrochloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrochloric_acid "Hydrochloric acid") (a solution of [hydrogen chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_chloride "Hydrogen chloride") that is found in [gastric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastric_acid "Gastric acid") in the stomach and activates [digestive enzymes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digestive_enzymes "Digestive enzymes")), [acetic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetic_acid "Acetic acid") (vinegar is a dilute aqueous solution of this liquid), [sulfuric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfuric_acid "Sulfuric acid") (used in [car batteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Car_battery "Car battery")), and [citric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citric_acid "Citric acid") (found in citrus fruits). As these examples show, acids (in the colloquial sense) can be solutions or pure substances, and can be derived from acids (in the strict[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-IUPAC_acid-1) sense) that are solids, liquids, or gases. [Strong acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_strength "Acid strength") and some concentrated weak acids are [corrosive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrosive_substance "Corrosive substance"), but there are exceptions such as [carboranes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carborane "Carborane") and [boric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boric_acid "Boric acid").

The second category of acids are [Lewis acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_acids_and_bases "Lewis acids and bases"), which form a covalent bond with an electron pair. An example is [boron trifluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron_trifluoride "Boron trifluoride") (BF3), whose boron atom has a vacant [orbital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_orbital "Atomic orbital") that can form a covalent bond by sharing a lone pair of electrons on an atom in a base, for example the nitrogen atom in [ammonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia "Ammonia") (NH3). [Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_N._Lewis "Gilbert N. Lewis") considered this as a generalization of the Brønsted definition, so that an acid is a chemical species that accepts electron pairs either directly *or* by releasing protons (H+) into the solution, which then accept electron pairs. Hydrogen chloride, acetic acid, and most other Brønsted–Lowry acids cannot form a covalent bond with an electron pair, however, and are therefore not Lewis acids.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Oxtoby8th-4) Conversely, many Lewis acids are not Arrhenius or Brønsted–Lowry acids. In modern terminology, an *acid* is implicitly a Brønsted acid and not a Lewis acid, since chemists almost always refer to a Lewis acid explicitly as such.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Oxtoby8th-4)

## Definitions and concepts

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=1 "Edit section: Definitions and concepts")]

Main article: [Acid–base reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid%E2%80%93base_reaction "Acid–base reaction")

Modern definitions are concerned with the fundamental chemical reactions common to all acids.

Most acids encountered in everyday life are [aqueous solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueous_solutions "Aqueous solutions"), or can be dissolved in water, so the Arrhenius and Brønsted–Lowry definitions are the most relevant.

The Brønsted–Lowry definition is the most widely used definition; unless otherwise specified, acid–base reactions are assumed to involve the transfer of a proton (H+) from an acid to a base.

Hydronium ions are acids according to all three definitions. Although alcohols and amines can be Brønsted–Lowry acids, they can also function as [Lewis bases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_base "Lewis base") due to the lone pairs of electrons on their oxygen and nitrogen atoms.

### Arrhenius acids

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=2 "Edit section: Arrhenius acids")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6c/Arrhenius2.jpg/150px-Arrhenius2.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Arrhenius2.jpg)

Svante Arrhenius

In 1884, [Svante Arrhenius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Svante_Arrhenius "Svante Arrhenius") attributed the properties of acidity to [hydrogen ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_ion "Hydrogen ion") (H+), later described as [protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton#Hydrogen_ion "Proton") or [hydrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydron_\(chemistry\) "Hydron (chemistry)"). An **Arrhenius acid** is a substance that, when added to water, increases the concentration of H+ ions in the water.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Oxtoby8th-4)[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Ebbing-5) Chemists often write H+(*aq*) and refer to the [hydrogen ion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_ion "Hydrogen ion") when describing acid–base reactions but the free hydrogen nucleus, a [proton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton "Proton"), does not exist alone in water, it exists as the **hydronium ion** (H3O+) or other forms (H5O2+, H9O4+). Thus, an Arrhenius acid can also be described as a substance that increases the concentration of hydronium ions when added to water. Examples include molecular substances such as hydrogen chloride and acetic acid.

An Arrhenius [base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_\(chemistry\) "Base (chemistry)"), on the other hand, is a substance that increases the concentration of [hydroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroxide "Hydroxide") (OH−) ions when dissolved in water. This decreases the concentration of hydronium because the ions react to form H2O molecules:

- H3O+\
  (aq) + OH−\
  (aq) ⇌ H2O(liq) + H2O(liq)

Due to this equilibrium, any increase in the concentration of hydronium is accompanied by a decrease in the concentration of hydroxide. Thus, an Arrhenius acid could also be said to be one that decreases hydroxide concentration, while an Arrhenius base increases it.

In an acidic solution, the concentration of hydronium ions is greater than 10−7 [moles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_\(unit\) "Mole (unit)") per liter. Since pH is defined as the negative logarithm of the concentration of hydronium ions, acidic solutions thus have a pH of less than 7.

### Brønsted–Lowry acids

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=3 "Edit section: Brønsted–Lowry acids")]

Main article: [Brønsted–Lowry acid–base theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Br%C3%B8nsted%E2%80%93Lowry_acid%E2%80%93base_theory "Brønsted–Lowry acid–base theory")

[![Acetic acid, CH3COOH, is composed of a methyl group, CH3, bound chemically to a carboxylate group, COOH. The carboxylate group can lose a proton and donate it to a water molecule, H20, leaving behind an acetate anion CH3COO- and creating a hydronium cation H3O. This is an equilibrium reaction, so the reverse process can also take place.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Acetic-acid-dissociation-3D-balls.png/350px-Acetic-acid-dissociation-3D-balls.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Acetic-acid-dissociation-3D-balls.png)

[Acetic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetic_acid "Acetic acid"), a [weak acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_acid "Weak acid"), donates a proton (hydrogen ion, highlighted in green) to water in an equilibrium reaction to give the [acetate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetate "Acetate") ion and the [hydronium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydronium "Hydronium") ion. Red: oxygen, black: carbon, white: hydrogen.

While the Arrhenius concept is useful for describing many reactions, it is also quite limited in its scope. In 1923, chemists [Johannes Nicolaus Brønsted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Nicolaus_Br%C3%B8nsted "Johannes Nicolaus Brønsted") and [Thomas Martin Lowry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Martin_Lowry "Thomas Martin Lowry") independently recognized that acid–base reactions involve the transfer of a proton. A **Brønsted–Lowry acid** (or simply Brønsted acid) is a species that donates a proton to a Brønsted–Lowry base.[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Ebbing-5) Brønsted–Lowry acid–base theory has several advantages over Arrhenius theory. Consider the following reactions of [acetic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetic_acid "Acetic acid") (CH3COOH), the [organic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_acid "Organic acid") that gives vinegar its characteristic taste:

- - CH3COOH + H2O ⇌ CH3COO− + H3O+
  - CH3COOH + NH3 ⇌ CH3COO− + NH+4

Both theories easily describe the first reaction: CH3COOH acts as an Arrhenius acid because it acts as a source of H3O+ when dissolved in water, and it acts as a Brønsted acid by donating a proton to water. In the second example CH3COOH undergoes the same transformation, in this case donating a proton to ammonia (NH3), but does not relate to the Arrhenius definition of an acid because the reaction does not produce hydronium. Nevertheless, CH3COOH is both an Arrhenius and a Brønsted–Lowry acid.

Brønsted–Lowry theory can be used to describe reactions of [molecular compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule "Molecule") in nonaqueous solution or the gas phase. [Hydrogen chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_chloride "Hydrogen chloride") (HCl) and ammonia combine under several different conditions to form [ammonium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonium_chloride "Ammonium chloride"), NH4Cl. In aqueous solution HCl behaves as [hydrochloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrochloric_acid "Hydrochloric acid") and exists as hydronium and chloride ions. The following reactions illustrate the limitations of Arrhenius's definition:

1. H3O+\
   (aq) + Cl−\
   (aq) + NH3 → Cl−\
   (aq) + NH+\
   4(aq) + H2O
2. HCl(benzene) + NH3(benzene) → NH4Cl(s)
3. HCl(g) + NH3(g) → NH4Cl(s)

As with the acetic acid reactions, both definitions work for the first example, where water is the solvent and hydronium ion is formed by the HCl solute. The next two reactions do not involve the formation of ions but are still proton-transfer reactions. In the second reaction hydrogen chloride and ammonia (dissolved in [benzene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzene "Benzene")) react to form solid ammonium chloride in a benzene solvent and in the third gaseous HCl and NH3 combine to form the solid.

### Lewis acids

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=4 "Edit section: Lewis acids")]

Main article: [Lewis acids and bases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_acids_and_bases "Lewis acids and bases")

A third, only marginally related concept was proposed in 1923 by [Gilbert N. Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_N._Lewis "Gilbert N. Lewis"), which includes reactions with acid–base characteristics that do not involve a proton transfer. A **Lewis acid** is a species that accepts a pair of electrons from another species; in other words, it is an electron pair acceptor.[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Ebbing-5) Brønsted acid–base reactions are proton transfer reactions while Lewis acid–base reactions are electron pair transfers. Many Lewis acids are not Brønsted–Lowry acids. Contrast how the following reactions are described in terms of acid–base chemistry:

- [![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4d/LewisAcid.png/374px-LewisAcid.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:LewisAcid.png)

In the first reaction a [fluoride ion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluoride "Fluoride"), F−, gives up an [electron pair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lone_pair "Lone pair") to [boron trifluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron_trifluoride "Boron trifluoride") to form the product [tetrafluoroborate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrafluoroborate "Tetrafluoroborate"). Fluoride "loses" a pair of [valence electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_electron "Valence electron") because the electrons shared in the B—F bond are located in the region of space between the two atomic [nuclei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus "Atomic nucleus") and are therefore more distant from the fluoride nucleus than they are in the lone fluoride ion. BF3 is a Lewis acid because it accepts the electron pair from fluoride. This reaction cannot be described in terms of Brønsted theory because there is no proton transfer.

The second reaction can be described using either theory. A proton is transferred from an unspecified Brønsted acid to ammonia, a Brønsted base; alternatively, ammonia acts as a Lewis base and transfers a lone pair of electrons to form a bond with a hydrogen ion. The species that gains the electron pair is the Lewis acid; for example, the oxygen atom in H3O+ gains a pair of electrons when one of the H—O bonds is broken and the electrons shared in the bond become localized on oxygen.

Depending on the context, a Lewis acid may also be described as an [oxidizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidizing_agent "Oxidizing agent") or an [electrophile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrophile "Electrophile"). Organic Brønsted acids, such as acetic, citric, or oxalic acid, are not Lewis acids.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Oxtoby8th-4) They dissociate in water to produce a Lewis acid, H+, but at the same time, they also yield an equal amount of a Lewis base (acetate, citrate, or oxalate, respectively, for the acids mentioned). This article deals mostly with Brønsted acids rather than Lewis acids.

## Dissociation and equilibrium

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=5 "Edit section: Dissociation and equilibrium")]

Reactions of acids are often generalized in the form HA ⇌ H+ + A−, where HA represents the acid and A− is the [conjugate base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_acid "Conjugate acid"). This reaction is referred to as **protolysis**. The protonated form (HA) of an acid is also sometimes referred to as the **free acid**.[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-6)

Acid–base conjugate pairs differ by one proton, and can be interconverted by the addition or removal of a proton ([protonation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protonation "Protonation") and [deprotonation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deprotonation "Deprotonation"), respectively). The acid can be the charged species and the conjugate base can be neutral in which case the generalized reaction scheme could be written as HA+ ⇌ H+ + A. In solution there exists an [equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_equilibrium "Chemical equilibrium") between the acid and its conjugate base. The [equilibrium constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_constant "Equilibrium constant") *K* is an expression of the equilibrium concentrations of the molecules or the ions in solution. Brackets indicate concentration, such that \[H2O] means *the concentration of H2O*. The [acid dissociation constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_dissociation_constant "Acid dissociation constant") *K*a is generally used in the context of acid–base reactions. The numerical value of *K*a is equal to the [product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_\(mathematics\) "Product (mathematics)") (multiplication) of the concentrations of the products divided by the concentration of the reactants, where the reactant is the acid (HA) and the products are the conjugate base and H+.

- ![{\displaystyle K\_{a}={\frac {{\ce {\[H+\] \[A^{-}\]}}}{{\ce {\[HA\]}}}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5ad1f54a70a45ea863263baa691826f3ee6cfb58)

The stronger of two acids will have a higher *K*a than the weaker acid; the ratio of hydrogen ions to acid will be higher for the stronger acid as the stronger acid has a greater tendency to lose its proton. Because the range of possible values for *K*a spans many orders of magnitude, a more manageable constant, p*K*a is more frequently used, where p*K*a = −log10 *K*a. Stronger acids have a smaller p*K*a than weaker acids. Experimentally determined p*K*a at 25 °C in aqueous solution are often quoted in textbooks and reference material.

## Nomenclature

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=6 "Edit section: Nomenclature")]

Arrhenius acids are named according to their [anions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anion "Anion"). In the classical naming system, the ionic suffix is dropped and replaced with a new suffix, according to the table following. The prefix "hydro-" is used when the acid is made up of just hydrogen and one other element. For example, HCl has [chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloride "Chloride") as its anion, so the hydro- prefix is used, and the -ide suffix makes the name take the form [hydrochloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrochloric_acid "Hydrochloric acid").

*Classical naming system:*

## Acid strength

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=7 "Edit section: Acid strength")]

Main article: [Acid strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_strength "Acid strength")

The strength of an acid refers to its ability or tendency to lose a proton. A strong acid is one that completely dissociates in water; in other words, one [mole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_\(unit\) "Mole (unit)") of a strong acid HA dissolves in water yielding one mole of H+ and one mole of the conjugate base, A−, and none of the protonated acid HA. In contrast, a weak acid only partially dissociates and at equilibrium both the acid and the conjugate base are in solution. Examples of [strong acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_acid "Strong acid") are [hydrochloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrochloric_acid "Hydrochloric acid") (HCl), [hydroiodic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroiodic_acid "Hydroiodic acid") (HI), [hydrobromic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrobromic_acid "Hydrobromic acid") (HBr), [perchloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perchloric_acid "Perchloric acid") (HClO4), [nitric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitric_acid "Nitric acid") (HNO3) and [sulfuric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfuric_acid "Sulfuric acid") (H2SO4). In water each of these essentially ionizes 100%. The stronger an acid is, the more easily it loses a proton, H+. Two key factors that contribute to the ease of deprotonation are the [polarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_polarity "Chemical polarity") of the H—A bond and the size of atom A, which determines the strength of the H—A bond. Acid strengths are also often discussed in terms of the stability of the conjugate base.

Stronger acids have a larger [acid dissociation constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_dissociation_constant "Acid dissociation constant"), *K*a and a lower p*K*a than weaker acids.

[Sulfonic acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfonic_acid "Sulfonic acid"), which are organic oxyacids, are a class of strong acids. A common example is [toluenesulfonic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toluenesulfonic_acid "Toluenesulfonic acid") (tosylic acid). Unlike sulfuric acid itself, sulfonic acids can be solids. In fact, [polystyrene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polystyrene "Polystyrene") functionalized into polystyrene sulfonate is a solid strongly acidic plastic that is filterable.

[Superacids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superacid "Superacid") are acids stronger than 100% sulfuric acid. Examples of superacids are [fluoroantimonic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluoroantimonic_acid "Fluoroantimonic acid"), [magic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_acid "Magic acid") and [perchloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perchloric_acid "Perchloric acid"). The strongest known acid is [helium hydride ion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium_hydride_ion "Helium hydride ion"),[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-chebi33689-7) with a [proton affinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton_affinity "Proton affinity") of 177.8kJ/mol.[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Epa-8) Superacids can permanently protonate water to give ionic, crystalline [hydronium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydronium "Hydronium") "salts". They can also quantitatively stabilize [carbocations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbocation "Carbocation").

While *K*a measures the strength of an acid compound, the strength of an aqueous acid solution is measured by pH, which is an indication of the concentration of hydronium in the solution. The pH of a simple solution of an acid compound in water is determined by the dilution of the compound and the compound's *K*a.

## Lewis acid strength in non-aqueous solutions

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=8 "Edit section: Lewis acid strength in non-aqueous solutions")]

[Lewis acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_acids "Lewis acids") have been classified in the [ECW model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ECW_model "ECW model") and it has been shown that there is no one order of acid strengths.[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-9) The relative acceptor strength of Lewis acids toward a series of bases, versus other Lewis acids, can be illustrated by [C-B plots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ECW_model "ECW model").[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-10)[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-11) It has been shown that to define the order of Lewis acid strength at least two properties must be considered. For Pearson's qualitative [HSAB theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HSAB_theory "HSAB theory") the two properties are [hardness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HSAB_theory "HSAB theory") and strength while for Drago's quantitative [ECW model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ECW_model "ECW model") the two properties are electrostatic and covalent.

## Chemical characteristics

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=9 "Edit section: Chemical characteristics")]

### Monoprotic acids

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=10 "Edit section: Monoprotic acids")]

See also: [Acid dissociation constant § Monoprotic acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_dissociation_constant#Monoprotic_acids "Acid dissociation constant")

Monoprotic acids, also known as monobasic acids, are those acids that are able to donate one [proton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton "Proton") per molecule during the process of [dissociation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissociation_\(chemistry\) "Dissociation (chemistry)") (sometimes called ionization) as shown below (symbolized by HA):

- HA (aq) + H2O (l) ⇌ H3O+ (aq) + A− (aq)      *K*a

Common examples of monoprotic acids in [mineral acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral_acid "Mineral acid") include [hydrochloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrochloric_acid "Hydrochloric acid") (HCl) and [nitric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitric_acid "Nitric acid") (HNO3). On the other hand, for [organic acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_acids "Organic acids") the term mainly indicates the presence of one [carboxylic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carboxylic_acid "Carboxylic acid") group and sometimes these acids are known as monocarboxylic acid. Examples in [organic acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_acids "Organic acids") include [formic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formic_acid "Formic acid") (HCOOH), [acetic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetic_acid "Acetic acid") (CH3COOH) and [benzoic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzoic_acid "Benzoic acid") (C6H5COOH).

### Polyprotic acids

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=11 "Edit section: Polyprotic acids")]

See also: [Acid dissociation constant § Polyprotic acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_dissociation_constant#Polyprotic_acids "Acid dissociation constant")

Polyprotic acids, also known as polybasic acids, are able to donate more than one proton per acid molecule, in contrast to monoprotic acids that only donate one proton per molecule. Specific types of polyprotic acids have more specific names, such as diprotic (or dibasic) acid (two potential protons to donate), and triprotic (or tribasic) acid (three potential protons to donate). Some macromolecules such as proteins and nucleic acids can have a very large number of acidic protons.[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-12)

A diprotic acid (here symbolized by H2A) can undergo one or two dissociations depending on the pH. Each dissociation has its own dissociation constant, Ka1 and Ka2.

- - H2A (aq) + H2O (l) ⇌ H3O+ (aq) + HA− (aq)     *K*a1
  - HA− (aq) + H2O (l) ⇌ H3O+ (aq) + A2− (aq)       *K*a2

The first dissociation constant is typically greater than the second (i.e., *K*a1 > *K*a2). For example, [sulfuric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfuric_acid "Sulfuric acid") (H2SO4) can donate one proton to form the [bisulfate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bisulfate "Bisulfate") anion (HSO−\
4\), for which *K*a1 is very large; then it can donate a second proton to form the [sulfate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfate "Sulfate") anion (SO2−\
4\), wherein the *K*a2 is intermediate strength. The large *K*a1 for the first dissociation makes sulfuric a strong acid. In a similar manner, the weak unstable [carbonic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonic_acid "Carbonic acid") (H2CO3) can lose one proton to form [bicarbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicarbonate "Bicarbonate") anion (HCO−\
3\) and lose a second to form [carbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonate "Carbonate") anion (CO2−\
3\). Both *K*a values are small, but *K*a1 > *K*a2 .

A triprotic acid (H3A) can undergo one, two, or three dissociations and has three dissociation constants, where *K*a1 > *K*a2 > *K*a3.

- - H3A (aq) + H2O (l) ⇌ H3O+ (aq) + H2A− (aq)      *K*a1
  - H2A− (aq) + H2O (l) ⇌ H3O+ (aq) + HA2− (aq)       *K*a2
  - HA2− (aq) + H2O (l) ⇌ H3O+ (aq) + A3− (aq)      *K*a3

An [inorganic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inorganic "Inorganic") example of a triprotic acid is orthophosphoric acid (H3PO4), usually just called [phosphoric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphoric_acid "Phosphoric acid"). All three protons can be successively lost to yield H2PO−\
4, then HPO2−\
4, and finally PO3−\
4, the orthophosphate ion, usually just called [phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphate "Phosphate"). Even though the positions of the three protons on the original phosphoric acid molecule are equivalent, the successive *K*a values differ since it is energetically less favorable to lose a proton if the conjugate base is more negatively charged. An [organic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_compound "Organic compound") example of a triprotic acid is [citric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citric_acid "Citric acid"), which can successively lose three protons to finally form the [citrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citrate "Citrate") ion.

Although the subsequent loss of each hydrogen ion is less favorable, all of the conjugate bases are present in solution. The fractional concentration, *α* (alpha), for each species can be calculated. For example, a generic diprotic acid will generate 3 species in solution: H2A, HA−, and A2−. The fractional concentrations can be calculated as below when given either the pH (which can be converted to the \[H+]) or the concentrations of the acid with all its conjugate bases:

- ![{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\alpha \_{{\ce {H2A}}}&={\frac {{\ce {\[H+\]^2}}}{{\ce {\[H+\]^2}}+\[{\ce {H+}}\]K\_{1}+K\_{1}K\_{2}}}={\frac {{\ce {\[H2A\]}}}{{\ce {{\[H2A\]}}}+\[HA^{-}\]+\[A^{2-}\]}}\\\\\alpha \_{{\ce {HA^-}}}&={\frac {\[{\ce {H+}}\]K\_{1}}{{\ce {\[H+\]^2}}+\[{\ce {H+}}\]K\_{1}+K\_{1}K\_{2}}}={\frac {{\ce {\[HA^-\]}}}{{\ce {\[H2A\]}}+{\[HA^{-}\]}+{\[A^{2-}\]}}}\\\\\alpha \_{{\ce {A^{2-}}}}&={\frac {K\_{1}K\_{2}}{{\ce {\[H+\]^2}}+\[{\ce {H+}}\]K\_{1}+K\_{1}K\_{2}}}={\frac {{\ce {\[A^{2-}\]}}}{{\ce {{\[H2A\]}}}+{\[HA^{-}\]}+{\[A^{2-}\]}}}\end{aligned}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8e09f46996e12e5d2c821158e6bc6f70f54edbe7)

A plot of these fractional concentrations against pH, for given *K*1 and *K*2, is known as a [Bjerrum plot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bjerrum_plot "Bjerrum plot"). A pattern is observed in the above equations and can be expanded to the general *n* -protic acid that has been deprotonated *i* -times:

where *K*0 = 1 and the other K-terms are the dissociation constants for the acid.

### Neutralization

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=12 "Edit section: Neutralization")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a0/Hydrochloric_acid_ammonia.jpg/220px-Hydrochloric_acid_ammonia.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Hydrochloric_acid_ammonia.jpg)

[Hydrochloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrochloric_acid "Hydrochloric acid") (in [beaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaker_\(glassware\) "Beaker (glassware)")) reacting with [ammonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia "Ammonia") fumes to produce [ammonium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonium_chloride "Ammonium chloride") (white smoke)

[Neutralization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutralization_\(chemistry\) "Neutralization (chemistry)") is the reaction between an acid and a base, producing a [salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_\(chemistry\) "Salt (chemistry)") and neutralized base; for example, [hydrochloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrochloric_acid "Hydrochloric acid") and [sodium hydroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_hydroxide "Sodium hydroxide") form [sodium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_chloride "Sodium chloride") and water:

- HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → H2O(l) + NaCl(aq)

Neutralization is the basis of [titration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titration "Titration"), where a [pH indicator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH_indicator "PH indicator") shows equivalence point when the equivalent number of moles of a base have been added to an acid. It is often wrongly assumed that neutralization should result in a solution with pH 7.0, which is only the case with similar acid and base strengths during a reaction.

Neutralization with a base weaker than the acid results in a weakly acidic salt. An example is the weakly acidic [ammonium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonium_chloride "Ammonium chloride"), which is produced from the strong acid [hydrogen chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_chloride "Hydrogen chloride") and the weak base [ammonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia "Ammonia"). Conversely, neutralizing a weak acid with a strong base gives a weakly basic salt (e.g., [sodium fluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_fluoride "Sodium fluoride") from [hydrogen fluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_fluoride "Hydrogen fluoride") and [sodium hydroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_hydroxide "Sodium hydroxide")).

### Weak acid–weak base equilibrium

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=13 "Edit section: Weak acid–weak base equilibrium")]

Main article: [Henderson–Hasselbalch equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henderson%E2%80%93Hasselbalch_equation "Henderson–Hasselbalch equation")

In order for a protonated acid to lose a proton, the pH of the system must rise above the p*K*a of the acid. The decreased concentration of H+ in that basic solution shifts the equilibrium towards the conjugate base form (the deprotonated form of the acid). In lower-pH (more acidic) solutions, there is a high enough H+ concentration in the solution to cause the acid to remain in its protonated form.

Solutions of weak acids and salts of their conjugate bases form [buffer solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buffer_solution "Buffer solution").

## Titration

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=14 "Edit section: Titration")]

Main article: [Acid–base titration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid%E2%80%93base_titration "Acid–base titration")

To determine the concentration of an acid in an aqueous solution, an acid–base titration is commonly performed. A strong base solution with a known concentration, usually NaOH or KOH, is added to neutralize the acid solution according to the color change of the indicator with the amount of base added.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-13) The titration curve of an acid titrated by a base has two axes, with the base volume on the x-axis and the solution's pH value on the y-axis. The pH of the solution always goes up as the base is added to the solution.

### Example: Diprotic acid

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=15 "Edit section: Example: Diprotic acid")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b9/Titration_alanine.jpg/220px-Titration_alanine.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Titration_alanine.jpg)

This is an ideal titration curve for [alanine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alanine "Alanine"), a diprotic amino acid.[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-14) Point 2 is the first equivalent point where the amount of NaOH added equals the amount of alanine in the original solution.

For each diprotic acid titration curve, from left to right, there are two midpoints, two equivalence points, and two buffer regions.[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-15)

#### Equivalence points

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=16 "Edit section: Equivalence points")]

Due to the successive dissociation processes, there are two equivalence points in the titration curve of a diprotic acid.[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-16) The first equivalence point occurs when all first hydrogen ions from the first ionization are titrated.[\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-learning-17) In other words, the amount of OH− added equals the original amount of H2A at the first equivalence point. The second equivalence point occurs when all hydrogen ions are titrated. Therefore, the amount of OH− added equals twice the amount of H2A at this time. For a weak diprotic acid titrated by a strong base, the second equivalence point must occur at pH above 7 due to the hydrolysis of the resulted salts in the solution.[\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-learning-17) At either equivalence point, adding a drop of base will cause the steepest rise of the pH value in the system.

#### Buffer regions and midpoints

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=17 "Edit section: Buffer regions and midpoints")]

A titration curve for a diprotic acid contains two midpoints where pH=pKa. Since there are two different Ka values, the first midpoint occurs at pH=pKa1 and the second one occurs at pH=pKa2.[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-18) Each segment of the curve that contains a midpoint at its center is called the buffer region. Because the buffer regions consist of the acid and its conjugate base, it can resist pH changes when base is added until the next equivalent points.[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Ebbing-5)

## Applications of acids

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=18 "Edit section: Applications of acids")]

### In industry

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=19 "Edit section: In industry")]

Acids are fundamental reagents in treating almost all processes in modern industry. Sulfuric acid, a diprotic acid, is the most widely used acid in industry, and is also the most-produced industrial chemical in the world. It is mainly used in producing fertilizer, detergent, batteries and dyes, as well as used in processing many products such like removing impurities.[\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-19) According to the statistics data in 2011, the annual production of sulfuric acid was around 200 million tonnes in the world.[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-20) For example, phosphate minerals react with sulfuric acid to produce [phosphoric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphoric_acid "Phosphoric acid") for the production of phosphate fertilizers, and [zinc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc "Zinc") is produced by dissolving zinc oxide into sulfuric acid, purifying the solution and electrowinning.

In the chemical industry, acids react in neutralization reactions to produce salts. For example, [nitric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitric_acid "Nitric acid") reacts with [ammonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia "Ammonia") to produce [ammonium nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonium_nitrate "Ammonium nitrate"), a fertilizer. Additionally, [carboxylic acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carboxylic_acid "Carboxylic acid") can be [esterified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esterification "Esterification") with alcohols, to produce [esters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ester "Ester").

Acids are often used to remove rust and other corrosion from metals in a process known as [pickling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pickling_\(metal\) "Pickling (metal)"). They may be used as an electrolyte in a [wet cell battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wet_cell_battery "Wet cell battery"), such as [sulfuric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfuric_acid "Sulfuric acid") in a [car battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Car_battery "Car battery").

### In food

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=20 "Edit section: In food")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/cf/Tumbler_of_cola_with_ice.jpg/220px-Tumbler_of_cola_with_ice.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Tumbler_of_cola_with_ice.jpg)

Carbonated water (H2CO3 aqueous solution) is commonly added to soft drinks to make them effervesce.

[Tartaric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tartaric_acid "Tartaric acid") is an important component of some commonly used foods like unripened mangoes and tamarind. Natural fruits and vegetables also contain acids. [Citric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citric_acid "Citric acid") is present in oranges, lemon and other citrus fruits. [Oxalic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxalic_acid "Oxalic acid") is present in tomatoes, spinach, and especially in [carambola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carambola "Carambola") and [rhubarb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhubarb "Rhubarb"); rhubarb leaves and unripe carambolas are toxic because of high concentrations of oxalic acid. [Ascorbic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascorbic_acid "Ascorbic acid") (Vitamin C) is an essential vitamin for the human body and is present in such foods as amla ([Indian gooseberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phyllanthus_emblica "Phyllanthus emblica")), lemon, citrus fruits, and guava.

Many acids can be found in various kinds of food as additives, as they alter their taste and serve as preservatives. [Phosphoric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphoric_acid "Phosphoric acid"), for example, is a component of [cola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cola "Cola") drinks. [Acetic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetic_acid "Acetic acid") is used in day-to-day life as vinegar. Citric acid is used as a preservative in sauces and pickles.

[Carbonic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonic_acid "Carbonic acid") is one of the most common acid additives that are widely added in [soft drinks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soft_drink "Soft drink"). During the manufacturing process, CO2 is usually pressurized to dissolve in these drinks to generate carbonic acid. Carbonic acid is very unstable and tends to decompose into water and CO2 at room temperature and pressure. Therefore, when bottles or cans of these kinds of soft drinks are opened, the soft drinks fizz and effervesce as CO2 bubbles come out.[\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-21)

Certain acids are used as drugs. [Acetylsalicylic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetylsalicylic_acid "Acetylsalicylic acid") (Aspirin) is used as a pain killer and for bringing down fevers.

### In human bodies

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=21 "Edit section: In human bodies")]

Acids play important roles in the human body. The hydrochloric acid present in the stomach aids digestion by breaking down large and complex food molecules. Amino acids are required for synthesis of proteins required for growth and repair of body tissues. Fatty acids are also required for growth and repair of body tissues. Nucleic acids are important for the manufacturing of DNA and RNA and transmitting of traits to offspring through genes. Carbonic acid is important for maintenance of pH equilibrium in the body.

Human bodies contain a variety of organic and inorganic compounds, among those [dicarboxylic acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dicarboxylic_acid "Dicarboxylic acid") play an essential role in many biological behaviors. Many of those acids are [amino acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acids "Amino acids"), which mainly serve as materials for the synthesis of proteins.[\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-22) Other weak acids serve as buffers with their conjugate bases to keep the body's pH from undergoing large scale changes that would be harmful to cells.[\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-23) The rest of the dicarboxylic acids also participate in the synthesis of various biologically important compounds in human bodies.

### Acid catalysis

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=22 "Edit section: Acid catalysis")]

Main article: [Acid catalysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_catalysis "Acid catalysis")

Acids are used as [catalysts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalyst "Catalyst") in industrial and organic chemistry; for example, [sulfuric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfuric_acid "Sulfuric acid") is used in very large quantities in the [alkylation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkylation "Alkylation") process to produce gasoline. Some acids, such as sulfuric, phosphoric, and hydrochloric acids, also effect [dehydration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dehydration_reaction "Dehydration reaction") and [condensation reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensation_reaction "Condensation reaction"). In biochemistry, many [enzymes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme "Enzyme") employ acid catalysis.[\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Voet_acid_cat-24)

## Biological occurrence

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=23 "Edit section: Biological occurrence")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/34/Aminoacid.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Aminoacid.png)

Basic structure of an [amino acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acid "Amino acid")

Many biologically important molecules are acids. [Nucleic acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleic_acid "Nucleic acid"), which contain acidic [phosphate groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphate "Phosphate"), include [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA "DNA") and [RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA "RNA"). Nucleic acids contain the genetic code that determines many of an organism's characteristics, and is passed from parents to offspring. DNA contains the chemical blueprint for the synthesis of [proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein "Protein"), which are made up of [amino acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acid "Amino acid") subunits. [Cell membranes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_membrane "Cell membrane") contain [fatty acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatty_acid "Fatty acid") [esters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ester "Ester") such as [phospholipids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phospholipids "Phospholipids").

An α-amino acid has a central carbon (the α or [*alpha* carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_and_beta_carbon "Alpha and beta carbon")) that is covalently bonded to a [carboxyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carboxyl "Carboxyl") group (thus they are [carboxylic acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carboxylic_acid "Carboxylic acid")), an [amino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amine "Amine") group, a hydrogen atom and a variable group. The variable group, also called the R group or side chain, determines the identity and many of the properties of a specific amino acid. In [glycine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glycine "Glycine"), the simplest amino acid, the R group is a hydrogen atom, but in all other amino acids it is contains one or more carbon atoms bonded to hydrogens, and may contain other elements such as sulfur, oxygen or nitrogen. With the exception of glycine, naturally occurring amino acids are [chiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirality_\(chemistry\) "Chirality (chemistry)") and almost invariably occur in the [L-configuration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirality_\(chemistry\)#By_configuration:_D-_and_L- "Chirality (chemistry)"). [Peptidoglycan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peptidoglycan "Peptidoglycan"), found in some bacterial [cell walls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_wall "Cell wall") contains some D-amino acids. At physiological pH, typically around 7, free amino acids exist in a charged form, where the acidic carboxyl group (-COOH) loses a proton (-COO−) and the basic amine group (-NH2) gains a proton (-NH+\
3\). The entire molecule has a net neutral charge and is a [zwitterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zwitterion "Zwitterion"), with the exception of amino acids with basic or acidic side chains. [Aspartic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspartic_acid "Aspartic acid"), for example, possesses one protonated amine and two deprotonated carboxyl groups, for a net charge of −1 at physiological pH.

Fatty acids and fatty acid derivatives are another group of carboxylic acids that play a significant role in biology. These contain long hydrocarbon chains and a carboxylic acid group on one end. The cell membrane of nearly all organisms is primarily made up of a [phospholipid bilayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phospholipid_bilayer "Phospholipid bilayer"), a [micelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micelle "Micelle") of hydrophobic fatty acid esters with polar, hydrophilic [phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphate "Phosphate") "head" groups. Membranes contain additional components, some of which can participate in acid–base reactions.

In humans and many other animals, [hydrochloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrochloric_acid "Hydrochloric acid") is a part of the [gastric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastric_acid "Gastric acid") secreted within the [stomach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stomach "Stomach") to help hydrolyze [proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein "Protein") and [polysaccharides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polysaccharide "Polysaccharide"), as well as converting the inactive pro-enzyme, [pepsinogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pepsinogen "Pepsinogen") into the [enzyme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digestive_enzyme "Digestive enzyme"), [pepsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pepsin "Pepsin"). Some organisms produce acids for defense; for example, ants produce [formic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formic_acid "Formic acid").

Acid–base equilibrium plays a critical role in regulating [mammalian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mammal "Mammal") breathing. [Oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_oxygen "Molecular oxygen") gas (O2) drives [cellular respiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_respiration "Cellular respiration"), the process by which animals release the chemical [potential energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_energy "Potential energy") stored in food, producing [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide "Carbon dioxide") (CO2) as a byproduct. Oxygen and carbon dioxide are exchanged in the [lungs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lungs "Lungs"), and the body responds to changing energy demands by adjusting the rate of [ventilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ventilation_\(physiology\) "Ventilation (physiology)"). For example, during periods of exertion the body rapidly breaks down stored [carbohydrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbohydrate "Carbohydrate") and fat, releasing CO2 into the blood stream. In aqueous solutions such as blood CO2 exists in equilibrium with [carbonic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonic_acid "Carbonic acid") and [bicarbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicarbonate "Bicarbonate") ion.

- CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO−3

It is the decrease in pH that signals the brain to breathe faster and deeper, expelling the excess CO2 and resupplying the cells with O2.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/67/Aspirin-skeletal.svg/220px-Aspirin-skeletal.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Aspirin-skeletal.svg)

[Aspirin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspirin "Aspirin") (acetylsalicylic acid) is a [carboxylic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carboxylic_acid "Carboxylic acid").

[Cell membranes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_membrane "Cell membrane") are generally impermeable to charged or large, polar molecules because of the [lipophilic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipophilicity "Lipophilicity") fatty acyl chains comprising their interior. Many biologically important molecules, including a number of pharmaceutical agents, are organic weak acids that can cross the membrane in their protonated, uncharged form but not in their charged form (i.e., as the conjugate base). For this reason the activity of many drugs can be enhanced or inhibited by the use of antacids or acidic foods. The charged form, however, is often more soluble in blood and [cytosol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cytosol "Cytosol"), both aqueous environments. When the extracellular environment is more acidic than the neutral pH within the cell, certain acids will exist in their neutral form and will be membrane soluble, allowing them to cross the phospholipid bilayer. Acids that lose a proton at the [intracellular pH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intracellular_pH "Intracellular pH") will exist in their soluble, charged form and are thus able to diffuse through the cytosol to their target. [Ibuprofen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibuprofen "Ibuprofen"), [aspirin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspirin "Aspirin") and [penicillin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penicillin "Penicillin") are examples of drugs that are weak acids.

### Mineral acids (inorganic acids)

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=25 "Edit section: Mineral acids (inorganic acids)")]

- Halogen oxoacids: [hypochlorous acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypochlorous_acid "Hypochlorous acid") (HClO), [chlorous acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorous_acid "Chlorous acid") (HClO2), [chloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloric_acid "Chloric acid") (HClO3), [perchloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perchloric_acid "Perchloric acid") (HClO4), and corresponding analogs for bromine and iodine

- [Fluoroboric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluoroboric_acid "Fluoroboric acid") (HBF4)
- [Hexafluorophosphoric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexafluorophosphoric_acid "Hexafluorophosphoric acid") (HPF6)
- [Chromic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromic_acid "Chromic acid") (H2CrO4)
- [Boric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boric_acid "Boric acid") (H3BO3)

### Sulfonic acids

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=26 "Edit section: Sulfonic acids")]

A [sulfonic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfonic_acid "Sulfonic acid") has the general formula RS(=O)2–OH, where R is an organic radical.

- [Methanesulfonic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methanesulfonic_acid "Methanesulfonic acid") (or mesylic acid, CH3SO3H)
- [Ethanesulfonic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethanesulfonic_acid "Ethanesulfonic acid") (or esylic acid, CH3CH2SO3H)
- [Benzenesulfonic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzenesulfonic_acid "Benzenesulfonic acid") (or besylic acid, C6H5SO3H)
- [p-Toluenesulfonic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-Toluenesulfonic_acid "P-Toluenesulfonic acid") (or tosylic acid, CH3C6H4SO3H)
- [Trifluoromethanesulfonic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trifluoromethanesulfonic_acid "Trifluoromethanesulfonic acid") (or triflic acid, CF3SO3H)
- [Polystyrene sulfonic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polystyrene_sulfonic_acid "Polystyrene sulfonic acid") (sulfonated [polystyrene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polystyrene "Polystyrene"), \[CH2CH(C6H4)SO3H]n)

### Carboxylic acids

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=27 "Edit section: Carboxylic acids")]

A [carboxylic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carboxylic_acid "Carboxylic acid") has the general formula R-C(O)OH, where R is an organic radical. The carboxyl group -C(O)OH contains a [carbonyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonyl "Carbonyl") group, C=O, and a [hydroxyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroxyl "Hydroxyl") group, O-H.

- [Acetic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetic_acid "Acetic acid") (CH3COOH)
- [Citric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citric_acid "Citric acid") (C6H8O7)
- [Formic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formic_acid "Formic acid") (HCOOH)
- [Gluconic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluconic_acid "Gluconic acid") HOCH2-(CHOH)4-COOH
- [Lactic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactic_acid "Lactic acid") (CH3-CHOH-COOH)
- [Oxalic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxalic_acid "Oxalic acid") (HOOC-COOH)
- [Tartaric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tartaric_acid "Tartaric acid") (HOOC-CHOH-CHOH-COOH)

### Halogenated carboxylic acids

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=28 "Edit section: Halogenated carboxylic acids")]

Halogenation at [alpha position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_and_beta_carbon "Alpha and beta carbon") increases acid strength, so that the following acids are all stronger than acetic acid.

- [Fluoroacetic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluoroacetic_acid "Fluoroacetic acid")
- [Trifluoroacetic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trifluoroacetic_acid "Trifluoroacetic acid")
- [Chloroacetic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroacetic_acid "Chloroacetic acid")
- [Dichloroacetic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dichloroacetic_acid "Dichloroacetic acid")
- [Trichloroacetic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichloroacetic_acid "Trichloroacetic acid")

### Vinylogous carboxylic acids

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Acid\&action=edit\&section=29 "Edit section: Vinylogous carboxylic acids")]

Normal carboxylic acids are the direct union of a carbonyl group and a hydroxyl group. In [vinylogous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinylogous "Vinylogous") carboxylic acids, a carbon-carbon double bond separates the carbonyl and hydroxyl groups.

- [Ascorbic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascorbic_acid "Ascorbic acid")
