# Android (robot)

Robot resembling a human

"Mechanoid" redirects here. For other meanings, see [Mechanoid (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanoid_\(disambiguation\) "Mechanoid (disambiguation)").

"Androids" redirects here. For other uses, see [Androids (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Androids_\(disambiguation\) "Androids (disambiguation)").

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/aa/Repliee_Q2.jpg/220px-Repliee_Q2.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Repliee_Q2.jpg)

Repliee Q2, an android, can mimic human functions such as blinking, breathing and speaking, with the ability to recognize and process speech and touch, and then respond in kind.

An **android** is a [humanoid robot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanoid_robot "Humanoid robot") or other artificial being, often made from a flesh-like material.[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Van_Riper_10-1)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Prucher2007-2)[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Stableford2006-3)[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Wilson2006-4) Historically, androids existed only in the domain of [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction "Science fiction") and were frequently seen in film and television, but advances in [robot technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robotics "Robotics") have allowed the design of functional and realistic humanoid robots.[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-5)[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-6)

## Terminology

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Android_\(robot\)\&action=edit\&section=1 "Edit section: Terminology")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9f/Android_Times_Tue_Dec_22_1795.jpg/220px-Android_Times_Tue_Dec_22_1795.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Android_Times_Tue_Dec_22_1795.jpg)

Early example of the term *androides* used to describe human-like mechanical devices, *[London Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Times "London Times")*, 22 December 1795

The *Oxford English Dictionary* traces the earliest use (as "Androides") to [Ephraim Chambers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephraim_Chambers "Ephraim Chambers")' 1728 *[Cyclopaedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclopaedia,_or_an_Universal_Dictionary_of_Arts_and_Sciences "Cyclopaedia, or an Universal Dictionary of Arts and Sciences"),* in reference to an [automaton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automaton "Automaton") that St. [Albertus Magnus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albertus_Magnus "Albertus Magnus") allegedly created.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Stableford2006-3)[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-7) By the late 1700s, "androides", elaborate mechanical devices resembling humans performing human activities, were displayed in exhibit halls.[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-8) The term "android" appears in US patents as early as 1863 in reference to miniature human-like toy automatons.[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-9) The term *android* was used in a more modern sense by the French author [Auguste Villiers de l'Isle-Adam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auguste_Villiers_de_l%27Isle-Adam "Auguste Villiers de l'Isle-Adam") in his work *[Tomorrow's Eve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Future_Eve "The Future Eve")* (1886), featuring an artificial humanoid robot named Hadaly.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Stableford2006-3) The term made an impact into English [pulp science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulp_science_fiction "Pulp science fiction") starting from [Jack Williamson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Williamson "Jack Williamson")'s *[The Cometeers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cometeers "The Cometeers")* (1936) and the distinction between mechanical robots and fleshy androids was popularized by [Edmond Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmond_Hamilton "Edmond Hamilton")'s [Captain Future](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captain_Future "Captain Future") stories (1940–1944).[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Stableford2006-3)

Although [Karel Čapek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karel_%C4%8Capek "Karel Čapek")'s robots in *[R.U.R. (Rossum's Universal Robots)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R.U.R. "R.U.R.")* (1921)—the play that introduced the word *[robot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robot "Robot")* to the world—were organic artificial humans, the word "robot" has come to primarily refer to mechanical humans, animals, and other beings.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Stableford2006-3) The term "android" can mean either one of these,[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Stableford2006-3) while a [cyborg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyborg "Cyborg") ("cybernetic organism" or "bionic man") would be a creature that is a combination of organic and mechanical parts.

The term "[droid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Droid_\(Star_Wars\) "Droid (Star Wars)")", popularized by [George Lucas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Lucas "George Lucas") in the original *[Star Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Wars_\(film\) "Star Wars (film)")* film and now used widely within science fiction, originated as an abridgment of "android", but has been used by Lucas and others to mean any robot, including distinctly non-human form machines like [R2-D2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R2-D2 "R2-D2"). The word "android" was used in *[Star Trek: The Original Series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Trek:_The_Original_Series "Star Trek: The Original Series")* episode "[What Are Little Girls Made Of?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_Are_Little_Girls_Made_Of%3F "What Are Little Girls Made Of?")" The abbreviation "andy", coined as a [pejorative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pejorative "Pejorative") by writer [Philip K. Dick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_K._Dick "Philip K. Dick") in his novel *[Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Do_Androids_Dream_of_Electric_Sheep%3F "Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?")*, has seen some further usage, such as within the TV series *[Total Recall 2070](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_Recall_2070 "Total Recall 2070")*.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-TR2070-10)

While the term "android" is used in reference to human-looking robots in general (not necessarily male-looking humanoid robots), a robot with a female appearance can also be referred to as a *[gynoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gynoid "Gynoid")*. Besides one can refer to robots without alluding to their sexual appearance by calling them *anthrobots* (a portmanteau of anthrōpos and robot; see *[anthrobotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthrobotics "Anthrobotics")*) or *anthropoids* (short for anthropoid robots; the term *humanoids* is not appropriate because it is already commonly used to refer to human-like organic species in the context of science fiction, futurism and speculative astrobiology).[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-11)

Authors have used the term *android* in more diverse ways than *[robot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robot "Robot")* or *[cyborg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyborg "Cyborg")*. In some fictional works, the difference between a robot and android is only superficial, with androids being made to look like humans on the outside but with robot-like internal mechanics.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Stableford2006-3) In other stories, authors have used the word "android" to mean a wholly organic, yet artificial, creation.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Stableford2006-3) Other fictional depictions of androids fall somewhere in between.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Stableford2006-3)

Eric G. Wilson, who defines an android as a "synthetic human being", distinguishes between three types of android, based on their body's composition:

- the mummy type – made of "dead things" or "stiff, inanimate, natural material", such as mummies, puppets, dolls and statues
- the golem type – made from flexible, possibly organic material, including golems and homunculi
- the automaton type – made from a mix of dead and living parts, including automatons and robots[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Wilson2006-4)

Although human morphology is not necessarily the ideal form for working robots, the fascination in developing robots that can mimic it can be found historically in the assimilation of two concepts: *[simulacra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simulacrum "Simulacrum")* (devices that exhibit likeness) and *[automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automata_theory "Automata theory")* (devices that have independence).

## Projects

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Android_\(robot\)\&action=edit\&section=2 "Edit section: Projects")]

Several projects aiming to create androids that look, and, to a certain degree, speak or act like a human being have been launched or are underway.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2c/Repliee_Q2_face.jpg/220px-Repliee_Q2_face.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Repliee_Q2_face.jpg)

Repliee Q2, a Japanese android

[Japanese robotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_robotics "Japanese robotics") have been leading the field since the 1970s.[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-12) [Waseda University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waseda_University "Waseda University") initiated the WABOT project in 1967, and in 1972 completed the WABOT-1, the first android, a full-scale humanoid intelligent robot.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-13)[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-androidworld.com-14) Its limb control system allowed it to walk with the lower limbs, and to grip and transport objects with hands, using [tactile sensors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tactile_sensor "Tactile sensor"). Its vision system allowed it to measure distances and directions to objects using external receptors, artificial eyes and ears. And its conversation system allowed it to communicate with a person in Japanese, with an artificial mouth.[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-androidworld.com-14)[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-15)[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-16)

In 1984, WABOT-2 was revealed, and made a number of improvements. It was capable of playing the organ. Wabot-2 had ten fingers and two feet, and was able to read a score of music. It was also able to accompany a person.[\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-17) In 1986, [Honda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honda "Honda") began its humanoid research and development program, to create humanoid robots capable of interacting successfully with humans.[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-18)

The Intelligent Robotics Lab, directed by [Hiroshi Ishiguro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiroshi_Ishiguro "Hiroshi Ishiguro") at [Osaka University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osaka_University "Osaka University"), and the Kokoro company demonstrated the [Actroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actroid "Actroid") at [Expo 2005](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expo_2005 "Expo 2005") in [Aichi Prefecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aichi_Prefecture "Aichi Prefecture"), Japan and released the [Telenoid R1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telenoid_R1 "Telenoid R1") in 2010. In 2006, Kokoro developed a new *DER 2* android. The height of the human body part of DER2 is 165 cm. There are 47 mobile points. DER2 can not only change its expression but also move its hands and feet and twist its body. The "air servosystem" which Kokoro developed originally is used for the actuator. As a result of having an actuator controlled precisely with air pressure via a servosystem, the movement is very fluid and there is very little noise. DER2 realized a slimmer body than that of the former version by using a smaller cylinder. Outwardly DER2 has a more beautiful proportion. Compared to the previous model, DER2 has thinner arms and a wider repertoire of expressions. Once programmed, it is able to choreograph its motions and gestures with its voice.

The Intelligent Mechatronics Lab, directed by Hiroshi Kobayashi at the [Tokyo University of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_University_of_Science "Tokyo University of Science"), has developed an android head called *Saya*, which was exhibited at Robodex 2002 in [Yokohama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yokohama "Yokohama"), Japan. There are several other initiatives around the world involving humanoid research and development at this time, which will hopefully introduce a broader spectrum of realized technology in the near future. Now Saya is *working* at the Science University of Tokyo as a guide.

The [Waseda University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waseda_University "Waseda University") (Japan) and [NTT docomo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NTT_docomo "NTT docomo")'s manufacturers have succeeded in creating a shape-shifting robot *WD-2*. It is capable of changing its face. At first, the creators decided the positions of the necessary points to express the outline, eyes, nose, and so on of a certain person. The robot expresses its face by moving all points to the decided positions, they say. The first version of the robot was first developed back in 2003. After that, a year later, they made a couple of major improvements to the design. The robot features an elastic mask made from the average head dummy. It uses a driving system with a 3DOF unit. The WD-2 robot can change its facial features by activating specific facial points on a mask, with each point possessing three [degrees of freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degrees_of_freedom_\(mechanics\) "Degrees of freedom (mechanics)"). This one has 17 facial points, for a total of 56 degrees of freedom. As for the materials they used, the WD-2's mask is fabricated with a highly elastic material called Septom, with bits of steel wool mixed in for added strength. Other technical features reveal a shaft driven behind the mask at the desired facial point, driven by a DC motor with a simple pulley and a slide screw. Apparently, the researchers can also modify the shape of the mask based on actual human faces. To "copy" a face, they need only a [3D scanner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_scanner "3D scanner") to determine the locations of an individual's 17 facial points. After that, they are then driven into position using a laptop and 56 motor control boards. In addition, the researchers also mention that the shifting robot can even display an individual's hair style and skin color if a photo of their face is projected onto the 3D Mask.

### Singapore

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Android_\(robot\)\&action=edit\&section=4 "Edit section: Singapore")]

Prof Nadia Thalmann, a Nanyang Technological University scientist, directed efforts of the Institute for Media Innovation along with the School of Computer Engineering in the development of a social robot, Nadine. Nadine is powered by software similar to Apple's [Siri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siri "Siri") or Microsoft's [Cortana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cortana_\(software\) "Cortana (software)"). Nadine may become a personal assistant in offices and homes in future, or she may become a companion for the young and the elderly.

Assoc Prof Gerald Seet from the School of Mechanical & Aerospace Engineering and the BeingThere Centre led a three-year R\&D development in [tele-presence robotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tele-presence_robotics "Tele-presence robotics"), creating EDGAR. A remote user can control EDGAR with the user's face and expressions displayed on the robot's face in real time. The robot also mimics their upper body movements. [\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-singapore_NTU-19)

### South Korea

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Android_\(robot\)\&action=edit\&section=5 "Edit section: South Korea")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/21/Ever-2.jpg/200px-Ever-2.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Ever-2.jpg)

[EveR-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EveR-2 "EveR-2"), the first android that can sing

[KITECH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KITECH "KITECH") researched and developed [EveR-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EveR-1 "EveR-1"), an android interpersonal communications model capable of emulating human emotional expression via facial "musculature" and capable of rudimentary conversation, having a vocabulary of around 400 words. She is 160 cm tall and weighs 50 kg, matching the average figure of a Korean woman in her twenties. EveR-1's name derives from the [Biblical Eve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eve "Eve"), plus the letter *r* for *robot*. EveR-1's advanced computing processing power enables [speech recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech_recognition "Speech recognition") and vocal synthesis, at the same time processing [lip synchronization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lip_synchronization "Lip synchronization") and visual recognition by 90-degree micro-[CCD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge-coupled_device "Charge-coupled device") cameras with [face recognition technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facial_recognition_system "Facial recognition system"). An independent microchip inside her artificial brain handles gesture expression, body coordination, and emotion expression. Her whole body is made of highly advanced synthetic jelly silicon and with 60 artificial joints in her face, neck, and lower body; she is able to demonstrate realistic facial expressions and sing while simultaneously dancing. In South Korea, the [Ministry of Information and Communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Information_and_Communication_\(South_Korea\) "Ministry of Information and Communication (South Korea)") had an ambitious plan to put a robot in every household by 2020.[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-20) Several robot cities have been planned for the country: the first will be built in 2016 at a cost of 500 billion won (US$440 million), of which 50 billion is direct government investment.[\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-21) The new robot city will feature research and development centers for manufacturers and part suppliers, as well as exhibition halls and a stadium for robot competitions. The country's new Robotics Ethics Charter will establish ground rules and laws for human interaction with robots in the future, setting standards for robotics users and manufacturers, as well as guidelines on ethical standards to be programmed into robots to prevent human abuse of robots and vice versa.[\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-22)

### United States

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Android_\(robot\)\&action=edit\&section=6 "Edit section: United States")]

[Walt Disney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Disney "Walt Disney") and a staff of [Imagineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imagineers "Imagineers") created [Great Moments with Mr. Lincoln](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Moments_with_Mr._Lincoln "Great Moments with Mr. Lincoln") that debuted at the [1964 New York World's Fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_New_York_World%27s_Fair "1964 New York World's Fair").[\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-illinois_pavillion-23)

Dr. William Barry, an Education Futurist and former visiting West Point Professor of Philosophy and Ethical Reasoning at the [United States Military Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Military_Academy "United States Military Academy"), created an AI android character named "Maria Bot". This Interface AI android was named after the infamous fictional robot Maria in the 1927 film *[Metropolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolis_\(1927_film\) "Metropolis (1927 film)")*, as a well-behaved distant relative. Maria Bot is the first AI Android Teaching Assistant at the university level.[\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-24)[\[25\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-25) Maria Bot has appeared as a keynote speaker as a duo with Barry for a TEDx talk in Everett, Washington in February 2020.[\[26\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-26)

Resembling a human from the shoulders up, Maria Bot is a virtual being android that has complex facial expressions and head movement and engages in conversation about a variety of subjects. She uses AI to process and synthesize information to make her own decisions on how to talk and engage. She collects data through conversations, direct data inputs such as books or articles, and through internet sources.

Maria Bot was built by an international high-tech company for Barry to help improve education quality and eliminate education poverty. Maria Bot is designed to create new ways for students to engage and discuss ethical issues raised by the increasing presence of robots and artificial intelligence. Barry also uses Maria Bot to demonstrate that programming a robot with life-affirming, ethical framework makes them more likely to help humans to do the same.[\[27\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-27)

Maria Bot is an ambassador robot for good and ethical AI technology.[\[28\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-28)

[Hanson Robotics, Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hanson_\(robotics_designer\) "David Hanson (robotics designer)"), of Texas and [KAIST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KAIST "KAIST") produced an android portrait of [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein"), using Hanson's facial android technology mounted on KAIST's life-size walking bipedal robot body. This Einstein android, also called "[Albert Hubo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Hubo "Albert Hubo")", thus represents the first full-body walking android in history.[\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-29) Hanson Robotics, the FedEx Institute of Technology,[\[30\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-30) and the University of Texas at Arlington also developed the android portrait of sci-fi author [Philip K. Dick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_K._Dick "Philip K. Dick") (creator of *[Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Do_Androids_Dream_of_Electric_Sheep%3F "Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?")*, the basis for the film *[Blade Runner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blade_Runner "Blade Runner")*), with full conversational capabilities that incorporated thousands of pages of the author's works.[\[31\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-31) In 2005, the PKD android won a first-place [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence") award from [AAAI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Artificial_Intelligence "Association for the Advancement of Artificial Intelligence").

## Use in fiction

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Android_\(robot\)\&action=edit\&section=7 "Edit section: Use in fiction")]

See also: [List of fictional robots and androids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fictional_robots_and_androids "List of fictional robots and androids")

Androids are a staple of science fiction. [Isaac Asimov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Asimov "Isaac Asimov") pioneered the fictionalization of the science of [robotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robotics "Robotics") and [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence"), notably in his 1950s series *[I, Robot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I,_Robot "I, Robot")*.[\[32\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-32) One thing common to most fictional androids is that the real-life technological challenges associated with creating thoroughly human-like robots — such as the creation of [strong artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_general_intelligence "Artificial general intelligence")—are assumed to have been solved.[\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Van_Riper_11-33) Fictional androids are often depicted as mentally and physically equal or superior to humans—moving, thinking and speaking as fluidly as them.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Stableford2006-3)[\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Van_Riper_11-33)

The tension between the nonhuman substance and the human appearance—or even human ambitions—of androids is the dramatic impetus behind most of their fictional depictions.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Wilson2006-4)[\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Van_Riper_11-33) Some android heroes seek, like [Pinocchio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinocchio "Pinocchio"), to become human, as in the film *[Bicentennial Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicentennial_Man_\(film\) "Bicentennial Man (film)")*,[\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Van_Riper_11-33) or [Data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_\(Star_Trek\) "Data (Star Trek)") in *[Star Trek: The Next Generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Trek:_The_Next_Generation "Star Trek: The Next Generation")*. Others, as in the film *[Westworld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westworld_\(film\) "Westworld (film)")*, rebel against abuse by careless humans.[\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Van_Riper_11-33) Android hunter [Deckard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rick_Deckard "Rick Deckard") in *[Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Do_Androids_Dream_of_Electric_Sheep%3F "Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?")* and its film adaptation *[Blade Runner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blade_Runner "Blade Runner")* discovers that his targets appear to be, in some ways, more "human" than he is.[\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Van_Riper_11-33) The sequel *[Blade Runner 2049](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blade_Runner_2049 "Blade Runner 2049")* involves android hunter K, himself an android, discovering the same thing. Android stories, therefore, are not essentially stories "about" androids; they are stories about the [human condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_condition "Human condition") and what it means to be human.[\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Van_Riper_11-33)

One aspect of writing about the meaning of humanity is to use discrimination against androids as a mechanism for exploring racism in society, as in *Blade Runner*.[\[34\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Dinello76-34) Perhaps the clearest example of this is [John Brunner's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Brunner_\(novelist\) "John Brunner (novelist)") 1968 novel *[Into the Slave Nebula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Into_the_Slave_Nebula "Into the Slave Nebula")*, where the blue-skinned android slaves are explicitly shown to be fully human.[\[35\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-35) More recently, the androids [Bishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_\(Aliens\) "Bishop (Aliens)") and Annalee Call in the films *[Aliens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aliens_\(film\) "Aliens (film)")* and *[Alien Resurrection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alien_Resurrection "Alien Resurrection")* are used as vehicles for exploring how humans deal with the presence of an "[Other](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Other_\(philosophy\) "Other (philosophy)")".[\[36\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-36) The 2018 video game *[Detroit: Become Human](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit:_Become_Human "Detroit: Become Human")* also explores how androids are treated as second class citizens in a near future society.

Female androids, or "[gynoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gynoid "Gynoid")", are often seen in science fiction, and can be viewed as a continuation of the long tradition of men attempting to create the stereotypical "perfect woman".[\[37\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-melzer202-37) Examples include the [Greek myth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mythology "Greek mythology") of *[Pygmalion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pygmalion_\(mythology\) "Pygmalion (mythology)")* and [the female robot Maria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maschinenmensch "Maschinenmensch") in [Fritz Lang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Lang "Fritz Lang")'s *[Metropolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolis_\(1927_film\) "Metropolis (1927 film)")*. Some gynoids, like Pris in *Blade Runner*, are designed as sex-objects, with the intent of "pleasing men's violent sexual desires",[\[38\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-38) or as submissive, servile companions, such as in *[The Stepford Wives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Stepford_Wives "The Stepford Wives")*. Fiction about gynoids has therefore been described as reinforcing "essentialist ideas of femininity",[\[39\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-39) although others have suggested that the treatment of androids is a way of exploring racism and [misogyny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misogyny "Misogyny") in society.[\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Dinello77-40)

The 2015 Japanese film *[Sayonara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayonara_\(2015_film\) "Sayonara (2015 film)")*, starring [Geminoid F](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geminoid_F "Geminoid F"), was promoted as "the first movie to feature an android performing opposite a human actor".[\[41\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-41)
