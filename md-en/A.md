**A** or **a** is the first [letter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letter_\(alphabet\) "Letter (alphabet)") and the first [vowel letter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vowel_letter "Vowel letter") of the [Latin alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_alphabet "Latin alphabet"),[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-1)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-FOOTNOTESimpsonWeiner19891-2) used in the modern [English alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_alphabet "English alphabet"), and others worldwide. Its name in English is *[a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_alphabet#Letter_names "English alphabet")* (pronounced [/ˈeɪ/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*AY*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key")), plural *aes*.[\[nb 1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-3)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-FOOTNOTESimpsonWeiner19891-2)

It is similar in shape to the [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek "Ancient Greek") letter [alpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha "Alpha"), from which it derives.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcCarter197454-4) The [uppercase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uppercase "Uppercase") version consists of the two slanting sides of a triangle, crossed in the middle by a horizontal bar. The lowercase version is often written in one of two forms: the double-storey |a| and single-storey |ɑ|. The latter is commonly used in handwriting and fonts based on it, especially fonts intended to be read by children, and is also found in [italic type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italic_type "Italic type").

In English, *[a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_articles "English articles")* is the [indefinite article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indefinite_article "Indefinite article"), with the alternative form *an*.

## Name

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=A\&action=edit\&section=1 "Edit section: Name")]

In English, the name of the letter is the *long A* sound, pronounced [/ˈeɪ/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"). Its name in most other languages matches the letter's pronunciation in [open syllables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_syllable "Open syllable").

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/64/Pronunciation_of_the_name_of_the_letter_%E2%9F%A8a%E2%9F%A9_in_European_languages.png/460px-Pronunciation_of_the_name_of_the_letter_%E2%9F%A8a%E2%9F%A9_in_European_languages.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Pronunciation_of_the_name_of_the_letter_%E2%9F%A8a%E2%9F%A9_in_European_languages.png "Pronunciation of the name of the letter ⟨a⟩ in European languages. /a/ and /aː/ can differ phonetically between \[a], \[ä], \[æ] and \[ɑ] depending on the language.")

[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Pronunciation_of_the_name_of_the_letter_%E2%9F%A8a%E2%9F%A9_in_European_languages.png "File:Pronunciation of the name of the letter ⟨a⟩ in European languages.png")

Pronunciation of the name of the letter ⟨a⟩ in European languages. /a/ and /aː/ can differ phonetically between \[[a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_front_unrounded_vowel "Open front unrounded vowel")], \[[ä](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_central_unrounded_vowel "Open central unrounded vowel")], \[[æ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near-open_front_unrounded_vowel "Near-open front unrounded vowel")] and \[[ɑ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_back_unrounded_vowel "Open back unrounded vowel")] depending on the language.

## History

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=A\&action=edit\&section=2 "Edit section: History")]

The earliest known ancestor of A is *[aleph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleph "Aleph")*—the first letter of the [Phoenician alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenician_alphabet "Phoenician alphabet")[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHoiberg20101-5)—where it represented a [glottal stop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glottal_stop "Glottal stop") \[ʔ], as Phoenician only used [consonantal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consonant "Consonant") letters. In turn, the ancestor of aleph may have been a [pictogram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pictogram "Pictogram") of an ox head in [proto-Sinaitic script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Sinaitic_script "Proto-Sinaitic script")[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHall-Quest19971-6) influenced by [Egyptian hieroglyphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_hieroglyphs "Egyptian hieroglyphs"), styled as a triangular head with two horns extended.

When the [ancient Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greeks "Ancient Greeks") adopted the alphabet, they had no use for a letter representing a [glottal stop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glottal_stop "Glottal stop")—so they adapted sign to represent the vowel /[a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_front_unrounded_vowel "Open front unrounded vowel")/, calling the letter by the similar name *[alpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha "Alpha")*. In the earliest Greek inscriptions dating to the 8th century BC following the [Greek Dark Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Dark_Ages "Greek Dark Ages"), the letter rests upon its side. However, in the later [Greek alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_alphabet "Greek alphabet") it generally resembles the modern capital form—though many local varieties can be distinguished by the shortening of one leg, or by the angle at which the cross line is set.

The [Etruscans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruscans "Etruscans") brought the Greek alphabet to the [Italian Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Peninsula "Italian Peninsula"), and left the form of alpha unchanged. When the Romans adopted the [Etruscan alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruscan_alphabet "Etruscan alphabet") to write [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin"), the resulting form used in the [Latin script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_script "Latin script") would come to be used to write many other languages, including English.

### Typographic variants

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=A\&action=edit\&section=3 "Edit section: Typographic variants")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/40/A-small_glyphs.svg/120px-A-small_glyphs.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:A-small_glyphs.svg)

Different [glyphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glyphs "Glyphs") of the lowercase letter ⟨a⟩

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/ff/LowercaseA.svg/120px-LowercaseA.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:LowercaseA.svg)

[Allographs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allograph "Allograph") include a double-storey ⟨a⟩ and single-storey ⟨ɑ⟩.

During Roman times, there were many variant forms of the letter A. First was the monumental or lapidary style, which was used when inscribing on stone or other more permanent media. There was also a [cursive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cursive "Cursive") style used for everyday or utilitarian writing, which was done on more perishable surfaces. Due to the perishable nature of these surfaces, there are not as many examples of this style as there are of the monumental, but there are still many surviving examples of different types of cursive, such as [majuscule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majuscule "Majuscule") cursive, [minuscule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minuscule "Minuscule") cursive, and semi-cursive minuscule. Variants also existed that were intermediate between the monumental and cursive styles. The known variants include the early [semi-uncial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-uncial "Semi-uncial"), the uncial, and the later semi-uncial.[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDiringer20001-7)

At the end of the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire") (5th century AD), several variants of the cursive minuscule developed through Western Europe. Among these were the semi-cursive minuscule of Italy, the [Merovingian script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merovingian_script "Merovingian script") in France, the [Visigothic script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visigothic_script "Visigothic script") in Spain, and the [Insular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insular_script "Insular script") or Anglo-Irish semi-uncial or Anglo-Saxon majuscule of Great Britain. By the ninth century, the [Caroline script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caroline_script "Caroline script"), which was very similar to the present-day form, was the principal form used in book-making, before the advent of the printing press. This form was derived through a combining of prior forms.[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDiringer20001-7)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/58/Road_sign_on_the_R639_north_of_Urlingford%2C_Ireland_-_20090502.jpg/220px-Road_sign_on_the_R639_north_of_Urlingford%2C_Ireland_-_20090502.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Road_sign_on_the_R639_north_of_Urlingford,_Ireland_-_20090502.jpg)

Road sign in Ireland showing the Irish "Latin alpha" form of ⟨a⟩ in lower and upper case forms

15th-century Italy saw the formation of the two main variants that are known today. These variants, the *Italic* and *Roman* forms, were derived from the Caroline Script version. The Italic form ⟨ɑ⟩, also called *script a*, is often used in [handwriting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handwriting "Handwriting"); it consists of a circle with a vertical stroke on its right. In the hands of medieval Irish and English writers, this form gradually developed from a 5th-century form resembling the Greek letter [tau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tau "Tau") ⟨τ⟩.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHoiberg20101-5) The Roman form ⟨a⟩ is found in most printed material, and consists of a small loop with an arc over it.[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDiringer20001-7) Both derive from the majuscule form ⟨A⟩. In Greek handwriting, it was common to join the left leg and horizontal stroke into a single loop, as demonstrated by the uncial version shown. Many fonts then made the right leg vertical. In some of these, the [serif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serif "Serif") that began the right leg stroke developed into an arc, resulting in the printed form, while in others it was dropped, resulting in the modern handwritten form. Graphic designers refer to the *Italic* and *Roman* forms as *single-decker a* and *double decker a* respectively.

[Italic type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italic_type "Italic type") is commonly used to mark emphasis or more generally to distinguish one part of a text from the rest set in Roman type. There are some other cases aside from italic type where *script a* ⟨ɑ⟩, also called *[Latin alpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_alpha "Latin alpha")*, is used in contrast with Latin ⟨a⟩, such as in the [International Phonetic Alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Phonetic_Alphabet "International Phonetic Alphabet").

### English

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=A\&action=edit\&section=5 "Edit section: English")]

Further information: [Pronunciation of English ⟨a⟩](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pronunciation_of_English_%E2%9F%A8a%E2%9F%A9 "Pronunciation of English ⟨a⟩")

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6b/A_for_apple.jpg/220px-A_for_apple.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:A_for_apple.jpg)

In English language education, the word *apple* is consistently associated with the letter A.[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-8)

In modern [English orthography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_orthography "English orthography"), the letter ⟨a⟩ represents at least seven different vowel sounds, here represented using the vowels of [Received Pronunciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Received_Pronunciation "Received Pronunciation"), with [effects of ⟨r⟩](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English-language_vowel_changes_before_historic_/r/ "English-language vowel changes before historic /r/") ignored and mergers in [General American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_American "General American") mentioned where relevant:

- the [near-open front unrounded vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near-open_front_unrounded_vowel "Near-open front unrounded vowel") /æ/ as in *pad*
- the [open back unrounded vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_back_unrounded_vowel "Open back unrounded vowel") /ɑː/ as in *father*—merged with /[ɒ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_back_rounded_vowel "Open back rounded vowel")/ as /[ɑ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_back_unrounded_vowel "Open back unrounded vowel")/ in [General American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_American "General American")—which is closer to its original Latin and Greek sound[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHall-Quest19971-6)
- the [open back rounded vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_back_rounded_vowel "Open back rounded vowel") /ɒ/ (merged with /ɑː/ as /[ɑ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_back_unrounded_vowel "Open back unrounded vowel")/ in [General American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_American "General American")) in *was* and *what*[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHoiberg20101-5)
- the [open-mid back rounded vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-mid_back_rounded_vowel "Open-mid back rounded vowel") /ɔː/ in *water*
- the [diphthong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diphthong "Diphthong") /eɪ/ as in *ace* and *major*, usually when ⟨a⟩ is followed by one, or occasionally two, consonants and then another vowel letter—this results from [Middle English lengthening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_English_lengthening "Middle English lengthening") followed by the [Great Vowel Shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Vowel_Shift "Great Vowel Shift")
- a [schwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwa "Schwa") /ə/ in many unstressed syllables, as in *about*, *comma*, *solar*

The double ⟨aa⟩ sequence does not occur in native English words, but is found in some words derived from foreign languages such as *Aaron* and *[aardvark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aardvark "Aardvark")*.[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-9) However, ⟨a⟩ occurs in [many common digraphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Latin-script_digraphs "List of Latin-script digraphs"), all with their own sound or sounds, particularly ⟨ai⟩, ⟨au⟩, ⟨aw⟩, ⟨ay⟩, ⟨ea⟩ and ⟨oa⟩.

⟨a⟩ is the third-most-commonly used letter in English after ⟨e⟩ and ⟨t⟩, as well as in French; it is the second most common in Spanish, and the most common in Portuguese. ⟨a⟩ represents approximately 8.2% of letters as used in English texts;[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-10) the figure is around 7.6% in French[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-11) 11.5% in Spanish,[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-12) and 14.6% in Portuguese.[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-13)

### Other languages

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=A\&action=edit\&section=6 "Edit section: Other languages")]

In most languages that use the Latin alphabet, ⟨a⟩ denotes an open unrounded vowel, such as /[a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_front_unrounded_vowel "Open front unrounded vowel")/, /[ä](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_central_unrounded_vowel "Open central unrounded vowel")/, or /[ɑ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_back_unrounded_vowel "Open back unrounded vowel")/. An exception is [Saanich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saanich_dialect "Saanich dialect"), in which ⟨a⟩—and the glyph ⟨[Á](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%81 "Á")⟩—stands for a [close-mid front unrounded vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Close-mid_front_unrounded_vowel "Close-mid front unrounded vowel") /e/.

### Other systems

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=A\&action=edit\&section=7 "Edit section: Other systems")]

- In the [International Phonetic Alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Phonetic_Alphabet "International Phonetic Alphabet"), ⟨a⟩ is used for the [open front unrounded vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_front_unrounded_vowel "Open front unrounded vowel"), ⟨ä⟩ is used for the [open central unrounded vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_central_unrounded_vowel "Open central unrounded vowel"), and ⟨ɑ⟩ is used for the [open back unrounded vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_back_unrounded_vowel "Open back unrounded vowel").
- In [X-SAMPA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-SAMPA "X-SAMPA"), ⟨a⟩ is used for the [open front unrounded vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_front_unrounded_vowel "Open front unrounded vowel") and ⟨A⟩ is used for the [open back unrounded vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_back_unrounded_vowel "Open back unrounded vowel").

## Other uses

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=A\&action=edit\&section=8 "Edit section: Other uses")]

Main article: [A (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_\(disambiguation\) "A (disambiguation)")

- When using [base-16](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base-16 "Base-16") notation, A or a is the conventional numeral corresponding to the number 10.
- In [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra "Algebra"), the letter *a* along with various other letters of the alphabet is often used to denote a [variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variable_\(mathematics\) "Variable (mathematics)"), with various conventional meanings in different areas of mathematics. In 1637, [René Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_Descartes "René Descartes") "invented the convention of representing unknowns in equations by x, y, and z, and knowns by a, b, and c",[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-14) and this convention is still often followed, especially in elementary algebra.
- In [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry"), capital Latin letters are used to denote objects including [line segments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_segment "Line segment"), [lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_\(geometry\) "Line (geometry)"), and [rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_\(geometry\)#Ray "Line (geometry)")[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDiringer20001-7) A capital A is also typically used as one of the letters to represent an angle in a [triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangle "Triangle"), the lowercase a representing the side opposite angle A.[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHall-Quest19971-6)
- A is often used to denote something or someone of a better or more prestigious quality or status: A−, A or A+, the best grade that can be assigned by teachers for students' schoolwork; "A grade" for clean restaurants; [A-list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A-list "A-list") celebrities, [A1 at Lloyd's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A1_at_Lloyd%27s "A1 at Lloyd's") for shipping, etc. Such associations can have a [motivating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motivation "Motivation") effect, as exposure to the letter A has been found to improve performance, when compared with other letters.[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-FOOTNOTECianiSheldon201099%E2%80%93100-15)
- A is used to denote size, as in a narrow size shoe,[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHall-Quest19971-6) or a small cup size in a [brassiere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brassiere "Brassiere").[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-16)

### Computing

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=A\&action=edit\&section=14 "Edit section: Computing")]

The Latin letters ⟨A⟩ and ⟨a⟩ have [Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode "Unicode") [encodings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code "Code") U+0041 A LATIN CAPITAL LETTER A and U+0061 a LATIN SMALL LETTER A. These are the same [code points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_point "Code point") as those used in [ASCII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASCII "ASCII") and [ISO 8859](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_8859 "ISO 8859"). There are also [precomposed character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precomposed_character "Precomposed character") encodings for ⟨A⟩ and ⟨a⟩ with diacritics, for most of those listed [above](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#Related_characters_in_the_Latin_alphabet); the remainder are produced using [combining diacritics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combining_diacritic "Combining diacritic").

Variant forms of the letter have unique code points for specialist use: the [alphanumeric symbols set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Alphanumeric_Symbols "Mathematical Alphanumeric Symbols") in mathematics and science, [Latin alpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_alpha "Latin alpha") in linguistics, and [halfwidth and fullwidth forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halfwidth_and_fullwidth_forms "Halfwidth and fullwidth forms") for legacy [CJK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CJK_characters "CJK characters") font compatibility. The Cyrillic and Greek [homoglyphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homoglyph "Homoglyph") of the Latin ⟨A⟩ have separate encodings U+0410 А [CYRILLIC CAPITAL LETTER A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_\(Cyrillic\) "A (Cyrillic)") and U+0391 Α [GREEK CAPITAL LETTER ALPHA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha "Alpha").
