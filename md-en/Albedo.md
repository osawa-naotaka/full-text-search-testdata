# Albedo

Ratio of how much light is reflected back from a body

For other uses, see [Albedo (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albedo_\(disambiguation\) "Albedo (disambiguation)").

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/72/Greenland_Albedo_Change.png/220px-Greenland_Albedo_Change.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Greenland_Albedo_Change.png)

Albedo change in [Greenland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland "Greenland"): the map shows the difference between the amount of sunlight [Greenland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland "Greenland") reflected in the summer of 2011 versus the average percent it reflected between 2000 and 2006. Some areas reflect close to 20 percent less light than a decade ago.[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-:2-1)

**Albedo** ([/ælˈbiːdoʊ/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*al-BEE-doh*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key"); from [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language "Latin language") *albedo* 'whiteness') is the fraction of [sunlight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunlight "Sunlight") that is [diffusely reflected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffuse_reflection "Diffuse reflection") by a body. It is measured on a scale from 0 (corresponding to a [black body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_body "Black body") that absorbs all incident radiation) to 1 (corresponding to a body that reflects all incident radiation). *Surface albedo* is defined as the ratio of [radiosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiosity_\(radiometry\) "Radiosity (radiometry)") *J*e to the [irradiance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irradiance "Irradiance") *E*e (flux per unit area) received by a surface.[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-2) The proportion reflected is not only determined by properties of the surface itself, but also by the spectral and angular distribution of solar radiation reaching the Earth's surface.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-3) These factors vary with atmospheric composition, geographic location, and time (see [position of the Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_of_the_Sun "Position of the Sun")).

While directional-hemispherical [reflectance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflectance "Reflectance") factor is calculated for a single angle of incidence (i.e., for a given position of the Sun), albedo is the directional integration of reflectance over all solar angles in a given period. The temporal resolution may range from seconds (as obtained from flux measurements) to daily, monthly, or annual averages.

Unless given for a specific wavelength (spectral albedo), albedo refers to the entire spectrum of solar radiation.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-4) Due to measurement constraints, it is often given for the spectrum in which most solar energy reaches the surface (between 0.3 and 3 μm). This spectrum includes [visible light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_spectrum "Visible spectrum") (0.4–0.7 μm), which explains why surfaces with a low albedo appear dark (e.g., trees absorb most radiation), whereas surfaces with a high albedo appear bright (e.g., snow reflects most radiation).

[Ice–albedo feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice%E2%80%93albedo_feedback "Ice–albedo feedback") is a [positive feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_feedback "Positive feedback") climate process where a change in the area of [ice caps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_caps "Ice caps"), [glaciers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glaciers "Glaciers"), and [sea ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_ice "Sea ice") alters the albedo and surface temperature of a planet. [Ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice "Ice") is very reflective, therefore it reflects far more solar energy back to space than the other types of land area or open water. Ice–albedo feedback plays an important role in global [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_\(general_concept\) "Climate change (general concept)").[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-5) Albedo is an important concept in [climate science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_science "Climate science").

Any albedo in visible light falls within a range of about 0.9 for fresh snow to about 0.04 for charcoal, one of the darkest substances. Deeply shadowed cavities can achieve an effective albedo approaching the zero of a [black body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_body "Black body"). When seen from a distance, the ocean surface has a low albedo, as do most forests, whereas desert areas have some of the highest albedos among landforms. Most land areas are in an albedo range of 0.1 to 0.4.[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-PhysicsWorld-14) The average albedo of [Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth "Earth") is about 0.3.[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Goode-15) This is far higher than for the ocean primarily because of the contribution of clouds.

Earth's surface albedo is regularly estimated via [Earth observation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_observation "Earth observation") satellite sensors such as [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA "NASA")'s [MODIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MODIS "MODIS") instruments on board the [Terra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terra_\(satellite\) "Terra (satellite)") and [Aqua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqua_\(satellite\) "Aqua (satellite)") satellites, and the CERES instrument on the [Suomi NPP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suomi_NPP "Suomi NPP") and [JPSS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_Polar_Satellite_System "Joint Polar Satellite System"). As the amount of reflected radiation is only measured for a single direction by satellite, not all directions, a mathematical model is used to translate a sample set of satellite reflectance measurements into estimates of [directional-hemispherical reflectance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directional-hemispherical_reflectance "Directional-hemispherical reflectance") and bi-hemispherical reflectance (e.g.,[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-NASA-16)). These calculations are based on the [bidirectional reflectance distribution function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bidirectional_reflectance_distribution_function "Bidirectional reflectance distribution function") (BRDF), which describes how the reflectance of a given surface depends on the view angle of the observer and the solar angle. BDRF can facilitate translations of observations of reflectance into albedo.\[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Earth's average surface temperature due to its albedo and the [greenhouse effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_effect "Greenhouse effect") is currently about 15 °C (59 °F). If Earth were frozen entirely (and hence be more reflective), the average temperature of the planet would drop below −40 °C (−40 °F).[\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-washington-17) If only the continental land masses became covered by glaciers, the mean temperature of the planet would drop to about 0 °C (32 °F).[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-clim-past-18) In contrast, if the entire Earth was covered by water – a so-called [ocean planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_planet "Ocean planet") – the average temperature on the planet would rise to almost 27 °C (81 °F).[\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Smith_Robin-19)

In 2021, scientists reported that Earth dimmed by \~0.5% over two decades (1998–2017) as measured by earthshine using modern photometric techniques. This may have both been co-caused by [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change "Climate change") as well as a substantial increase in global warming. However, the link to climate change has not been explored to date and it is unclear whether or not this represents an ongoing trend.[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-20)[\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-21)

### White-sky, black-sky, and blue-sky albedo

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Albedo\&action=edit\&section=2 "Edit section: White-sky, black-sky, and blue-sky albedo")]

For land surfaces, it has been shown that the albedo at a particular [solar zenith angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_zenith_angle "Solar zenith angle") *θ**i* can be approximated by the proportionate sum of two terms:

- the [directional-hemispherical reflectance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directional-hemispherical_reflectance "Directional-hemispherical reflectance") at that solar zenith angle, ![{\displaystyle {{\bar {\alpha }}(\theta \_{i})}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d21d2965b6b0594054d53e34fcd5c624c7f47cc7), sometimes referred to as black-sky albedo, and
- the [bi-hemispherical reflectance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bi-hemispherical_reflectance "Bi-hemispherical reflectance"), ![{\displaystyle {\bar {\bar {\alpha }}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a2a19826b7107e707aead3e838e72304d5f90277), sometimes referred to as white-sky albedo.

with ![{\displaystyle {1-D}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fd202359a35c5d9ea37c6dd482127cb0691e1f92) being the proportion of direct radiation from a given solar angle, and ![{\displaystyle {D}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2b48bdc67a19ddefb0aa409e442e815517a87e7b) being the proportion of diffuse illumination, the actual albedo ![{\displaystyle {\alpha }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6289622b3b197c568f6e73e678814e9cb4c8a81b) (also called blue-sky albedo) can then be given as:

- ![{\displaystyle \alpha =(1-D){\bar {\alpha }}(\theta \_{i})+D{\bar {\bar {\alpha }}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ae29d63f9daf13713460293e29346313642a5713)

This formula is important because it allows the albedo to be calculated for any given illumination conditions from a knowledge of the intrinsic properties of the surface.[\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-BlueskyAlbedo-22)

### Changes to albedo due to human activities

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Albedo\&action=edit\&section=3 "Edit section: Changes to albedo due to human activities")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/90/%28Almer%C3%ADa%29_Greenhouses_of_Almer%C3%ADa%2C_Spain_%28cropped%29.jpg/220px-%28Almer%C3%ADa%29_Greenhouses_of_Almer%C3%ADa%2C_Spain_%28cropped%29.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:\(Almer%C3%ADa\)_Greenhouses_of_Almer%C3%ADa,_Spain_\(cropped\).jpg)

Greenhouses of Almería, Spain

Human activities (e.g., deforestation, farming, and urbanization) change the albedo of various areas around the globe.[\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-23) [Human impacts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_impact_on_the_environment "Human impact on the environment") to "the physical properties of the land surface can perturb the climate by altering the Earth’s radiative energy balance" even on a small scale or when undetected by satellites.[\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-:0-24)

[Urbanization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbanization "Urbanization") generally decreases albedo (commonly being 0.01–0.02 lower than adjacent [croplands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croplands "Croplands")), which contributes to [global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming "Global warming"). Deliberately increasing albedo in urban areas can mitigate the [urban heat island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island "Urban heat island") effect. An estimate in 2022 found that on a global scale, "an albedo increase of 0.1 in worldwide urban areas would result in a cooling effect that is equivalent to absorbing \~44 [Gt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gigatons "Gigatons") of CO2 emissions."[\[25\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-25)

Intentionally enhancing the albedo of the Earth's surface, along with its daytime [thermal emittance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_emittance "Thermal emittance"), has been proposed as a [solar radiation management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_Radiation_Management "Solar Radiation Management") strategy to mitigate [energy crises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_crisis "Energy crisis") and global warming known as [passive daytime radiative cooling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passive_daytime_radiative_cooling "Passive daytime radiative cooling") (PDRC).[\[26\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-:1-26)[\[27\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-:5-27)[\[28\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-:02-28) Efforts toward widespread implementation of PDRCs may focus on maximizing the albedo of surfaces from very low to high values, so long as a thermal emittance of at least 90% can be achieved.[\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-:22-29)

The tens of thousands of [hectares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hectare "Hectare") of greenhouses in [Almería, Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almer%C3%ADa,_Spain "Almería, Spain") form a large expanse of whitened plastic roofs. A 2008 study found that this anthropogenic change lowered the local surface area temperature of the high-albedo area, although changes were localized.[\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-:0-24) A follow-up study found that "CO2-eq. emissions associated to changes in surface albedo are a consequence of land transformation" and can reduce surface temperature increases associated with climate change.[\[30\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-30)

### Illumination

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Albedo\&action=edit\&section=5 "Edit section: Illumination")]

Albedo is not directly dependent on the illumination because changing the amount of incoming light proportionally changes the amount of reflected light, except in circumstances where a change in illumination induces a change in the Earth's surface at that location (e.g. through melting of reflective ice). However, albedo and illumination both vary by latitude. Albedo is highest near the poles and lowest in the subtropics, with a local maximum in the tropics.[\[31\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Winston-31)

### Insolation effects

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Albedo\&action=edit\&section=6 "Edit section: Insolation effects")]

The intensity of albedo temperature effects depends on the amount of albedo and the level of local [insolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insolation "Insolation") ([solar irradiance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_irradiance "Solar irradiance")); high albedo areas in the [Arctic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic "Arctic") and [Antarctic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctic "Antarctic") regions are cold due to low insolation, whereas areas such as the [Sahara Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahara_Desert "Sahara Desert"), which also have a relatively high albedo, will be hotter due to high insolation. [Tropical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical "Tropical") and [sub-tropical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-tropical "Sub-tropical") [rainforest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainforest "Rainforest") areas have low albedo, and are much hotter than their [temperate forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperate_forest "Temperate forest") counterparts, which have lower insolation. Because insolation plays such a big role in the heating and cooling effects of albedo, high insolation areas like the tropics will tend to show a more pronounced fluctuation in local temperature when local albedo changes.[\[32\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-32)

Arctic regions notably release more heat back into space than what they absorb, effectively cooling the [Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth "Earth"). This has been a concern since arctic ice and [snow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snow "Snow") has been melting at higher rates due to higher temperatures, creating regions in the arctic that are notably darker (being water or ground which is darker color) and reflects less heat back into space. This [feedback loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice%E2%80%93albedo_feedback "Ice–albedo feedback") results in a reduced albedo effect.[\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-33)

### Climate and weather

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Albedo\&action=edit\&section=7 "Edit section: Climate and weather")]

See also: [Climate change feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_feedback "Climate change feedback")

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a4/20220726_Feedbacks_affecting_global_warming_and_climate_change_-_block_diagram.svg/330px-20220726_Feedbacks_affecting_global_warming_and_climate_change_-_block_diagram.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:20220726_Feedbacks_affecting_global_warming_and_climate_change_-_block_diagram.svg)

Some effects of global warming can either enhance ([positive feedbacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_feedback "Positive feedback") such as the ice-albedo feedback) or inhibit ([negative feedbacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_feedback "Negative feedback")) warming.[\[34\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-NASA_IntegratedSystem-34)[\[35\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-IPCC_AR6_SGI_FigTS.17-35)

Albedo affects [climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate "Climate") by determining how much [radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation "Radiation") a planet absorbs.[\[36\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-36) The uneven heating of Earth from albedo variations between land, ice, or ocean surfaces can drive [weather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather "Weather").\[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

The response of the climate system to an initial forcing is modified by feedbacks: increased by ["self-reinforcing" or "positive" feedbacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_feedback "Positive feedback") and reduced by ["balancing" or "negative" feedbacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_feedback "Negative feedback").[\[37\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-37) The main reinforcing feedbacks are the [water-vapour feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapour_feedback "Water vapour feedback"), the [ice–albedo feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice%E2%80%93albedo_feedback "Ice–albedo feedback"), and the net effect of clouds.[\[38\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-38): 58 

### Albedo–temperature feedback

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Albedo\&action=edit\&section=8 "Edit section: Albedo–temperature feedback")]

Further information: [Ice–albedo feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice%E2%80%93albedo_feedback "Ice–albedo feedback")

When an area's albedo changes due to snowfall, a snow–temperature [feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feedback "Feedback") results. A layer of snowfall increases local albedo, reflecting away sunlight, leading to local cooling. In principle, if no outside temperature change affects this area (e.g., a warm [air mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_mass "Air mass")), the raised albedo and lower temperature would maintain the current snow and invite further snowfall, deepening the snow–temperature feedback. However, because local [weather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather "Weather") is dynamic due to the change of [seasons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Season "Season"), eventually warm air masses and a more direct angle of sunlight (higher [insolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insolation "Insolation")) cause melting. When the melted area reveals surfaces with lower albedo, such as grass, soil, or ocean, the effect is reversed: the darkening surface lowers albedo, increasing local temperatures, which induces more melting and thus reducing the albedo further, resulting in still more heating.

### Snow

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Albedo\&action=edit\&section=9 "Edit section: Snow")]

Snow albedo is highly variable, ranging from as high as 0.9 for freshly fallen snow, to about 0.4 for melting snow, and as low as 0.2 for dirty snow.[\[39\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-39) Over [Antarctica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctica "Antarctica"), snow albedo averages a little more than 0.8. If a marginally snow-covered area warms, snow tends to melt, lowering the albedo, and hence leading to more snowmelt because more radiation is being absorbed by the snowpack (referred to as the [ice–albedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice%E2%80%93albedo_feedback "Ice–albedo feedback") [positive feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_feedback "Positive feedback")).

In [Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switzerland "Switzerland"), the citizens have been protecting their glaciers with large white tarpaulins to slow down the ice melt. These large white sheets are helping to reject the rays from the sun and defecting the heat. Although this method is very expensive, it has been shown to work, reducing snow and ice melt by 60%.[\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-40)

Just as fresh snow has a higher albedo than does dirty snow, the albedo of snow-covered sea ice is far higher than that of sea water. Sea water absorbs more [solar radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_radiation "Solar radiation") than would the same surface covered with reflective snow. When sea ice melts, either due to a rise in sea temperature or in response to increased solar radiation from above, the snow-covered surface is reduced, and more surface of sea water is exposed, so the rate of energy absorption increases. The extra absorbed energy heats the sea water, which in turn increases the rate at which sea ice melts. As with the preceding example of snowmelt, the process of melting of sea ice is thus another example of a positive feedback.[\[41\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-41) Both positive feedback loops have long been recognized as important for [global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming "Global warming").\[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

[Cryoconite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryoconite "Cryoconite"), powdery windblown [dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust "Dust") containing soot, sometimes reduces albedo on glaciers and ice sheets.[\[42\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Nat._Geo-42)

The dynamical nature of albedo in response to positive feedback, together with the effects of small errors in the measurement of albedo, can lead to large errors in energy estimates. Because of this, in order to reduce the error of energy estimates, it is important to measure the albedo of snow-covered areas through [remote sensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_sensing "Remote sensing") techniques rather than applying a single value for albedo over broad regions.\[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

### Small-scale effects

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Albedo\&action=edit\&section=10 "Edit section: Small-scale effects")]

Albedo works on a smaller scale, too. In sunlight, dark clothes absorb more heat and light-coloured clothes reflect it better, thus allowing some control over body temperature by exploiting the albedo effect of the colour of external clothing.[\[43\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-ranknfile-ue-43)

### Solar photovoltaic effects

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Albedo\&action=edit\&section=11 "Edit section: Solar photovoltaic effects")]

Albedo can affect the [electrical energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_energy "Electrical energy") output of solar [photovoltaic devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photovoltaic_system "Photovoltaic system"). For example, the effects of a spectrally responsive albedo are illustrated by the differences between the spectrally weighted albedo of solar photovoltaic technology based on hydrogenated amorphous silicon (a-Si:H) and crystalline silicon (c-Si)-based compared to traditional spectral-integrated albedo predictions. Research showed impacts of over 10% for vertically (90°) mounted systems, but such effects were substantially lower for systems with lower surface tilts.[\[44\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-44) Spectral albedo strongly affects the performance of [bifacial solar cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bifacial_solar_cells "Bifacial solar cells") where rear surface performance gains of over 20% have been observed for c-Si cells installed above healthy vegetation.[\[45\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-45) An analysis on the bias due to the specular reflectivity of 22 commonly occurring surface materials (both human-made and natural) provided effective albedo values for simulating the performance of seven photovoltaic materials mounted on three common photovoltaic system topologies: industrial (solar farms), commercial flat rooftops and residential pitched-roof applications.[\[46\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-46)

### Trees

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Albedo\&action=edit\&section=12 "Edit section: Trees")]

Forests generally have a low albedo because the majority of the ultraviolet and [visible spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_spectrum "Visible spectrum") is absorbed through [photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis "Photosynthesis"). For this reason, the greater heat absorption by trees could offset some of the carbon benefits of [afforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afforestation "Afforestation") (or offset the negative climate impacts of [deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation "Deforestation")). In other words: The [climate change mitigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_mitigation "Climate change mitigation") effect of [carbon sequestration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_sequestration "Carbon sequestration") by forests is partially counterbalanced in that [reforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reforestation "Reforestation") can decrease the reflection of sunlight (albedo).[\[47\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-47)

In the case of evergreen forests with seasonal snow cover, albedo reduction may be significant enough for deforestation to cause a net cooling effect.[\[48\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Betts-48) Trees also impact climate in extremely complicated ways through [evapotranspiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evapotranspiration "Evapotranspiration"). The water vapor causes cooling on the land surface, causes heating where it condenses, acts as strong greenhouse gas, and can increase albedo when it condenses into clouds.[\[49\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-49) Scientists generally treat evapotranspiration as a net cooling impact, and the net climate impact of albedo and evapotranspiration changes from deforestation depends greatly on local climate.[\[50\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-50)

Mid-to-high-latitude forests have a much lower albedo during snow seasons than flat ground, thus contributing to warming. Modeling that compares the effects of albedo differences between forests and grasslands suggests that expanding the land area of forests in temperate zones offers only a temporary mitigation benefit.[\[51\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-51)[\[52\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-52)[\[53\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-53)[\[54\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-54)

In seasonally snow-covered zones, winter albedos of treeless areas are 10% to 50% higher than nearby forested areas because snow does not cover the trees as readily. [Deciduous trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deciduous_trees "Deciduous trees") have an albedo value of about 0.15 to 0.18 whereas [coniferous trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coniferous_trees "Coniferous trees") have a value of about 0.09 to 0.15.[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-mmutrees-9) Variation in summer albedo across both forest types is associated with maximum rates of photosynthesis because plants with high growth capacity display a greater fraction of their foliage for direct interception of incoming radiation in the upper canopy.[\[55\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Ollinger-55) The result is that wavelengths of light not used in photosynthesis are more likely to be reflected back to space rather than being absorbed by other surfaces lower in the canopy.

Studies by the [Hadley Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadley_Centre "Hadley Centre") have investigated the relative (generally warming) effect of albedo change and (cooling) effect of [carbon sequestration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_sequestration "Carbon sequestration") on planting forests. They found that new forests in tropical and midlatitude areas tended to cool; new forests in high latitudes (e.g., Siberia) were neutral or perhaps warming.[\[48\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Betts-48)

Research in 2023, drawing from 176 flux stations globally, revealed a climate trade-off: increased carbon uptake from [afforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afforestation "Afforestation") results in reduced albedo. Initially, this reduction may lead to moderate global warming over a span of approximately 20 years, but it is expected to transition into significant cooling thereafter.[\[56\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-56)

### Water

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Albedo\&action=edit\&section=13 "Edit section: Water")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/7/7f/Water_reflectivity.jpg/290px-Water_reflectivity.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Water_reflectivity.jpg)

Reflectivity of smooth water at 20 °C (68 °F) (refractive index=1.333)

Water reflects light very differently from typical terrestrial materials. The reflectivity of a water surface is calculated using the [Fresnel equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresnel_equations "Fresnel equations").

At the scale of the wavelength of light even wavy water is always smooth so the light is reflected in a locally [specular manner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specular_reflection "Specular reflection") (not [diffusely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffuse_reflection "Diffuse reflection")). The glint of light off water is a commonplace effect of this. At small [angles of incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_incidence_\(optics\) "Angle of incidence (optics)") light, [waviness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waviness "Waviness") results in reduced reflectivity because of the steepness of the reflectivity-vs.-incident-angle curve and a locally increased average incident angle.[\[57\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Fresnel-57)

Although the reflectivity of water is very low at low and medium angles of incident light, it becomes very high at high angles of incident light such as those that occur on the illuminated side of Earth near the [terminator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminator_\(solar\) "Terminator (solar)") (early morning, late afternoon, and near the poles). However, as mentioned above, waviness causes an appreciable reduction. Because light specularly reflected from water does not usually reach the viewer, water is usually considered to have a very low albedo in spite of its high reflectivity at high angles of incident light.

Note that white caps on waves look white (and have high albedo) because the water is foamed up, so there are many superimposed bubble surfaces which reflect, adding up their reflectivities. Fresh 'black' ice exhibits Fresnel reflection. Snow on top of this sea ice increases the albedo to 0.9.[\[58\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-58)

### Clouds

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Albedo\&action=edit\&section=14 "Edit section: Clouds")]

[Cloud albedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_albedo "Cloud albedo") has substantial influence over atmospheric temperatures. Different types of clouds exhibit different reflectivity, theoretically ranging in albedo from a minimum of near 0 to a maximum approaching 0.8. "On any given day, about half of Earth is covered by clouds, which reflect more sunlight than land and water. Clouds keep Earth cool by reflecting sunlight, but they can also serve as blankets to trap warmth."[\[59\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-livescience-59)

Albedo and climate in some areas are affected by artificial clouds, such as those created by the [contrails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contrail "Contrail") of heavy commercial airliner traffic.[\[60\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-60) A study following the burning of the Kuwaiti oil fields during Iraqi occupation showed that temperatures under the burning oil fires were as much as 10 °C (18 °F) colder than temperatures several miles away under clear skies.[\[61\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-harvard-61)

### Aerosol effects

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Albedo\&action=edit\&section=15 "Edit section: Aerosol effects")]

[Aerosols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerosols "Aerosols") (very fine particles/droplets in the atmosphere) have both direct and indirect effects on Earth's radiative balance. The direct (albedo) effect is generally to cool the planet; the indirect effect (the particles act as [cloud condensation nuclei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_condensation_nuclei "Cloud condensation nuclei") and thereby change cloud properties) is less certain.[\[62\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-girda-62)

### Black carbon

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Albedo\&action=edit\&section=16 "Edit section: Black carbon")]

Another albedo-related effect on the climate is from [black carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_carbon "Black carbon") particles. The size of this effect is difficult to quantify: the [Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intergovernmental_Panel_on_Climate_Change "Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change") estimates that the global mean [radiative forcing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiative_forcing "Radiative forcing") for black carbon aerosols from fossil fuels is +0.2 W m−2, with a range +0.1 to +0.4 W m−2.[\[63\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-girda_1-63) Black carbon is a bigger cause of the melting of the polar ice cap in the Arctic than carbon dioxide due to its effect on the albedo.[\[64\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-64)\[*[failed verification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability "Wikipedia:Verifiability")*]

## Astronomical albedo

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Albedo\&action=edit\&section=17 "Edit section: Astronomical albedo")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/36/Titan_and_Saturn_-_May_6_2012_-_combined_%2835516187116%29.jpg/260px-Titan_and_Saturn_-_May_6_2012_-_combined_%2835516187116%29.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Titan_and_Saturn_-_May_6_2012_-_combined_\(35516187116\).jpg)

The moon [Titan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titan_\(moon\) "Titan (moon)") is darker than [Saturn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn "Saturn") even though they receive the same amount of sunlight. This is due to a difference in albedo (0.22 versus 0.499 in [geometric albedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_albedo "Geometric albedo")).

In astronomy, the term **albedo** can be defined in several different ways, depending upon the application and the wavelength of electromagnetic radiation involved.

### Optical or visual albedo

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Albedo\&action=edit\&section=18 "Edit section: Optical or visual albedo")]

The albedos of [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet "Planet"), [satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_satellite "Natural satellite") and [minor planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_planet "Minor planet") such as [asteroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid "Asteroid") can be used to infer much about their properties. The study of albedos, their dependence on wavelength, lighting angle ("phase angle"), and variation in time composes a major part of the astronomical field of [photometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photometry_\(astronomy\) "Photometry (astronomy)"). For small and far objects that cannot be resolved by telescopes, much of what we know comes from the study of their albedos. For example, the absolute albedo can indicate the surface ice content of outer [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System "Solar System") objects, the variation of albedo with phase angle gives information about [regolith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regolith "Regolith") properties, whereas unusually high radar albedo is indicative of high metal content in [asteroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid "Asteroid").

[Enceladus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enceladus "Enceladus"), a moon of Saturn, has one of the highest known optical albedos of any body in the Solar System, with an albedo of 0.99. Another notable high-albedo body is [Eris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eris_\(dwarf_planet\) "Eris (dwarf planet)"), with an albedo of 0.96.[\[65\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-sicardy-65) Many small objects in the outer Solar System[\[66\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-tnoalbedo-66) and [asteroid belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid_belt "Asteroid belt") have low albedos down to about 0.05.[\[67\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-astalbedo-67) A typical [comet nucleus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comet_nucleus "Comet nucleus") has an albedo of 0.04.[\[68\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-dark-68) Such a dark surface is thought to be indicative of a primitive and heavily [space weathered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_weathering "Space weathering") surface containing some [organic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_compound "Organic compound").

The overall albedo of the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon "Moon") is measured to be around 0.14,[\[69\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-CERESmoon-69) but it is strongly directional and non-[Lambertian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambertian_reflectance "Lambertian reflectance"), displaying also a strong [opposition effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opposition_effect "Opposition effect").[\[70\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-medkeff-70) Although such reflectance properties are different from those of any terrestrial terrains, they are typical of the [regolith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regolith "Regolith") surfaces of airless Solar System bodies.

Two common optical albedos that are used in astronomy are the (V-band) [geometric albedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_albedo "Geometric albedo") (measuring brightness when illumination comes from directly behind the observer) and the [Bond albedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_albedo "Bond albedo") (measuring total proportion of electromagnetic energy reflected). Their values can differ significantly, which is a common source of confusion.

In detailed studies, the directional reflectance properties of astronomical bodies are often expressed in terms of the five [Hapke parameters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hapke_parameters "Hapke parameters") which semi-empirically describe the variation of albedo with [phase angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_angle_\(astronomy\) "Phase angle (astronomy)"), including a characterization of the opposition effect of [regolith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regolith "Regolith") surfaces. One of these five parameters is yet another type of albedo called the [single-scattering albedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-scattering_albedo "Single-scattering albedo"). It is used to define scattering of electromagnetic waves on small particles. It depends on properties of the material ([refractive index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refractive_index "Refractive index")), the size of the particle, and the wavelength of the incoming radiation.

An important relationship between an object's astronomical (geometric) albedo, [absolute magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_magnitude#Absolute_magnitude_for_planets_\(H\) "Absolute magnitude") and diameter is given by:[\[81\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-bruton-81) ![{\displaystyle A=\left({\frac {1329\times 10^{-H/5}}{D}}\right)^{2},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0ab01ee571320948a61e4a2d02540e97ff1cc749) where ![{\displaystyle A}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7daff47fa58cdfd29dc333def748ff5fa4c923e3) is the astronomical albedo, ![{\displaystyle D}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f34a0c600395e5d4345287e21fb26efd386990e6) is the diameter in kilometers, and ![{\displaystyle H}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/75a9edddcca2f782014371f75dca39d7e13a9c1b) is the absolute magnitude.

### Radar albedo

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Albedo\&action=edit\&section=19 "Edit section: Radar albedo")]

In planetary [radar astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_astronomy "Radar astronomy"), a microwave (or radar) pulse is transmitted toward a planetary target (e.g. Moon, asteroid, etc.) and the echo from the target is measured. In most instances, the transmitted pulse is [circularly polarized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_polarization "Circular polarization") and the received pulse is measured in the same sense of polarization as the transmitted pulse (SC) and the opposite sense (OC).[\[82\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Ostro_Planetary_Radar-82)[\[83\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Ostro_Asteroid_Radar-83) The echo power is measured in terms of [radar cross-section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_cross-section "Radar cross-section"), ![{\displaystyle {\sigma }\_{OC}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/107b31648e019ce51d2abbaf442664f77a83da82), ![{\displaystyle {\sigma }\_{SC}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/990b21b1b84c4b5ea532d17d043cf81529b866c1), or ![{\displaystyle {\sigma }\_{T}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9bae3d334ea66ad564a5ba7e600c1446426c675e) (total power, SC + OC) and is equal to the cross-sectional area of a metallic sphere (perfect reflector) at the same distance as the target that would return the same echo power.[\[82\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Ostro_Planetary_Radar-82)

Those components of the received echo that return from first-surface reflections (as from a smooth or mirror-like surface) are dominated by the OC component as there is a reversal in polarization upon reflection. If the surface is rough at the wavelength scale or there is significant penetration into the regolith, there will be a significant SC component in the echo caused by multiple scattering.[\[83\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Ostro_Asteroid_Radar-83)

For most objects in the solar system, the OC echo dominates and the most commonly reported radar albedo parameter is the (normalized) OC radar albedo (often shortened to radar albedo):[\[82\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Ostro_Planetary_Radar-82) ![{\displaystyle {\hat {\sigma }}\_{\text{OC}}={\frac {{\sigma }\_{\text{OC}}}{\pi r^{2}}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/44c3e09368cc1980602ce98e637090662fc0465f)

where the denominator is the effective cross-sectional area of the target object with mean radius, ![{\displaystyle r}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0d1ecb613aa2984f0576f70f86650b7c2a132538). A smooth metallic sphere would have ![{\displaystyle {\hat {\sigma }}\_{\text{OC}}=1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/53be889f475cb87fa61cb03ae7366a7841e58d0f).

## History

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Albedo\&action=edit\&section=22 "Edit section: History")]
