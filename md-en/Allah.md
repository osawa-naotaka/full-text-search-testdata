# Allah

Arabic word for God

This article is about the Arabic word for God. For the Islamic conception of God, see [God in Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Islam "God in Islam"). For other uses, see [Allah (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allah_\(disambiguation\) "Allah (disambiguation)").

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fa/Allah3.svg/220px-Allah3.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Allah3.svg)

The word 'Allah' in *[thuluth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thuluth "Thuluth")* [calligraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_calligraphy "Islamic calligraphy")

**Allah** ([/ˈælə, ˈɑːlə, əˈlɑː/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*A(H)L-ə, ə-LAH*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key");[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-1)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-2)[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-3) [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language "Arabic language"): الله, IPA: [\[ɑɫˈɫɑːh\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Arabic "Help:IPA/Arabic") [](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/9/98/Ar-Allah.oga/Ar-Allah.oga.mp3 "Play audio")[ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Ar-Allah.oga "File:Ar-Allah.oga")) is an Arabic word for [God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God "God"), particularly the [God of Abraham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Abrahamic_religions "God in Abrahamic religions"). Outside of the [Middle East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East "Middle East"), it is principally associated with [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Islam "God in Islam") (in which it is also considered the proper name), although the term was used in [pre-Islamic Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Islamic_Arabia "Pre-Islamic Arabia") and continues to be used today by Arabic-speaking adherents of any of the [Abrahamic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic_religions "Abrahamic religions"), including [Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Judaism "God in Judaism") and [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Christianity "God in Christianity").[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-4)[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-5)[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-gardet-allah-6)[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-7) It is thought to be derived by contraction from *[al](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_definite_article "Arabic definite article")-[ilāh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilah "Ilah")* (الاله, lit. 'the god') and is linguistically related to God's names in other [Semitic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitic_languages "Semitic languages"), such as [Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic "Aramaic") (ܐܲܠܵܗܵܐ *ʼAlāhā*) and [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language "Hebrew language") (אֱלוֹהַּ *ʾĔlōah*).[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-8)[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-9)

The word "Allah" now implies the superiority or sole existence of [one God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism "Monotheism"),[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Robin304-10) but among the [pre-Islamic Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_pre-Islamic_Arabia#Role_of_Allah "Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia"), [Allah was a supreme deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creator_deity "Creator deity") and was worshipped alongside lesser deities in a [pantheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheon_\(religion\) "Pantheon (religion)").[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-auto-11) Many Jews, Christians, and [early Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Muslims "Early Muslims") used "Allah" and "al-ilah" interchangeably in [Classical Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Arabic "Classical Arabic"). The word is also frequently, albeit not exclusively, used by [Bábists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%A1bism "Bábism"), [Baháʼís](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%AD_Faith "Baháʼí Faith"), [Mandaeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandaeans "Mandaeans"), [Indonesian Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Indonesia "Christianity in Indonesia"), [Maltese Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Malta "Christianity in Malta"), and [Sephardic Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sephardic_Jews "Sephardic Jews"),[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Britannica-12)[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-EncMMENA-13)[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-14) as well as by the [Gagauz people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gagauz_people "Gagauz people").[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-15)

While it is an Arabic word and has historically been used by Muslims and non-Muslims alike in the [Arab world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_world "Arab world"), the usage of "Allah" by non-Muslims has been controversial in non-Arab parts of the [Muslim world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world "Muslim world"), especially [Malaysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Malaysia "Islam in Malaysia"), where for a time [it became illegal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_of_Kuala_Lumpur_v_Menteri_Dalam_Negeri "Archbishop of Kuala Lumpur v Menteri Dalam Negeri") for non-Muslims to use "Allah" after the country experienced a social and political upheaval in the face of the word being used by [Malaysian Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Malaysia "Christianity in Malaysia") and [Sikhs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism_in_Malaysia "Sikhism in Malaysia") (the ban was overturned in 2021).[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-16)[\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-17)[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-18)[\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-10-point-19)

## Etymology

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Allah\&action=edit\&section=1 "Edit section: Etymology")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/91/Component_letters_in_Allah.svg/240px-Component_letters_in_Allah.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Component_letters_in_Allah.svg)

The Arabic components that make up the word "Allah":

1. [alif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleph#Arabic "Aleph")
2. [hamzat waṣl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamza#Hamzat_al-wa%E1%B9%A3l_\(%D9%B1\) "Hamza") (همزة وصل)
3. [lām](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C4%81m "Lām")
4. lām
5. [shadda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadda "Shadda") (شدة)
6. [alif khunjāriyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagger_alif "Dagger alif") (ألف خنجرية)
7. [hāʾ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C4%81%CA%BE "Hāʾ")

The [etymology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etymology "Etymology") of the word *Allāh* has been discussed extensively by classical Arab philologists.[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-EI2-Ilah-20) Most consider it to be derived from a [contraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synalepha "Synalepha") of the [Arabic definite article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_definite_article "Arabic definite article") *al-* and **ilāh** "[deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deity "Deity"), god" to **al-lāh** meaning "the deity, the God".[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-EI2-Ilah-20) Indeed, there is "the interchangeability of *al-ilāh* and *allāh* in early Arabic poetry even when composed by the Christian [ʿAdī ibn Zayd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adi_ibn_Zayd "Adi ibn Zayd").[\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-21) The majority of scholars accept this hypothesis. A minority hypothesis, seen with more skepticism, is that the term is a loanword from [Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_language "Syriac language") *Alāhā*.[\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-22)[\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-23)

Grammarians of the [Basra school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasan_of_Basra "Hasan of Basra") regarded it as either formed "spontaneously" (*murtajal*) or as the definite form of *lāh* (from the verbal root *lyh* with the meaning of "lofty" or "hidden").[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-EI2-Ilah-20) Other Muslims scholars suggested that the term derives from *wilah* (the object of mystery) since the nature of God is a mystery and perplexing for humans.[\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-24)[\[25\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Baydawi-2016-25): 162 

The use of Allah as the name of a deity appears as early as the [first century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_century "First century"). An inscription using the [Ancient South Arabian script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_South_Arabian_script "Ancient South Arabian script") in [Old Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Arabic "Old Arabic") from [Qaryat al-Fāw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qaryat_al-F%C4%81w "Qaryat al-Fāw") reads, "to [Kahl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kahl_\(god\) "Kahl (god)") and lh and ʿAththar (b-khl w-lh w-ʿṯr)".[\[26\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-26)

[Cognates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognates "Cognates") of the name "Allāh" exist in other [Semitic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitic_languages "Semitic languages"), including [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language "Hebrew language") and [Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic_language "Aramaic language").[\[27\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-autogenerated1-27) The corresponding [Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic "Aramaic") form is *ʼElāh* (אלה), but its emphatic state is **ʼElāhā** (אלהא). It is written as ܐܠܗܐ (*ʼĔlāhā*) in [Biblical Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical_Aramaic "Biblical Aramaic") and ܐܲܠܵܗܵܐ (*ʼAlāhā*) in [Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_language "Syriac language"), both meaning simply "God".[\[28\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-cal-28) The unusual Syriac form is likely an imitation of the Arabic.[\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-29)[\[30\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-30)

Regional variants of the word *Allah* occur in both pagan and Christian pre-Islamic inscriptions.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Robin304-10)[\[31\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-31) According to [Marshall Hodgson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Hodgson "Marshall Hodgson"), it seems that in the pre-Islamic times, some Arab Christians made pilgrimage to the [Kaaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaaba "Kaaba"), a pagan temple at that time, honoring Allah there as God the Creator.[\[32\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-32)

The Syriac word ܐܠܗܐ (*ʼĔlāhā*) can be found in the reports and the lists of names of Christian martyrs in South Arabia,[\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-33)[\[34\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-34) as reported by antique Syriac documents of the names of those martyrs from the era of the [Himyarite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himyarite "Himyarite") and [Aksumite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aksumite "Aksumite") kingdoms[\[35\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-35)

In an inscription of Christian martyrion dated back to 512, references to al-ilah (الاله)[\[36\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Kugener-36) can be found in both Arabic and Aramaic. The inscription starts with the statement "By the Help of al-ilah".[\[37\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-37)[\[38\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-38)

Archaeological excavation quests have led to the discovery of ancient [pre-Islamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Islamic_Arabia "Pre-Islamic Arabia") inscriptions and tombs made by [Arab Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Christians "Arab Christians") in the ruins of a church at [Umm el-Jimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umm_el-Jimal "Umm el-Jimal") in Northern [Jordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan "Jordan"), which initially, according to [Enno Littmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enno_Littmann "Enno Littmann") (1949), contained references to *Allah* as the proper name of God. However, on a second revision by Bellamy et al. (1985 & 1988) the five-verse inscription was retranslated: "(1)This \[inscription] was set up by colleagues of ʿUlayh, (2) son of ʿUbaydah, secretary (3) of the cohort Augusta Secunda (4) Philadelphiana; may he go mad who (5) effaces it."[\[39\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-39)[\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-40)[\[41\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-41)

[Irfan Shahîd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irfan_Shah%C3%AEd "Irfan Shahîd") quoting the 10th-century encyclopedic collection [Kitab al-Aghani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitab_al-Aghani "Kitab al-Aghani") notes that pre-Islamic Arab Christians have been reported to have raised the battle cry "*Ya La Ibad Allah*" (O slaves of Allah) to invoke each other into battle.[\[42\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-42) According to Shahid, on the authority of 10th-century Muslim scholar [Al-Marzubani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Marzubani "Al-Marzubani"), "Allah" was also mentioned in pre-Islamic Christian poems by some [Ghassanid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghassanid "Ghassanid") and [Tanukhid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanukhids "Tanukhids") poets in [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria "Syria") and Northern [Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabia "Arabia").[\[43\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-43)[\[44\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-44)[\[45\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-45)

Different theories have been proposed regarding the role of Allah in pre-Islamic [polytheistic cults](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytheism "Polytheism"). According to the Quran exegete [Ibn Kathir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Kathir "Ibn Kathir"), Arab pagans considered Allah as an unseen God who created and controlled the Universe. Pagans believed worship of humans or animals who had lucky events in their life brought them closer to God. Pre-Islamic Meccans worshiped Allah alongside a host of lesser gods and those whom they called the "daughters of Allah."[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-auto-11) Islam forbade worship of anyone or anything other than God.[\[46\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-46) Some authors have suggested that polytheistic Arabs used the name as a reference to a [creator god](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creator_god "Creator god") or a supreme deity of their [pantheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheon_\(religion\) "Pantheon (religion)").[\[47\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-EoI-47)[\[48\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-48) The term may have been vague in the [Meccan religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca "Mecca").[\[47\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-EoI-47)[\[49\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-GodEoQ-49)

According to one hypothesis, which goes back to [Julius Wellhausen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Wellhausen "Julius Wellhausen"), Allah (the supreme deity of the tribal federation around [Quraysh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quraysh "Quraysh")) was a designation that consecrated the superiority of [Hubal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubal "Hubal") (the supreme deity of Quraysh) over the other gods.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Robin304-10) However, there is also evidence that Allah and Hubal were two distinct deities.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Robin304-10) According to that hypothesis, the [Kaaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaaba "Kaaba") was first consecrated to a supreme deity named Allah and then hosted the pantheon of Quraysh after their conquest of [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca "Mecca"), about a century before the time of [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad "Muhammad").[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Robin304-10) Some inscriptions seem to indicate the use of Allah as a name of a polytheist deity centuries earlier, but nothing precise is known about this use.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Robin304-10) Some scholars have suggested that Allah may have represented a remote creator god who was gradually eclipsed by more particularized local deities.[\[50\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Berkey-50)[\[51\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Peterson2007-51) There is disagreement on whether Allah played a major role in the Meccan religious cult.[\[50\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Berkey-50)[\[52\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Peters107-52) No iconic representation of Allah is known to have existed.[\[52\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Peters107-52)[\[53\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Zeitlin33-53) Muhammad's father's name was [*ʿAbd-Allāh*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_Allah_ibn_Abd_al_Muttalib "Abd Allah ibn Abd al Muttalib") meaning "the slave of Allāh".[\[49\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-GodEoQ-49) The interpretation that Pre-Islamic Arabs once practiced [Abrahamic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic_religions "Abrahamic religions") is supported by some literary evidence, being the prevalence of [Ishmael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ishmael "Ishmael"), whose God was that of [Abraham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham "Abraham"), in pre-Islamic Arab culture.[\[54\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-54)[\[55\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-55)[\[56\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-56)

### Islamic period

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Allah\&action=edit\&section=4 "Edit section: Islamic period")]

Main article: [God in Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Islam "God in Islam")

See also: [Names of God in Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_God_in_Islam "Names of God in Islam")

In contrast with pre-Islamic Arabian [polytheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytheism "Polytheism"), as stated by [Gerhard Böwering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerhard_B%C3%B6wering "Gerhard Böwering"), God in Islam does not have associates and companions, nor is there any kinship between God and [jinn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn "Jinn").[\[57\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-EoQ-57) Pre-Islamic pagan Arabs believed in a blind, powerful, inexorable and insensible fate over which man had no control. This was replaced with the Islamic notion of a powerful but provident and merciful God.[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Britannica-12) According to [Francis Edward Peters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Edward_Peters "Francis Edward Peters"), "The [Qur'ān](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran "Quran") insists, Muslims believe, and historians affirm that [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad "Muhammad") and his followers worship the same God as the Jews ([29:46](https://quran.com/29?startingVerse=46)). The Qur'an's Allah is the same Creator God who covenanted with [Abraham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham "Abraham")". Peters states that the Qur'an portrays Allah as both more powerful and more remote than [Yahweh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yahweh "Yahweh"), and as a universal deity, unlike Yahweh who closely follows [Israelites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel "Israel").[\[58\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Peters1-58)

Since the first centuries of Islam, Arabic-speaking commentators of Jewish, Christian, and Islamic faith used the term *Allah* as a generic term for the supreme being.[\[59\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-auto1-59) [Saadia Gaon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saadia_Gaon "Saadia Gaon") used the term *Allah* interchangeably with the term *[ʾĔlōhīm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elohim "Elohim")*.[\[59\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-auto1-59) [Theodore Abu Qurrah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Abu_Qurrah "Theodore Abu Qurrah") translates *theos* as *Allah* in his Bible, as in John 1:1 "the Word was with Allah".[\[59\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-auto1-59) Muslim commentators likewise used the term Allah for the Biblical concept of God. [Ibn Qutayba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Qutayba "Ibn Qutayba") writes "You cannot serve both Allah and Mammon.".[\[59\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-auto1-59) However, Muslim translators of the Middle East, North Africa, and Asia rarely translated the [Tetragrammaton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetragrammaton "Tetragrammaton"), referring to the supreme being in Israelite tradition, as *Allah*. Instead, most commentators either translated [Yahweh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yahweh "Yahweh") as either *yahwah* or *rabb*, the latter corresponding to the Jewish custom to refer to Yahweh as *Adonai*.[\[59\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-auto1-59)

Most Qur'an [commentators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tafsir "Tafsir"), including [al-Tabari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Tabari "Al-Tabari") (d. 923), [al-Zamakhshari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Zamakhshari "Al-Zamakhshari") (d. 1143/44), and [al-Razi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fakhr_al-Din_al-Razi "Fakhr al-Din al-Razi") (d. 1209), regard *Allah* to be a proper name.[\[60\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-auto2-60) While other names of [God in Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Islam "God in Islam") denote attributes or adjectives, the term *Allah* specifically refers to his essence as his real name (*ism'alam li-dhatih*).[\[60\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-auto2-60) The other names are known as the [99 Names of Allah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/99_Names_of_God "99 Names of God") (**al-asmā' al-ḥusná** lit. meaning: 'the best names' or 'the most beautiful names') and considered attributes, each of which evoke a distinct characteristic of Allah.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-EncMMENA-13)[\[61\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Ben-61) All these names refer to Allah, the supreme and all-comprehensive divine name.[\[62\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Tao-Islam-62) Among the 99 names of God, the most famous and most frequent of these names are "the Merciful" (*[ar-Raḥmān](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rahman_\(name\) "Rahman (name)")*) and "the Compassionate" (**ar-Raḥīm**),[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-EncMMENA-13)[\[61\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Ben-61) including the forementioned above *al-Aḥad* ("the One, the Indivisible") and *al-Wāḥid* ("the Unique, the Single").

According to Islamic belief, Allah is the most common word to represent God,[\[57\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-EoQ-57) and humble submission to his will, divine ordinances and commandments is the pivot of the Muslim faith.[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Britannica-12) "He is the only God, creator of the universe, and the judge of humankind."[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Britannica-12)[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-EncMMENA-13) "He is unique (**wāḥid**) and inherently one (**aḥad**), all-merciful and omnipotent."[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Britannica-12) No human eyes can see Allah till the Day Of Judgement.[\[63\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-63) The Qur'an declares "the reality of Allah, His inaccessible mystery, His various names, and His actions on behalf of His creatures."[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Britannica-12) Allah does not depend on anything.[\[64\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-64) Allah is not considered a part of the Christian Trinity.[\[65\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-65) God has no parents and no children.[\[66\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-66)

The concept correlates to the [Tawhid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tawhid "Tawhid"), where chapter 112 of the [Qur'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran "Quran") ([*Al-'Ikhlās*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ikhlas "Al-Ikhlas"), The Sincerity) reads:[\[67\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-67)

> قُلْ هُوَ ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ [۝](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%DB%9D "۝") ٱللَّهُ ٱلصَّمَدُ۝ لَمْ يَلِدْ وَلَمْ يُولَدْ ۝ وَلَمْ يَكُن لَّهُۥ كُفُوًا أَحَدٌۢ ۝١
>
> - - [۝](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%DB%9D "۝") Say, God is one God;
>   - ۝ the eternal God:
>   - ۝ He begetteth not, neither is He begotten:
>   - ۝ and there is not any one like unto Him.[\[68\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-68)

In a [Sufi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufi "Sufi") practice known as *dhikr Allāh* ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic "Arabic"): ذِكر الله, lit. "Remembrance of God"), the Sufi repeats and contemplates the name *Allah* or other associated divine names to Him while controlling his or her breath.[\[69\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-69)

The Islamic tradition to use *Allah* as the personal name of God became disputed in contemporary scholarship, including the question, whether or not the word *Allah* should be translated as *God*.[\[70\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-70) [Umar Faruq Abd-Allah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umar_Faruq_Abd-Allah "Umar Faruq Abd-Allah") urged English-speaking Muslims to use God instead of Allah for the sake of finding "extensive middle ground we share with other Abrahamic and universal traditions".[\[60\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-auto2-60)

Most Muslims use the Arabic phrase *[*in shā'a llāh*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insha%27Allah "Insha'Allah")* (meaning 'if God wills') untranslated after references to future events.[\[71\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-71) Muslim discursive piety encourages beginning things with the invocation of *[*bi-smi llāh*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basmala "Basmala")* (meaning 'In the name of God').[\[72\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-72) There are certain other phrases in praise of God that are favored by Muslims and left untranslated, including "[*Subḥāna llāh*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subhan%27allah "Subhan'allah")" (Glory be to God), "[*al-ḥamdu li-llāh*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alhamdulillah "Alhamdulillah")" (Praise be to God), "[*lā ilāha illā llāh*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shahada "Shahada")" (There is no deity but God) or sometimes "*lā ilāha illā inta/ huwa*" (There is no deity but *You*/ *Him*) and "[*Allāhu Akbar*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takbir "Takbir")" (God is the Most Great) as a devotional exercise of remembering God ([dhikr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhikr "Dhikr")).[\[73\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-73)

#### Christianity

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Allah\&action=edit\&section=7 "Edit section: Christianity")]

The [Christian Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Christians "Arab Christians") of today have no other word for "God" than "Allah".[\[74\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Cambridge-74) Similarly, the [Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic "Aramaic") word for "God" in the language of [Assyrian Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_Christians "Assyrian Christians") is *ʼĔlāhā*, or **Alaha**. (Even the Arabic-descended [Maltese language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maltese_language "Maltese language") of [Malta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malta "Malta"), whose population is almost entirely [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church "Catholic Church"), uses *Alla* for "God".)

[Arab Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Christians "Arab Christians") have used two forms of invocations that were [affixed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affix "Affix") to the beginning of their written works. They adopted the Muslim **bismillāh**, and also created their own [Trinitized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity "Trinity") **bismillāh** as early as the 8th century.[\[75\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Thomas-75) The Muslim **bismillāh** reads: "In the name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful." The Trinitized **bismillāh** reads: "In the name of the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, One God." The [Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_language "Syriac language"), [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") and [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language") invocations do not have the words "One God" at the end. This addition was made to emphasize the [monotheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism "Monotheism") aspect of Trinitarian belief and also to make it more palatable to Muslims.[\[75\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Thomas-75)

## Pronunciation

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Allah\&action=edit\&section=8 "Edit section: Pronunciation")]

The word *Allāh* is generally pronounced \[ɑɫˈɫɑː(h)], exhibiting a heavy *lām*, \[ɫ], a [velarized alveolar lateral approximant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velarized_alveolar_lateral_approximant "Velarized alveolar lateral approximant"), a marginal phoneme in [Modern Standard Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_phonology "Arabic phonology"). Since the initial alef has no [hamza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamza "Hamza"), the initial \[a] is elided when a preceding word ends in a vowel. If the preceding vowel is /i/, the *lām* is light, \[l], as in, for instance, the [Basmala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basmala "Basmala").[\[76\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-ARABIC_for_NERDS-76)

## As a loanword

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Allah\&action=edit\&section=9 "Edit section: As a loanword")]

### English and other European languages

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Allah\&action=edit\&section=10 "Edit section: English and other European languages")]

The history of the name *Allāh* in English was probably influenced by the study of [comparative religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion "Comparative religion") in the 19th century; for example, [Thomas Carlyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Carlyle "Thomas Carlyle") (1840) sometimes used the term Allah but without any implication that Allah was anything different from God. However, in his biography of Muḥammad (1934), [Tor Andræ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tor_Andr%C3%A6 "Tor Andræ") always used the term *Allah*, though he allows that this "conception of God" seems to imply that it is different from that of the Jewish and Christian theologies.[\[77\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Watt45-77)

Languages which may not commonly use the term *Allah* to denote God may still contain popular expressions which use the word. For example, because of the centuries long [Muslim presence in the Iberian Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Andalus "Al-Andalus"), the word *ojalá* in the Spanish language and *oxalá* in the [Portuguese language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_language "Portuguese language") exist today, borrowed from [Andalusi Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andalusi_Arabic "Andalusi Arabic") *law šá lláh*[\[78\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-DRAE-78) similar to *[inshalla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inshalla "Inshalla")* ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language "Arabic language"): إِنْ شَاءَ ٱللَّٰهُ). This phrase literally means 'if God wills'.[\[79\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-79) The German poet [Mahlmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siegfried_August_Mahlmann "Siegfried August Mahlmann") used the form "Allah" as the title of a poem about the ultimate deity, though it is unclear how much Islamic thought he intended to convey.

Some Muslims leave the name "Allāh" untranslated in English, rather than using the English translation "God".[\[80\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-80) The word has also been applied to certain living human beings as [personifications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropomorphism "Anthropomorphism") of the term and concept.[\[81\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-81)[\[82\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-82)

### Malaysian and Indonesian language

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Allah\&action=edit\&section=11 "Edit section: Malaysian and Indonesian language")]

Main articles: [Titular Roman Catholic Archbishop of Kuala Lumpur v. Menteri Dalam Negeri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titular_Roman_Catholic_Archbishop_of_Kuala_Lumpur_v._Menteri_Dalam_Negeri "Titular Roman Catholic Archbishop of Kuala Lumpur v. [![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2d/Vocabularium%2C_ofte_Woordenboek%2C_in_%27t_Duytsch_en_Maleys_%28IA_vocabulariumoft00dancgoog%29.djvu/page77-220px-Vocabularium%2C_ofte_Woordenboek%2C_in_%27t_Duytsch_en_Maleys_%28IA_vocabulariumoft00dancgoog%29.djvu.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Vocabularium,_ofte_Woordenboek,_in_%27t_Duytsch_en_Maleys_\(IA_vocabulariumoft00dancgoog\).djvu%3Fpage%3D77 "File:Vocabularium, ofte Woordenboek, in 't Duytsch en Maleys (IA vocabulariumoft00dancgoog).djvu?page=77")

The first dictionary of Dutch-Malay by [A.C. Ruyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Cornelius_Ruyl "Albert Cornelius Ruyl"), Justus Heurnius, and Caspar Wiltens in 1650 recorded *Allah* as the translation of the Dutch word *Godt*.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/dc/GKKA_Banjarmasin.jpg/220px-GKKA_Banjarmasin.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:GKKA_Banjarmasin.jpg)

*[Gereja Kebangunan Kalam Allah](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Gereja_Kebangunan_Kalam_Allah\&action=edit\&redlink=1 "Gereja Kebangunan Kalam Allah (page does not exist)") \[[id](https://id.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gereja_Kebangunan_Kalam_Allah "id:Gereja Kebangunan Kalam Allah")]* (Word of God Revival Church) in [Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia "Indonesia"). *Allah* is the word for "God" in the [Indonesian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesian_language "Indonesian language") - even in *Alkitab* (Christian [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible "Bible"), from الكتاب, *al-kitāb* = the book) translations, while *[Tuhan](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Tuhan "wikt:Tuhan")* is the word for "Lord".

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e2/Seremban-Annunciation-feast-3808.jpg/220px-Seremban-Annunciation-feast-3808.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Seremban-Annunciation-feast-3808.jpg)

[Christians in Malaysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Malaysia "Christianity in Malaysia") also use the word *Allah* for "God".

Christians in Malaysia and Indonesia use *Allah* to refer to God in the [Malaysian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysian_language "Malaysian language") and [Indonesian languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesian_language "Indonesian language") (both of them standardized forms of the [Malay language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malay_language "Malay language")). Mainstream Bible translations in the language use *Allah* as the translation of Hebrew *[Elohim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elohim "Elohim")* (translated in English Bibles as "God").[\[83\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-83) This goes back to early translation work by [Francis Xavier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Xavier "Francis Xavier") in the 16th century.[\[84\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-84)[\[85\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-85) The first dictionary of Dutch-Malay by Albert Cornelius Ruyl, Justus Heurnius, and Caspar Wiltens in 1650 (revised edition from 1623 edition and 1631 Latin edition) recorded *Allah*" as the translation of the Dutch word *Godt*.[\[86\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-86) Ruyl also translated the [Gospel of Matthew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_Matthew "Gospel of Matthew") in 1612 into the Malay language (an early Bible translation into a non-European language,[\[87\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-87) made a year after the publication of the [King James Version](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_James_Version "King James Version")[\[88\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-88)[\[89\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-89)), which was printed in the Netherlands in 1629. Then he translated the [Gospel of Mark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_Mark "Gospel of Mark"), published in 1638.[\[90\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-90)[\[91\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-91)

The [government of Malaysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Malaysia "Government of Malaysia") in 2007 outlawed usage of the term *Allah* in any other but Muslim contexts, but the [Malayan High Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Court_of_Malaya "High Court of Malaya") in 2009 [revoked the law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_of_Kuala_Lumpur_v_Menteri_Dalam_Negeri "Archbishop of Kuala Lumpur v Menteri Dalam Negeri"), ruling it unconstitutional. While *Allah* had been used for the Christian God in Malay for more than four centuries, the contemporary controversy was triggered by usage of *Allah* by the Roman Catholic newspaper [*The Herald*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Herald_\(Malaysian_Catholic_Weekly\) "The Herald (Malaysian Catholic Weekly)"). The government appealed the court ruling, and the High Court suspended implementation of its verdict until the hearing of the appeal. In October 2013 the court ruled in favor of the government's ban.[\[92\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-92) In early 2014 the Malaysian government confiscated more than 300 bibles for using the word to refer to the Christian God in Peninsular Malaysia.[\[93\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-93) However, the use of *Allah* is not prohibited in the two Malaysian states of [Sabah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabah "Sabah") and [Sarawak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarawak "Sarawak").[\[94\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-settle-94)[\[95\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-95) The main reason it is not prohibited in these two states is that usage has been long-established and local Alkitab ([Bibles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibles "Bibles")) have been widely distributed freely in East Malaysia without restrictions for years.[\[94\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-settle-94) Both states also do not have similar Islamic state laws as those in West Malaysia.[\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-10-point-19)

In reaction to some media criticism, the Malaysian government has introduced a "10-point solution" to avoid confusion and misleading information.[\[96\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-96)[\[97\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-97) The 10-point solution is in line with the spirit of the [18](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/18-point_agreement "18-point agreement")- and [20-point agreements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/20-point_agreement "20-point agreement") of Sarawak and Sabah.[\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-10-point-19)

## Typography

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Allah\&action=edit\&section=13 "Edit section: Typography")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/92/Allah_name_in_different_languages.png/280px-Allah_name_in_different_languages.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Allah_name_in_different_languages.png)

The word *Allah* written in different [writing systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writing_system "Writing system")

The word **Allāh** is always written without an [*alif*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleph "Aleph") to spell the **ā** vowel. This is because the spelling was settled before Arabic spelling started habitually using **alif** to spell **ā**. However, in vocalized spelling, a [small diacritic **alif**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagger_alif "Dagger alif") is added on top of the *[*shaddah*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadda "Shadda")* to indicate the pronunciation.

In the pre-Islamic [Zabad inscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zabad_inscription "Zabad inscription"),[\[98\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-98) God is referred to by the term الاله, that is, alif-lam-alif-lam-ha.[\[36\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Kugener-36) This presumably indicates **Al-'ilāh** means "the god", without **alif** for *ā*.

Many Arabic type fonts feature special [ligatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typographic_ligature "Typographic ligature") for Allah.[\[99\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Typ1-99)

Since [Arabic script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_script "Arabic script") is used to write other texts rather than Koran only, rendering **lām** + **lām** + **hā'** as the previous ligature is considered faulty which is the case with most common Arabic typefaces.

> This simplified style is often preferred for clarity, especially in non-Arabic languages, but may not be considered appropriate in situations where a more elaborate style of calligraphy is preferred.
>
> - —[SIL International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SIL_International "SIL International")[\[100\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-100)

### Unicode

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Allah\&action=edit\&section=14 "Edit section: Unicode")]

[Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode "Unicode") has a code point reserved for **Allāh**, U+FDF2 ﷲ ARABIC LIGATURE ALLAH ISOLATED FORM, in the [Arabic Presentation Forms-A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_Presentation_Forms-A "Arabic Presentation Forms-A") block, which exists solely for "compatibility with some older, legacy character sets that encoded presentation forms directly";[\[101\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-101)[\[102\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Uni-102) this is discouraged for new text. Instead, the word **Allāh** should be represented by its individual Arabic letters, while modern font technologies will render the desired ligature.

The calligraphic variant of the word used as the [emblem of Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emblem_of_Iran "Emblem of Iran") is encoded in Unicode, in the [Miscellaneous Symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miscellaneous_Symbols "Miscellaneous Symbols") range, at code point U+262B (☫). The [flags that include the word](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#National_flags_with_%22Allah%22_written_on_them) are also present in the [regional indicator symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_indicator_symbol "Regional indicator symbol") of Unicode: 🇮🇶, 🇸🇦, 🇦🇫, 🇮🇷, 🇺🇿.
