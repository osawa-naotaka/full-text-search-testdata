# Analysis of variance

Collection of statistical models

**Analysis of variance** (**ANOVA**) is a collection of [statistical models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_model "Statistical model") and their associated estimation procedures (such as the "variation" among and between groups) used to analyze the differences between groups. It uses F-test by comparing variance between groups and taking noise, or assumed normal distribution of group, into consideration by dividing by variance between elements in a group. ANOVA was developed by the [statistician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistician "Statistician") [Ronald Fisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Fisher "Ronald Fisher"). ANOVA is based on the [law of total variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_total_variance "Law of total variance"), where the observed [variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variance "Variance") in a particular variable is partitioned into components attributable to different sources of variation. In its simplest form, ANOVA provides a [statistical test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_test "Statistical test") of whether two or more population [means](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean "Mean") are equal, and therefore generalizes the [*t*-test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Student%27s_t-test#Independent_two-sample_t-test "Student's t-test") beyond two means. In other words, the ANOVA is used to test the difference between two or more means.

## History

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=1 "Edit section: History")]

While the analysis of variance reached fruition in the 20th century, antecedents extend centuries into the past according to [Stigler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Stigler "Stephen Stigler").[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-1) These include hypothesis testing, the partitioning of sums of squares, experimental techniques and the additive model. [Laplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Simon_Laplace "Pierre-Simon Laplace") was performing hypothesis testing in the 1770s.[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-2) Around 1800, Laplace and [Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Gauss "Carl Friedrich Gauss") developed the least-squares method for combining observations, which improved upon methods then used in astronomy and [geodesy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesy "Geodesy"). It also initiated much study of the contributions to sums of squares. Laplace knew how to estimate a variance from a residual (rather than a total) sum of squares.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-3) By 1827, Laplace was using [least squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_squares "Least squares") methods to address ANOVA problems regarding measurements of atmospheric tides.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-4) Before 1800, astronomers had isolated observational errors resulting from reaction times (the "[personal equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_equation "Personal equation")") and had developed methods of reducing the errors.[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-5) The experimental methods used in the study of the personal equation were later accepted by the emerging field of psychology [\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-6) which developed strong (full factorial) experimental methods to which randomization and blinding were soon added.[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-7) An eloquent non-mathematical explanation of the additive effects model was available in 1885.[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-8)

[Ronald Fisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Fisher "Ronald Fisher") introduced the term [variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variance "Variance") and proposed its formal analysis in a 1918 article on theoretical population genetics, *[The Correlation Between Relatives on the Supposition of Mendelian Inheritance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Correlation_between_Relatives_on_the_Supposition_of_Mendelian_Inheritance "The Correlation between Relatives on the Supposition of Mendelian Inheritance")*.[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-9) His first application of the analysis of variance to data analysis was published in 1921, *Studies in Crop Variation I*.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-10) This divided the variation of a time series into components representing annual causes and slow deterioration. Fisher's next piece, *Studies in Crop Variation II*, written with [Winifred Mackenzie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winifred_Mackenzie "Winifred Mackenzie") and published in 1923, studied the variation in yield across plots sown with different varieties and subjected to different fertiliser treatments.[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-11) Analysis of variance became widely known after being included in Fisher's 1925 book *[Statistical Methods for Research Workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_Methods_for_Research_Workers "Statistical Methods for Research Workers")*.

Randomization models were developed by several researchers. The first was published in Polish by [Jerzy Neyman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerzy_Neyman "Jerzy Neyman") in 1923.[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-12)

Fair fit: Pet vs Working breed and less athletic vs more athletic

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/af/ANOVA_very_good_fit.jpg/220px-ANOVA_very_good_fit.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:ANOVA_very_good_fit.jpg)

Very good fit: Weight by breed

The analysis of variance can be used to describe otherwise complex relations among variables. A dog show provides an example. A dog show is not a random sampling of the breed: it is typically limited to dogs that are adult, pure-bred, and exemplary. A histogram of dog weights from a show is likely to be rather complicated, like the yellow-orange distribution shown in the illustrations. Suppose we wanted to predict the weight of a dog based on a certain set of characteristics of each dog. One way to do that is to *explain* the distribution of weights by dividing the dog population into groups based on those characteristics. A successful grouping will split dogs such that (a) each group has a low variance of dog weights (meaning the group is relatively homogeneous) and (b) the mean of each group is distinct (if two groups have the same mean, then it isn't reasonable to conclude that the groups are, in fact, separate in any meaningful way).

In the illustrations to the right, groups are identified as *X*1, *X*2, etc. In the first illustration, the dogs are divided according to the product (interaction) of two binary groupings: young vs old, and short-haired vs long-haired (e.g., group 1 is young, short-haired dogs, group 2 is young, long-haired dogs, etc.). Since the distributions of dog weight within each of the groups (shown in blue) has a relatively large variance, and since the means are very similar across groups, grouping dogs by these characteristics does not produce an effective way to explain the variation in dog weights: knowing which group a dog is in doesn't allow us to predict its weight much better than simply knowing the dog is in a dog show. Thus, this grouping fails to explain the variation in the overall distribution (yellow-orange).

An attempt to explain the weight distribution by grouping dogs as *pet vs working breed* and *less athletic vs more athletic* would probably be somewhat more successful (fair fit). The heaviest show dogs are likely to be big, strong, working breeds, while breeds kept as pets tend to be smaller and thus lighter. As shown by the second illustration, the distributions have variances that are considerably smaller than in the first case, and the means are more distinguishable. However, the significant overlap of distributions, for example, means that we cannot distinguish *X*1 and *X*2 reliably. Grouping dogs according to a coin flip might produce distributions that look similar.

An attempt to explain weight by breed is likely to produce a very good fit. All Chihuahuas are light and all St Bernards are heavy. The difference in weights between Setters and Pointers does not justify separate breeds. The analysis of variance provides the formal tools to justify these intuitive judgments. A common use of the method is the analysis of experimental data or the development of models. The method has some advantages over correlation: not all of the data must be numeric and one result of the method is a judgment in the confidence in an explanatory relationship.

## Classes of models

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=3 "Edit section: Classes of models")]

There are three classes of models used in the analysis of variance, and these are outlined here.

### Fixed-effects models

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=4 "Edit section: Fixed-effects models")]

Main article: [Fixed effects model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_effects_model "Fixed effects model")

The fixed-effects model (class I) of analysis of variance applies to situations in which the experimenter applies one or more treatments to the subjects of the experiment to see whether the [response variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Response_variable "Response variable") values change. This allows the experimenter to estimate the ranges of response variable values that the treatment would generate in the population as a whole.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/66/Fixed_effects_vs_Random_effects.pdf/page1-291px-Fixed_effects_vs_Random_effects.pdf.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Fixed_effects_vs_Random_effects.pdf)

Fixed effects vs Random effects

### Random-effects models

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=5 "Edit section: Random-effects models")]

Main article: [Random effects model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_effects_model "Random effects model")

Random-effects model (class II) is used when the treatments are not fixed. This occurs when the various factor levels are sampled from a larger population. Because the levels themselves are [random variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_variable "Random variable"), some assumptions and the method of contrasting the treatments (a multi-variable generalization of simple differences) differ from the fixed-effects model.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-13)

### Mixed-effects models

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=6 "Edit section: Mixed-effects models")]

Main article: [Mixed model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_model "Mixed model")

A mixed-effects model (class III) contains experimental factors of both fixed and random-effects types, with appropriately different interpretations and analysis for the two types.

### Example

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=7 "Edit section: Example")]

Teaching experiments could be performed by a college or university department to find a good introductory textbook, with each text considered a treatment. The fixed-effects model would compare a list of candidate texts. The random-effects model would determine whether important differences exist among a list of randomly selected texts. The mixed-effects model would compare the (fixed) incumbent texts to randomly selected alternatives.

Defining fixed and random effects has proven elusive, with multiple competing definitions.[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-14)

## Assumptions

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=8 "Edit section: Assumptions")]

The analysis of variance has been studied from several approaches, the most common of which uses a [linear model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_model "Linear model") that relates the response to the treatments and blocks. Note that the model is linear in parameters but may be nonlinear across factor levels. Interpretation is easy when data is balanced across factors but much deeper understanding is needed for unbalanced data.

### Textbook analysis using a normal distribution

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=9 "Edit section: Textbook analysis using a normal distribution")]

The analysis of variance can be presented in terms of a [linear model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_model "Linear model"), which makes the following assumptions about the [probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_distribution "Probability distribution") of the responses:[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-15)[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-16)[\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-17)[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-18)

- [Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence "Statistical independence") of observations – this is an assumption of the model that simplifies the statistical analysis.
- [Normality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_distribution "Normal distribution") – the distributions of the [residuals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Residual_\(statistics\) "Residual (statistics)") are [normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_distribution "Normal distribution").
- Equality (or "homogeneity") of variances, called [homoscedasticity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homoscedasticity "Homoscedasticity")—the variance of data in groups should be the same.

The separate assumptions of the textbook model imply that the [errors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errors_and_residuals_in_statistics "Errors and residuals in statistics") are independently, identically, and normally distributed for fixed effects models, that is, that the errors (![{\displaystyle \varepsilon }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a30c89172e5b88edbd45d3e2772c7f5e562e5173)) are independent and ![{\displaystyle \varepsilon \thicksim N(0,\sigma ^{2}).}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4951cc2b2c8aa28954cdaaaa40d5935e8cd81aea)

### Randomization-based analysis

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=10 "Edit section: Randomization-based analysis")]

See also: [Random assignment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_assignment "Random assignment") and [Randomization test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomization_test "Randomization test")

In a [randomized controlled experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomized_controlled_trial "Randomized controlled trial"), the treatments are randomly assigned to experimental units, following the experimental protocol. This randomization is objective and declared before the experiment is carried out. The objective random-assignment is used to test the significance of the [null hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_hypothesis "Null hypothesis"), following the ideas of [C. S. Peirce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Sanders_Peirce "Charles Sanders Peirce") and [Ronald Fisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Fisher "Ronald Fisher"). This design-based analysis was discussed and developed by [Francis J. Anscombe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_J._Anscombe "Francis J. Anscombe") at [Rothamsted Experimental Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rothamsted_Experimental_Station "Rothamsted Experimental Station") and by [Oscar Kempthorne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Kempthorne "Oscar Kempthorne") at [Iowa State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iowa_State_University "Iowa State University").[\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-19) Kempthorne and his students make an assumption of *unit treatment additivity*, which is discussed in the books of Kempthorne and [David R. Cox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_R._Cox "David R. Cox").[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-20)[\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-21)

#### Unit-treatment additivity

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=11 "Edit section: Unit-treatment additivity")]

In its simplest form, the assumption of unit-treatment additivity[\[nb 1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-22) states that the observed response ![{\displaystyle y\_{i,j}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/94aa421a472049823de2f3d146c48ac8bf39c322) from experimental unit ![{\displaystyle i}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/add78d8608ad86e54951b8c8bd6c8d8416533d20) when receiving treatment ![{\displaystyle j}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2f461e54f5c093e92a55547b9764291390f0b5d0) can be written as the sum of the unit's response ![{\displaystyle y\_{i}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/67d30d30b6c2dbe4d6f150d699de040937ecc95f) and the treatment-effect ![{\displaystyle t\_{j}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/53942a7888623b7eff84a0e43183e046c9f66d65), that is [\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-23)[\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Cox-24)[\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-25) ![{\displaystyle y\_{i,j}=y\_{i}+t\_{j}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/501bb67fab131ec0b4057727f77976e6ab987d15) The assumption of unit-treatment additivity implies that, for every treatment ![{\displaystyle j}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2f461e54f5c093e92a55547b9764291390f0b5d0), the ![{\displaystyle j}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2f461e54f5c093e92a55547b9764291390f0b5d0)th treatment has exactly the same effect ![{\displaystyle t\_{j}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/53942a7888623b7eff84a0e43183e046c9f66d65) on every experiment unit.

The assumption of unit treatment additivity usually cannot be directly [falsified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falsifiability "Falsifiability"), according to Cox and Kempthorne. However, many *consequences* of treatment-unit additivity can be falsified. For a randomized experiment, the assumption of unit-treatment additivity *implies* that the variance is constant for all treatments. Therefore, by [contraposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contraposition "Contraposition"), a necessary condition for unit-treatment additivity is that the variance is constant.

The use of unit treatment additivity and randomization is similar to the design-based inference that is standard in finite-population [survey sampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survey_sampling "Survey sampling").

#### Derived linear model

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=12 "Edit section: Derived linear model")]

Kempthorne uses the randomization-distribution and the assumption of *unit treatment additivity* to produce a *derived linear model*, very similar to the textbook model discussed previously.[\[25\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-26) The test statistics of this derived linear model are closely approximated by the test statistics of an appropriate normal linear model, according to approximation theorems and simulation studies.[\[26\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-HinkelmannKempthorne-27) However, there are differences. For example, the randomization-based analysis results in a small but (strictly) negative correlation between the observations.[\[27\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-28)[\[28\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-29) In the randomization-based analysis, there is *no assumption* of a *normal* distribution and certainly *no assumption* of *independence*. On the contrary, *the observations are dependent*!

The randomization-based analysis has the disadvantage that its exposition involves tedious algebra and extensive time. Since the randomization-based analysis is complicated and is closely approximated by the approach using a normal linear model, most teachers emphasize the normal linear model approach. Few statisticians object to model-based analysis of balanced randomized experiments.

#### Statistical models for observational data

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=13 "Edit section: Statistical models for observational data")]

However, when applied to data from non-randomized experiments or [observational studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observational_study "Observational study"), model-based analysis lacks the warrant of randomization.[\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-30) For observational data, the derivation of confidence intervals must use *subjective* models, as emphasized by [Ronald Fisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Fisher "Ronald Fisher") and his followers. In practice, the estimates of treatment-effects from observational studies generally are often inconsistent. In practice, "statistical models" and observational data are useful for suggesting hypotheses that should be treated very cautiously by the public.[\[30\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-31)

### Summary of assumptions

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=14 "Edit section: Summary of assumptions")]

See also: [Shapiro–Wilk test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shapiro%E2%80%93Wilk_test "Shapiro–Wilk test"), [Bartlett's test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartlett%27s_test "Bartlett's test"), and [Levene's test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levene%27s_test "Levene's test")

The normal-model based ANOVA analysis assumes the independence, normality, and homogeneity of variances of the residuals. The randomization-based analysis assumes only the homogeneity of the variances of the residuals (as a consequence of unit-treatment additivity) and uses the randomization procedure of the experiment. Both these analyses require [homoscedasticity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homoscedasticity "Homoscedasticity"), as an assumption for the normal-model analysis and as a consequence of randomization and additivity for the randomization-based analysis.

However, studies of processes that change variances rather than means (called dispersion effects) have been successfully conducted using ANOVA.[\[31\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-32) There are *no* necessary assumptions for ANOVA in its full generality, but the *F*-test used for ANOVA hypothesis testing has assumptions and practical limitations which are of continuing interest.

Problems which do not satisfy the assumptions of ANOVA can often be transformed to satisfy the assumptions. The property of unit-treatment additivity is not invariant under a "change of scale", so statisticians often use transformations to achieve unit-treatment additivity. If the response variable is expected to follow a parametric family of probability distributions, then the statistician may specify (in the protocol for the experiment or observational study) that the responses be transformed to stabilize the variance.[\[32\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-33) Also, a statistician may specify that logarithmic transforms be applied to the responses which are believed to follow a multiplicative model.[\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Cox-24)[\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-34) According to Cauchy's [functional equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_equation "Functional equation") theorem, the [logarithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithm "Logarithm") is the only continuous transformation that transforms real multiplication to addition.\[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

## Characteristics

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=15 "Edit section: Characteristics")]

ANOVA is used in the analysis of comparative experiments, those in which only the difference in outcomes is of interest. The statistical significance of the experiment is determined by a ratio of two variances. This ratio is independent of several possible alterations to the experimental observations: Adding a constant to all observations does not alter significance. Multiplying all observations by a constant does not alter significance. So ANOVA statistical significance result is independent of constant bias and scaling errors as well as the units used in expressing observations. In the era of mechanical calculation it was common to subtract a constant from all observations (when equivalent to dropping leading digits) to simplify data entry.[\[34\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-35)[\[35\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-36) This is an example of data [coding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coding_\(social_sciences\) "Coding (social sciences)").

## Algorithm

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=16 "Edit section: Algorithm")]

The calculations of ANOVA can be characterized as computing a number of means and variances, dividing two variances and comparing the ratio to a handbook value to determine statistical significance. Calculating a treatment effect is then trivial: "the effect of any treatment is estimated by taking the difference between the mean of the observations which receive the treatment and the general mean".[\[36\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-37)

### Partitioning of the sum of squares

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=17 "Edit section: Partitioning of the sum of squares")]

Main article: [Partition of sums of squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_sums_of_squares "Partition of sums of squares")

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/da/Example_ANOVA_Table.png/324px-Example_ANOVA_Table.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Example_ANOVA_Table.png)

One-factor ANOVA table showing example output data

See also: [Lack-of-fit sum of squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lack-of-fit_sum_of_squares "Lack-of-fit sum of squares")

ANOVA uses traditional standardized terminology. The definitional equation of sample variance is ![{\textstyle s^{2}={\frac {1}{n-1}}\sum \_{i}(y\_{i}-{\bar {y}})^{2}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c789c2aefb4b8db8c62ae49ff36d220f5880a15e), where the divisor is called the degrees of freedom (DF), the summation is called the sum of squares (SS), the result is called the mean square (MS) and the squared terms are deviations from the sample mean. ANOVA estimates 3 sample variances: a total variance based on all the observation deviations from the grand mean, an error variance based on all the observation deviations from their appropriate treatment means, and a treatment variance. The treatment variance is based on the deviations of treatment means from the grand mean, the result being multiplied by the number of observations in each treatment to account for the difference between the variance of observations and the variance of means.

The fundamental technique is a partitioning of the total [sum of squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sum_of_squares_\(statistics\) "Sum of squares (statistics)") *SS* into components related to the effects used in the model. For example, the model for a simplified ANOVA with one type of treatment at different levels.

![{\displaystyle SS\_{\text{Total}}=SS\_{\text{Error}}+SS\_{\text{Treatments}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8c14dce05d2630d72f059194ebb5ee4313076460)

The number of [degrees of freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degrees_of_freedom_\(statistics\) "Degrees of freedom (statistics)") *DF* can be partitioned in a similar way: one of these components (that for error) specifies a [chi-squared distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chi-squared_distribution "Chi-squared distribution") which describes the associated sum of squares, while the same is true for "treatments" if there is no treatment effect.

![{\displaystyle DF\_{\text{Total}}=DF\_{\text{Error}}+DF\_{\text{Treatments}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/15f1d15917c292f1222c03d0684fda8d075c2c77)

### The *F*-test

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=18 "Edit section: The F-test")]

Main article: [F-test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-test "F-test")

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/51/F-Distribution_Table.png/338px-F-Distribution_Table.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:F-Distribution_Table.png)

To check for statistical significance of a one-way ANOVA, we consult the F-probability table using degrees of freedom at the 0.05 alpha level. After computing the F-statistic, we compare the value at the intersection of each degrees of freedom, also known as the critical value. If one's F-statistic is greater in magnitude than their critical value, we can say there is statistical significance at the 0.05 alpha level.

The [*F*-test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-test "F-test") is used for comparing the factors of the total deviation. For example, in one-way, or single-factor ANOVA, statistical significance is tested for by comparing the F test statistic

![{\displaystyle F={\frac {\text{variance between treatments}}{\text{variance within treatments}}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0ade25bf617936071e81b18854b055fe70b3aea2) ![{\displaystyle F={\frac {MS\_{\text{Treatments}}}{MS\_{\text{Error}}}}={{SS\_{\text{Treatments}}/(I-1)} \over {SS\_{\text{Error}}/(n\_{T}-I)}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/12ace46c6898830709e4c0940817597d6d0119b0)

where *MS* is mean square, ![{\displaystyle I}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/535ea7fc4134a31cbe2251d9d3511374bc41be9f) is the number of treatments and ![{\displaystyle n\_{T}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1972fb23b45426057388210f0927fb46d6ff64ca) is the total number of cases

to the [*F*-distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-distribution "F-distribution") with ![{\displaystyle I-1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/223af0e915179ab1ee23d7c85a34b76f6d5bf071) being the numerator degrees of freedom and ![{\displaystyle n\_{T}-I}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8504189094a94b413c98f05b20738611c1a019b5) the denominator degrees of freedom. Using the *F*-distribution is a natural candidate because the test statistic is the ratio of two scaled sums of squares each of which follows a scaled [chi-squared distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chi-squared_distribution "Chi-squared distribution").

The expected value of F is ![{\displaystyle 1+{n\sigma \_{\text{Treatment}}^{2}}/{\sigma \_{\text{Error}}^{2}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1213400260b18cf5a8c6b2216543e1bcdea72849) (where ![{\displaystyle n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a601995d55609f2d9f5e233e36fbe9ea26011b3b) is the treatment sample size) which is 1 for no treatment effect. As values of F increase above 1, the evidence is increasingly inconsistent with the null hypothesis. Two apparent experimental methods of increasing F are increasing the sample size and reducing the error variance by tight experimental controls.

There are two methods of concluding the ANOVA hypothesis test, both of which produce the same result:

- The textbook method is to compare the observed value of F with the critical value of F determined from tables. The critical value of F is a function of the degrees of freedom of the numerator and the denominator and the significance level (*α*). If F ≥ FCritical, the null hypothesis is rejected.
- The computer method calculates the probability (p-value) of a value of F greater than or equal to the observed value. The null hypothesis is rejected if this probability is less than or equal to the significance level (*α*).

The ANOVA *F*-test is known to be nearly optimal in the sense of minimizing false negative errors for a fixed rate of false positive errors (i.e. maximizing power for a fixed significance level). For example, to test the hypothesis that various medical treatments have exactly the same effect, the [*F*-test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-test "F-test")'s *p*-values closely approximate the [permutation test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permutation_test "Permutation test")'s [p-values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-value "P-value"): The approximation is particularly close when the design is balanced.[\[26\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-HinkelmannKempthorne-27)[\[37\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-38) Such [permutation tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permutation_test "Permutation test") characterize [tests with maximum power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniformly_most_powerful_test "Uniformly most powerful test") against all [alternative hypotheses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternative_hypothesis "Alternative hypothesis"), as observed by [Rosenbaum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_R._Rosenbaum "Paul R. Rosenbaum").[\[nb 2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-39) The ANOVA *F*-test (of the null-hypothesis that all treatments have exactly the same effect) is recommended as a practical test, because of its robustness against many alternative distributions.[\[38\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-40)[\[nb 3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-41)

### Extended algorithm

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=19 "Edit section: Extended algorithm")]

ANOVA consists of separable parts; partitioning sources of variance and hypothesis testing can be used individually. ANOVA is used to support other statistical tools. Regression is first used to fit more complex models to data, then ANOVA is used to compare models with the objective of selecting simple(r) models that adequately describe the data. "Such models could be fit without any reference to ANOVA, but ANOVA tools could then be used to make some sense of the fitted models, and to test hypotheses about batches of coefficients."[\[39\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Gelman-42) "\[W]e think of the analysis of variance as a way of understanding and structuring multilevel models—not as an alternative to regression but as a tool for summarizing complex high-dimensional inferences ..."[\[39\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Gelman-42)

## For a single factor

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=20 "Edit section: For a single factor")]

Main article: [One-way analysis of variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-way_analysis_of_variance "One-way analysis of variance")

The simplest experiment suitable for ANOVA analysis is the completely randomized experiment with a single factor. More complex experiments with a single factor involve constraints on randomization and include completely randomized blocks and [Latin squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_square "Latin square") (and variants: [Graeco-Latin squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutually_orthogonal_Latin_squares "Mutually orthogonal Latin squares"), etc.). The more complex experiments share many of the complexities of multiple factors.

There are some alternatives to conventional one-way analysis of variance, e.g.: Welch's heteroscedastic F test, Welch's heteroscedastic F test with trimmed means and Winsorized variances, Brown-Forsythe test, Alexander-Govern test, James second order test and Kruskal-Wallis test, available in [onewaytests](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/onewaytests/index.html) [R](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R_package "R package")

It is useful to represent each data point in the following form, called a statistical model: ![{\displaystyle Y\_{ij}=\mu +\tau \_{j}+\varepsilon \_{ij}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8d9d12663330435f12c058890388c1a9527b4bfd) where

- *i* = 1, 2, 3, ..., *R*
- *j* = 1, 2, 3, ..., *C*
- *μ* = overall average (mean)
- *τ**j* = differential effect (response) associated with the *j* level of X;this assumes that overall the values of *τ**j* add to zero (that is, ![{\textstyle \sum \_{j=1}^{C}\tau \_{j}=0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2e56b7a7ddc51dc65deb203efbed7222c48f752f))
- *ε**ij* = noise or error associated with the particular *ij* data value

That is, we envision an additive model that says every data point can be represented by summing three quantities: the true mean, averaged over all factor levels being investigated, plus an incremental component associated with the particular column (factor level), plus a final component associated with everything else affecting that specific data value.

## For multiple factors

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=21 "Edit section: For multiple factors")]

Main article: [Two-way analysis of variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-way_analysis_of_variance "Two-way analysis of variance")

ANOVA generalizes to the study of the effects of multiple factors. When the experiment includes observations at all combinations of levels of each factor, it is termed [factorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factorial_experiment "Factorial experiment"). Factorial experiments are more efficient than a series of single factor experiments and the efficiency grows as the number of factors increases.[\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Montgomery-43) Consequently, factorial designs are heavily used.

The use of ANOVA to study the effects of multiple factors has a complication. In a 3-way ANOVA with factors x, y and z, the ANOVA model includes terms for the main effects (x, y, z) and terms for [interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interaction_\(statistics\) "Interaction (statistics)") (xy, xz, yz, xyz). All terms require hypothesis tests. The proliferation of interaction terms increases the risk that some hypothesis test will produce a false positive by chance. Fortunately, experience says that high order interactions are rare.[\[41\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-44) \[*[verification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability "Wikipedia:Verifiability")*] The ability to detect interactions is a major advantage of multiple factor ANOVA. Testing one factor at a time hides interactions, but produces apparently inconsistent experimental results.[\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Montgomery-43)

Caution is advised when encountering interactions; Test interaction terms first and expand the analysis beyond ANOVA if interactions are found. Texts vary in their recommendations regarding the continuation of the ANOVA procedure after encountering an interaction. Interactions complicate the interpretation of experimental data. Neither the calculations of significance nor the estimated treatment effects can be taken at face value. "A significant interaction will often mask the significance of main effects."[\[42\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-45) Graphical methods are recommended to enhance understanding. Regression is often useful. A lengthy discussion of interactions is available in Cox (1958).[\[43\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-46) Some interactions can be removed (by transformations) while others cannot.

A variety of techniques are used with multiple factor ANOVA to reduce expense. One technique used in factorial designs is to minimize replication (possibly no replication with support of [analytical trickery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tukey%27s_test_of_additivity "Tukey's test of additivity")) and to combine groups when effects are found to be statistically (or practically) insignificant. An experiment with many insignificant factors may collapse into one with a few factors supported by many replications.[\[44\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-47)

## Associated analysis

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=22 "Edit section: Associated analysis")]

Some analysis is required in support of the *design* of the experiment while other analysis is performed after changes in the factors are formally found to produce statistically significant changes in the responses. Because experimentation is iterative, the results of one experiment alter plans for following experiments.

### Preparatory analysis

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=23 "Edit section: Preparatory analysis")]

#### The number of experimental units

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=24 "Edit section: The number of experimental units")]

In the design of an experiment, the number of experimental units is planned to satisfy the goals of the experiment. Experimentation is often sequential.

Early experiments are often designed to provide mean-unbiased estimates of treatment effects and of experimental error. Later experiments are often designed to test a hypothesis that a treatment effect has an important magnitude; in this case, the number of experimental units is chosen so that the experiment is within budget and has adequate power, among other goals.

Reporting sample size analysis is generally required in psychology. "Provide information on sample size and the process that led to sample size decisions."[\[45\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-48) The analysis, which is written in the experimental protocol before the experiment is conducted, is examined in grant applications and administrative review boards.

Besides the power analysis, there are less formal methods for selecting the number of experimental units. These include graphical methods based on limiting the probability of false negative errors, graphical methods based on an expected variation increase (above the residuals) and methods based on achieving a desired confidence interval.[\[46\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-49)

#### Power analysis

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=25 "Edit section: Power analysis")]

[Power analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_power "Statistical power") is often applied in the context of ANOVA in order to assess the probability of successfully rejecting the null hypothesis if we assume a certain ANOVA design, effect size in the population, sample size and significance level. Power analysis can assist in study design by determining what sample size would be required in order to have a reasonable chance of rejecting the null hypothesis when the alternative hypothesis is true.[\[47\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-50)[\[48\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-51)[\[49\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-52)[\[50\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-53)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1e/Effect_size.png/220px-Effect_size.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Effect_size.png)

Effect size

#### Effect size

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=26 "Edit section: Effect size")]

Main article: [Effect size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effect_size "Effect size")

Several standardized measures of effect have been proposed for ANOVA to summarize the strength of the association between a predictor(s) and the dependent variable or the overall standardized difference of the complete model. Standardized effect-size estimates facilitate comparison of findings across studies and disciplines. However, while standardized effect sizes are commonly used in much of the professional literature, a non-standardized measure of effect size that has immediately "meaningful" units may be preferable for reporting purposes.[\[51\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Wilkinson-54)

#### Model confirmation

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=27 "Edit section: Model confirmation")]

Sometimes tests are conducted to determine whether the assumptions of ANOVA appear to be violated. Residuals are examined or analyzed to confirm [homoscedasticity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homoscedasticity "Homoscedasticity") and gross normality.[\[52\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-55) Residuals should have the appearance of (zero mean normal distribution) noise when plotted as a function of anything including time and modeled data values. Trends hint at interactions among factors or among observations.

#### Follow-up tests

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=28 "Edit section: Follow-up tests")]

A statistically significant effect in ANOVA is often followed by additional tests. This can be done in order to assess which groups are different from which other groups or to test various other focused hypotheses. Follow-up tests are often distinguished in terms of whether they are "planned" ([a priori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_priori_and_a_posteriori "A priori and a posteriori")) or ["post hoc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-hoc_analysis "Post-hoc analysis")." Planned tests are determined before looking at the data, and post hoc tests are conceived only after looking at the data (though the term "post hoc" is inconsistently used).

The follow-up tests may be "simple" pairwise comparisons of individual group means or may be "compound" comparisons (e.g., comparing the mean pooling across groups A, B and C to the mean of group D). Comparisons can also look at tests of trend, such as linear and quadratic relationships, when the independent variable involves ordered levels. Often the follow-up tests incorporate a method of adjusting for the [multiple comparisons problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_comparisons_problem "Multiple comparisons problem").

Follow-up tests to identify which specific groups, variables, or factors have statistically different means include the [Tukey's range test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tukey%27s_range_test "Tukey's range test"), and [Duncan's new multiple range test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duncan%27s_new_multiple_range_test "Duncan's new multiple range test"). In turn, these tests are often followed with a [Compact Letter Display (CLD)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_Letter_Display_\(CLD\) "Compact Letter Display (CLD)") methodology in order to render the output of the mentioned tests more transparent to a non-statistician audience.

## Study designs

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=29 "Edit section: Study designs")]

There are several types of ANOVA. Many statisticians base ANOVA on the [design of the experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_design "Experimental design"),[\[53\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-56) especially on the protocol that specifies the [random assignment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_assignment "Random assignment") of treatments to subjects; the protocol's description of the assignment mechanism should include a specification of the structure of the treatments and of any [blocking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blocking_\(statistics\) "Blocking (statistics)"). It is also common to apply ANOVA to observational data using an appropriate statistical model.[\[54\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-57)

Some popular designs use the following types of ANOVA:

- [One-way ANOVA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-way_ANOVA "One-way ANOVA") is used to test for differences among two or more [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence "Statistical independence") groups (means), e.g. different levels of urea application in a crop, or different levels of antibiotic action on several different bacterial species,[\[55\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-58) or different levels of effect of some medicine on groups of patients. However, should these groups not be independent, and there is an order in the groups (such as mild, moderate and severe disease), or in the dose of a drug (such as 5 mg/mL, 10 mg/mL, 20 mg/mL) given to the same group of patients, then a [linear trend estimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_trend_estimation "Linear trend estimation") should be used. Typically, however, the one-way ANOVA is used to test for differences among at least three groups, since the two-group case can be covered by a [t-test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-test "T-test").[\[56\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-59) When there are only two means to compare, the [t-test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-test "T-test") and the ANOVA [*F*-test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-test "F-test") are equivalent; the relation between ANOVA and *t* is given by *F* = *t*2.
- [Factorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factorial_experiment "Factorial experiment") ANOVA is used when there is more than one factor.
- [Repeated measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repeated_measures "Repeated measures") ANOVA is used when the same subjects are used for each factor (e.g., in a [longitudinal study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitudinal_study "Longitudinal study")).
- [Multivariate analysis of variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_analysis_of_variance "Multivariate analysis of variance") (MANOVA) is used when there is more than one [response variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dependent_variable "Dependent variable").

## Cautions

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=30 "Edit section: Cautions")]

Balanced experiments (those with an equal sample size for each treatment) are relatively easy to interpret; unbalanced experiments offer more complexity. For single-factor (one-way) ANOVA, the adjustment for unbalanced data is easy, but the unbalanced analysis lacks both robustness and power.[\[57\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-60) For more complex designs the lack of balance leads to further complications. "The orthogonality property of main effects and interactions present in balanced data does not carry over to the unbalanced case. This means that the usual analysis of variance techniques do not apply. Consequently, the analysis of unbalanced factorials is much more difficult than that for balanced designs."[\[58\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-61) In the general case, "The analysis of variance can also be applied to unbalanced data, but then the sums of squares, mean squares, and *F*-ratios will depend on the order in which the sources of variation are considered."[\[39\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Gelman-42)

ANOVA is (in part) a test of statistical significance. The American Psychological Association (and many other organisations) holds the view that simply reporting statistical significance is insufficient and that reporting confidence bounds is preferred.[\[51\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Wilkinson-54)

## Generalizations

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=31 "Edit section: Generalizations")]

ANOVA is considered to be a special case of [linear regression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_regression "Linear regression")[\[59\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-62)[\[60\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-63) which in turn is a special case of the [general linear model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_linear_model "General linear model").[\[61\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-64) All consider the observations to be the sum of a model (fit) and a residual (error) to be minimized.

The [Kruskal-Wallis test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kruskal-Wallis_test "Kruskal-Wallis test") and the [Friedman test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedman_test "Friedman test") are [nonparametric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonparametric "Nonparametric") tests which do not rely on an assumption of normality.[\[62\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-65)[\[63\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-66)

### Connection to linear regression

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=32 "Edit section: Connection to linear regression")]

Below we make clear the connection between multi-way ANOVA and linear regression.

Linearly re-order the data so that ![{\displaystyle k}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c3c9a2c7b599b37105512c5d570edc034056dd40)-th observation is associated with a response ![{\displaystyle y\_{k}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4b2ab0248723a410cc2c67ce06ad5c043dcbb933) and factors ![{\displaystyle Z\_{k,b}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/dfd980bd28882b4d8cd3a2615dadf66eff4af64b) where ![{\displaystyle b\in \\{1,2,\ldots ,B\\}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ef45a3e38168411ee8b7b62cbcd60ee6d2946b65) denotes the different factors and ![{\displaystyle B}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/47136aad860d145f75f3eed3022df827cee94d7a) is the total number of factors. In one-way ANOVA ![{\displaystyle B=1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3f9cd3a9b87af7f457ce808ce2020f4af99d543d) and in two-way ANOVA ![{\displaystyle B=2}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4a46dbc619c25f03bb05880f1c44bdcf0753d52d). Furthermore, we assume the ![{\displaystyle b}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f11423fbb2e967f986e36804a8ae4271734917c3)-th factor has ![{\displaystyle I\_{b}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5468195bb8c7d158a80efe38ceadcdb2b8fe4d4e) levels, namely ![{\displaystyle \\{1,2,\ldots ,I\_{b}\\}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a5d693f4dcde56a20aad6c4c9d4aac1b900bf564). Now, we can [one-hot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-hot "One-hot") encode the factors into the ![{\textstyle \sum \_{b=1}^{B}I\_{b}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2e78af60bc178864732a14c9dae5df886d27ab37) dimensional vector ![{\displaystyle v\_{k}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d142b4083872eb72f81c1e20fd2c91d02b4a9838).

The one-hot encoding function ![{\displaystyle g\_{b}:\\{1,2,\ldots ,I\_{b}\\}\mapsto \\{0,1\\}^{I\_{b}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/bbebac510e860e7fa1c41ade5d38dd7cf807fc40) is defined such that the ![{\displaystyle i}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/add78d8608ad86e54951b8c8bd6c8d8416533d20)-th entry of ![{\displaystyle g\_{b}(Z\_{k,b})}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/44bc321d5fa01555ebe73dddd9f0a4753e644693) is ![{\displaystyle g\_{b}(Z\_{k,b})\_{i}={\begin{cases}1&{\text{if }}i=Z\_{k,b}\\\0&{\text{otherwise}}\end{cases}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0470e461810140e0c7ee0eb3de8ac95b7477e8f9) The vector ![{\displaystyle v\_{k}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d142b4083872eb72f81c1e20fd2c91d02b4a9838) is the concatenation of all of the above vectors for all ![{\displaystyle b}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f11423fbb2e967f986e36804a8ae4271734917c3). Thus, ![{\displaystyle v\_{k}=\[g\_{1}(Z\_{k,1}),g\_{2}(Z\_{k,2}),\ldots ,g\_{B}(Z\_{k,B})\]}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8476d72369317405eaaeddaea47d3d02a6f65689). In order to obtain a fully general ![{\displaystyle B}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/47136aad860d145f75f3eed3022df827cee94d7a)-way interaction ANOVA we must also concatenate every additional interaction term in the vector ![{\displaystyle v\_{k}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d142b4083872eb72f81c1e20fd2c91d02b4a9838) and then add an intercept term. Let that vector be ![{\displaystyle X\_{k}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/33c25229c6989c235f9cbb7908331f6d01d0abfe).

With this notation in place, we now have the exact connection with linear regression. We simply regress response ![{\displaystyle y\_{k}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4b2ab0248723a410cc2c67ce06ad5c043dcbb933) against the vector ![{\displaystyle X\_{k}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/33c25229c6989c235f9cbb7908331f6d01d0abfe). However, there is a concern about [identifiability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identifiability "Identifiability"). In order to overcome such issues we assume that the sum of the parameters within each set of interactions is equal to zero. From here, one can use *F*-statistics or other methods to determine the relevance of the individual factors.

#### Example

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=33 "Edit section: Example")]

We can consider the 2-way interaction example where we assume that the first factor has 2 levels and the second factor has 3 levels.

## Footnotes

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Analysis_of_variance\&action=edit\&section=35 "Edit section: Footnotes")]

1. **[^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_ref-22)** Unit-treatment additivity is simply termed additivity in most texts. Hinkelmann and Kempthorne add adjectives and distinguish between additivity in the strict and broad senses. This allows a detailed consideration of multiple error sources (treatment, state, selection, measurement and sampling) on page 161.
2. **[^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_ref-39)** Rosenbaum (2002, page 40) cites Section 5.7 (Permutation Tests), Theorem 2.3 (actually Theorem 3, page 184) of [Lehmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Leo_Lehmann "Erich Leo Lehmann")'s *Testing Statistical Hypotheses* (1959).
3. **[^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_ref-41)** The *F*-test for the comparison of variances has a mixed reputation. It is not recommended as a hypothesis test to determine whether two *different* samples have the same variance. It is recommended for ANOVA where two estimates of the variance of the *same* sample are compared. While the *F*-test is not generally robust against departures from normality, it has been found to be robust in the special case of ANOVA. Citations from Moore & McCabe (2003): "Analysis of variance uses F statistics, but these are not the same as the F statistic for comparing two population standard deviations." (page 554) "The F test and other procedures for inference about variances are so lacking in robustness as to be of little use in practice." (page 556) "\[The ANOVA *F*-test] is relatively insensitive to moderate nonnormality and unequal variances, especially when the sample sizes are similar." (page 763) ANOVA assumes homoscedasticity, but it is robust. The statistical test for homoscedasticity (the *F*-test) is not robust. Moore & McCabe recommend a rule of thumb.
