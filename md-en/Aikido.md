**Aikido** ([Shinjitai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinjitai "Shinjitai"): 合気道, [Kyūjitai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ky%C5%ABjitai "Kyūjitai"): 合氣道, *aikidō*, Japanese pronunciation: [\[aikiꜜdoː\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Japanese "Help:IPA/Japanese")) is a [modern Japanese martial art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gendai_bud%C5%8D "Gendai budō") which is split into many different styles including [Iwama Ryu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwama_Ryu "Iwama Ryu"), [Iwama Shin Shin Aiki Shuren Kai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwama_Shin_Shin_Aiki_Shuren_Kai "Iwama Shin Shin Aiki Shuren Kai"), [Shodokan Aikido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shodokan_Aikido "Shodokan Aikido"), [Yoshinkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshinkan "Yoshinkan"), [Renshinkai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renshinkai "Renshinkai"), [Aikikai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aikikai "Aikikai"), and [Ki Aikido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ki_Aikido "Ki Aikido"). Aikido is now practiced in around 140 countries. It was originally developed by [Morihei Ueshiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morihei_Ueshiba "Morihei Ueshiba"), as a synthesis of his martial studies, philosophy and religious beliefs. Ueshiba's goal was to create an art which practitioners could use to defend themselves while also protecting their attackers from injury.[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-1)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-2) Aikido is often translated as "the way of unifying (with) [life energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qi "Qi")"[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-3) or as "the way of harmonious spirit".[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-ADS-4) According to the founder's philosophy, the primary goal in the practice of aikido is to overcome oneself instead of cultivating violence or aggressiveness.[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Jones2015-5) Morihei Ueshiba used the phrase *masakatsu agatsu katsuhayabi"* (正勝吾勝勝速日, "true victory, final victory over oneself, here and now") to refer to this principle.[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Gordon2019-6)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0f/Ueshiba_Mitsuteru_%28Nippon_Budokan%2C_2017%29.webm/220px--Ueshiba_Mitsuteru_%28Nippon_Budokan%2C_2017%29.webm.jpg)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/0/0f/Ueshiba_Mitsuteru_%28Nippon_Budokan%2C_2017%29.webm/Ueshiba_Mitsuteru_%28Nippon_Budokan%2C_2017%29.webm.480p.vp9.webm)

[Mitsuteru Ueshiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsuteru_Ueshiba "Mitsuteru Ueshiba") at the 55th All Japan Aikido Demonstration held at the Nippon Budokan (May 2017)

Aikido's fundamental principles include: *[irimi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irimi "Irimi")*[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-7) (entering), *[atemi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atemi "Atemi")*,[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-8)[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-9) *kokyu-ho* (breathing control), *sankaku-ho* (triangular principle), and *[tenkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenkan "Tenkan")* (turning) movements which redirect the opponent's attack [momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum "Momentum"). Its curriculum comprises various techniques, primarily [throws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Throw_\(grappling\) "Throw (grappling)") and [joint locks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_lock "Joint lock").[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Aikido-10) It includes a weapons system encompassing the *[bokken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bokken "Bokken")*, *[tantō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tant%C5%8D "Tantō")*, and *[jō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C5%8D "Jō")*. Aikido derives mainly from the martial art of [Daitō-ryū Aiki-jūjutsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dait%C5%8D-ry%C5%AB_Aiki-j%C5%ABjutsu "Daitō-ryū Aiki-jūjutsu"), but began to diverge from it in the late 1920s, partly due to Ueshiba's involvement with the [Ōmoto-kyō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Cmoto-ky%C5%8D "Ōmoto-kyō") religion. Ueshiba's early students' documents bear the term *aiki-jūjutsu*.[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Pranin-Aikijujutsu-11)

Ueshiba's senior students have different approaches to aikido, depending partly on when they studied with him. Today, aikido is found all over the world in a number of styles, with broad ranges of interpretation and emphasis. However they all share techniques formulated by Ueshiba and most have concern for the well-being of the attacker.

## Etymology and basic philosophy

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Aikido\&action=edit\&section=1 "Edit section: Etymology and basic philosophy")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6b/%E5%90%88%E6%B0%A3%E9%81%93.svg/80px-%E5%90%88%E6%B0%A3%E9%81%93.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:%E5%90%88%E6%B0%A3%E9%81%93.svg)

"Aikidō" written with "[ki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qi "Qi")" in its [old character form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ky%C5%ABjitai "Kyūjitai")

The word "aikido" is formed of three [kanji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanji "Kanji"):

- [合](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/en:%E5%90%88 "wikt:en:合") – *ai* – harmony, unifying
- [気](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/en:%E6%B0%97 "wikt:en:気") – *ki* – energy, spirit
- [道](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/en:%E9%81%93 "wikt:en:道") – *dō* – way, path

The term *[aiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aiki_\(martial_arts_principle\) "Aiki (martial arts principle)")* does not readily appear in the Japanese language outside the scope of [budō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bud%C5%8D "Budō"). This has led to many possible interpretations of the word. 合 (*Ai*) is mainly used in compounds to mean 'combine, unite, join, meet', examples being 合同 (combined/united), 合成 (composition), 結合 (unite/combine/join), 連合 (union/alliance/association), 統合 (combine/unify), and 合意 (mutual agreement). There is an idea of [reciprocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norm_of_reciprocity "Norm of reciprocity"), 知り合う (to get to know one another), 話し合い (talk/discussion/negotiation), and 待ち合わせる (meet by appointment).

気 (*ki*) is often used to describe a feeling or emotive action, as in X気がする ('I feel X', as in terms of thinking but with less cognitive reasoning), and 気持ち (feeling/sensation); it is used to mean energy or force, as in 電気 (electricity) and 磁気 (magnetism); it can also refer to qualities or aspects of people or things, as in 気質 (spirit/trait/temperament).

The term *dō* in Aikido is found in many other Japanese martial arts such as, [judo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judo "Judo") and [kendo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kendo "Kendo"), and in various non-martial arts, such as [Japanese calligraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_calligraphy "Japanese calligraphy") (*shodō*), [flower arranging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ikebana "Ikebana") (*kadō*) and [tea ceremony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_tea_ceremony "Japanese tea ceremony") (*chadō* or *sadō*). Therefore, from a purely literal interpretation, aikido is the "Way of combining forces" or "Way of unifying energy", in which the term *aiki* refers to the martial arts principle or tactic of blending with an attacker's movements for the purpose of controlling their actions with minimal effort.[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-12) One applies *aiki* by understanding the rhythm and intent of the attacker to find the optimal position and timing to apply a counter-technique.

## History

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Aikido\&action=edit\&section=2 "Edit section: History")]

[![A middle-aged, mustachioed man in a kimono](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e3/Morihei_Ueshiba_1939.jpg/110px-Morihei_Ueshiba_1939.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Morihei_Ueshiba_1939.jpg)

Ueshiba in Tokyo in 1939

Aikido was created by [Morihei Ueshiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ueshiba_Morihei "Ueshiba Morihei") (植芝 盛平) (1883–1969), referred to by some aikido practitioners as *Ōsensei* (*Great Teacher*).[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-13) The term *aikido* was coined in the 20th century.[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-14) Ueshiba envisioned aikido not only as the synthesis of his martial training, but as an expression of his personal philosophy of universal peace and reconciliation. During Ueshiba's lifetime and continuing today, aikido has evolved from the *aiki* that Ueshiba studied into a variety of expressions by martial artists throughout the world.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Aikido-10)

### Initial development

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Aikido\&action=edit\&section=3 "Edit section: Initial development")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b6/Takeda_Sokaku.jpg/150px-Takeda_Sokaku.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Takeda_Sokaku.jpg)

Takeda Sōkaku

Ueshiba developed aikido primarily during the late 1920s through the 1930s through the synthesis of the older martial arts that he had studied.[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-AWH-15) The core martial art from which aikido derives is [Daitō-ryū Aiki-jūjutsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dait%C5%8D-ry%C5%AB_Aiki-j%C5%ABjutsu "Daitō-ryū Aiki-jūjutsu"), which Ueshiba studied directly with [Takeda Sōkaku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takeda_S%C5%8Dkaku "Takeda Sōkaku"), the reviver of that art. Additionally, Ueshiba is known to have studied [Tenjin Shin'yō-ryū](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenjin_Shin%27y%C5%8D-ry%C5%AB "Tenjin Shin'yō-ryū") with Tozawa Tokusaburō in [Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo "Tokyo") in 1901, [Gotōha Yagyū Shingan-ryū](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yagy%C5%AB_Shingan-ry%C5%AB "Yagyū Shingan-ryū") under Nakai Masakatsu in [Sakai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sakai,_Osaka "Sakai, Osaka") from 1903 to 1908, and [judo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judo "Judo") with Kiyoichi Takagi (高木 喜代市) (1894–1972) in [Tanabe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanabe,_Wakayama "Tanabe, Wakayama") in 1911.[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-16)

The art of *Daitō-ryū* is the primary technical influence on aikido. Along with empty-handed throwing and joint-locking techniques, Ueshiba incorporated training movements with weapons, such as those for the [spear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spear "Spear") (*[yari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yari "Yari")*), short [staff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stick_fighting "Stick fighting") (*[jō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C5%8D "Jō")*), and possibly the [bayonet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayonet "Bayonet") (銃剣, *jūken*). Aikido also derives much of its technical structure from the art of swordsmanship (*[kenjutsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenjutsu "Kenjutsu")*).[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-ADS-4)[\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-TSoA-17) Ueshiba moved to [Hokkaidō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hokkaid%C5%8D "Hokkaidō") in 1912, and began studying under Takeda Sokaku in 1915; His official association with Daitō-ryū continued until 1937.[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-AWH-15) However, during the latter part of that period, Ueshiba had already begun to distance himself from Takeda and the *Daitō-ryū*. At that time Ueshiba referred to his martial art as "**Aiki Budō**". It is unclear exactly when Ueshiba began using the name "aikido", but it became the official name of the art in 1942 when the Greater Japan Martial Virtue Society (*[Dai Nippon Butoku Kai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dai_Nippon_Butoku_Kai "Dai Nippon Butoku Kai")*) was engaged in a government sponsored reorganization and centralization of Japanese martial arts.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Aikido-10)

### Religious influences

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Aikido\&action=edit\&section=4 "Edit section: Religious influences")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Onisaburo_Deguchi_2.jpg/150px-Onisaburo_Deguchi_2.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Onisaburo_Deguchi_2.jpg)

Onisaburo Deguchi

After Ueshiba left Hokkaidō in 1919, he met and was profoundly influenced by [Onisaburo Deguchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onisaburo_Deguchi "Onisaburo Deguchi"), the spiritual leader of the [Ōmoto-kyō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Cmoto-ky%C5%8D "Ōmoto-kyō") religion (a neo-[Shinto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinto "Shinto") movement) in [Ayabe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayabe,_Kyoto "Ayabe, Kyoto").[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-18) One of the primary features of Ōmoto-kyō is its emphasis on the attainment of [utopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utopia "Utopia") during one's life. The idea was a great influence on Ueshiba's martial arts philosophy of extending love and compassion especially to those who seek to harm others. Aikido demonstrates this philosophy in its emphasis on mastering martial arts so that one may receive an attack and harmlessly redirect it. In an ideal resolution, not only is the receiver unharmed, but so is the attacker.[\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-19) In addition to the effect on his spiritual growth, the connection with Deguchi gave Ueshiba entry to elite political and military circles as a martial artist. As a result of the exposure, he was able to attract not only financial backing but also gifted students. Several of the students went on to begin their own styles of aikido.[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Styles-20)

### International dissemination

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Aikido\&action=edit\&section=5 "Edit section: International dissemination")]

Aikido was first introduced to the rest of the world in 1951 by [Minoru Mochizuki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minoru_Mochizuki "Minoru Mochizuki") with a visit to [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France "France"), where he demonstrated aikido techniques to judo students.[\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Mochizuki-21) He was followed by [Tadashi Abe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tadashi_Abe "Tadashi Abe") in 1952, who came as the official [Aikikai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aikikai "Aikikai") Hombu representative, remaining in France for seven years. [Kenji Tomiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenji_Tomiki "Kenji Tomiki") toured with a delegation of various martial arts through 15 continental states of the United States in 1953.[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Styles-20)[\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-22) Later in the same year, [Koichi Tohei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koichi_Tohei "Koichi Tohei") was sent by Aikikai Hombu to [Hawaii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaii "Hawaii") for a full year, where he set up several [dōjō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C5%8Dj%C5%8D "Dōjō"). The trip was followed by several visits and is considered the formal introduction of aikido to the United States. The United Kingdom followed in 1955; Italy in 1964 by [Hiroshi Tada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiroshi_Tada "Hiroshi Tada"); and Germany in 1965 by [Katsuaki Asai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katsuaki_Asai "Katsuaki Asai"). Designated the "Official Delegate for Europe and Africa" by Morihei Ueshiba, [Masamichi Noro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masamichi_Noro "Masamichi Noro") arrived in France in September 1961. [Seiichi Sugano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seiichi_Sugano "Seiichi Sugano") was appointed to introduce aikido to Australia in 1965. Today there are aikido dōjō throughout the world. [Steven Seagal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Seagal "Steven Seagal")'s film debut *[Above the Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Above_the_Law_\(1988_film\) "Above the Law (1988 film)")* (1988) is regarded as the first American film to feature aikido in fight sequences.[\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-23)[\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-24)

### Proliferation of independent organizations

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Aikido\&action=edit\&section=6 "Edit section: Proliferation of independent organizations")]

Further information: [Aikido styles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aikido_styles "Aikido styles")

See also: [List of aikidōka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_aikidoka "List of aikidoka")

The largest aikido organization is the [Aikikai Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aikikai_Foundation "Aikikai Foundation"), which remains under the control of the Ueshiba family. However, aikido has developed into many styles, most of which were formed by Morihei Ueshiba's major students.[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Styles-20) The earliest independent styles to emerge were [Yoseikan Aikido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoseikan_Aikido "Yoseikan Aikido"), begun by [Minoru Mochizuki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minoru_Mochizuki "Minoru Mochizuki") in 1931,[\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Mochizuki-21) [Yoshinkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshinkan "Yoshinkan") Aikido, founded by [Gozo Shioda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gozo_Shioda "Gozo Shioda") in 1955,[\[25\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-25) and [Shodokan Aikido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shodokan_Aikido "Shodokan Aikido"), founded by [Kenji Tomiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenji_Tomiki "Kenji Tomiki") in 1967.[\[26\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-ACE-26) The emergence of these styles pre-dated Ueshiba's death and did not cause any major upheavals when they were formalized. Shodokan Aikido, however, was controversial, since it introduced a unique rule-based competition that some felt was contrary to the spirit of aikido.[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Styles-20)

After Ueshiba's death in 1969, two more major styles emerged. Significant controversy arose with the departure of the [Aikikai Hombu Dojo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aikikai_Hombu_Dojo "Aikikai Hombu Dojo")'s chief instructor [Koichi Tohei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koichi_Tohei "Koichi Tohei"), in 1974. Tohei left as a result of a disagreement with the son of the founder, [Kisshomaru Ueshiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kisshomaru_Ueshiba "Kisshomaru Ueshiba"), who at that time headed the Aikikai Foundation. The disagreement was over the proper role of *ki* development in regular aikido training. After Tohei left, he formed his own style, called [Shin Shin Toitsu Aikido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shin_Shin_Toitsu_Aikido "Shin Shin Toitsu Aikido"), and the organization that governs it, the [Ki Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ki_Society "Ki Society") (*Ki no Kenkyūkai*).[\[27\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-27) A final major style evolved from Ueshiba's retirement in [Iwama, Ibaraki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwama,_Ibaraki "Iwama, Ibaraki") and the teaching methodology of long term student [Morihiro Saito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morihiro_Saito "Morihiro Saito"). It is unofficially referred to as the "[Iwama style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwama_style "Iwama style")", and at one point a number of its followers formed a loose network of schools they called [Iwama Ryu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwama_Ryu "Iwama Ryu"). Although Iwama style practitioners remained part of the Aikikai until Saito's death in 2002, followers of Saito subsequently split into two groups. One remained with the Aikikai and the other formed the independent [Shinshin Aikishuren Kai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinshin_Aikishuren_Kai "Shinshin Aikishuren Kai") in 2004 around Saito's son [Hitohiro Saito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitohiro_Saito "Hitohiro Saito").

Today, the major styles of aikido are each run by a separate governing organization, have their own headquarters (本部道場, *honbu dōjō*) in Japan, and are taught throughout the world.[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Styles-20)

## *Ki*

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Aikido\&action=edit\&section=7 "Edit section: Ki")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0a/Ki_obsolete.svg/100px-Ki_obsolete.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Ki_obsolete.svg)

The [kanji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanji "Kanji") for *ki* until 1946 is shown; in 1946 [it was changed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%8Dy%C5%8D_kanji "Tōyō kanji") to [気](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%B0%97 "wikt:気").

The study of *[ki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qi "Qi")* is an important component of aikido. The term does not specifically refer to either physical or mental training, as it encompasses both. The [kanji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanji "Kanji") for *ki* was written in its older form as 氣 up until the Japanese governmental [writing reforms after World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%8Dy%C5%8D_kanji "Tōyō kanji"), and now is more prevalently seen in its modern form of 気. This form has the removal of the eight directions denoting the pre and post natal energies of *genki* (元氣) (Chinese – *[yuán qì](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuanqi "Yuanqi")*) also known in the Art of Aikido as "Source energy".

The character for *ki* is used in everyday Japanese terms, such as "health" (元気, *genki*), or "shyness" (内気, *uchiki*). *Ki* has many meanings, including "ambience", "mind", "mood", or "intention and action", however, in traditional martial arts and medicine it is often referred to in its more general terminology as "life energy". [Gozo Shioda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gozo_Shioda "Gozo Shioda")'s [Yoshinkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshinkan "Yoshinkan") Aikido, considered one of the "hard styles", largely follows Ueshiba's teachings from before World War II, and surmises that the secret to *ki* lies in timing and the application of the whole body's strength to a single point.[\[28\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Shugyo-28) In later years, Ueshiba's application of *ki* in aikido took on a softer, more gentle feel. The concept was known as [Takemusu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takemusu "Takemusu") Aiki, and many of his later students teach about *ki* from this perspective. [Koichi Tohei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koichi_Tohei "Koichi Tohei")'s [Ki Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ki_Society "Ki Society") centers almost exclusively around the study of the [empirical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical "Empirical") (albeit subjective) experience of *ki*, with students' proficiency in aikido techniques and *ki* development ranked separately.[\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-29)

## Training

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Aikido\&action=edit\&section=8 "Edit section: Training")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/62/A%C3%AFkido-shihoo_nage.jpg/200px-A%C3%AFkido-shihoo_nage.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:A%C3%AFkido-shihoo_nage.jpg)

Training ([稽古](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%A8%BD%E5%8F%A4 "ja:稽古") Keiko). *[Ukemi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uke_\(martial_arts\) "Uke (martial arts)")* (受け身) is very important for safe practice.

In aikido, as in virtually all Japanese [martial arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martial_arts "Martial arts"), there are both physical and mental aspects of training. The physical training in aikido is diverse, covering both general physical fitness and [conditioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exercise "Exercise"), as well as specific techniques.[\[30\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-AFL-30) Because a substantial portion of any aikido curriculum consists of [throws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Throw_\(grappling\) "Throw (grappling)"), beginners learn how to safely fall or roll.[\[30\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-AFL-30) The specific techniques for attack include both strikes and grabs; the techniques for defense consist of throws and [pins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinning_hold "Pinning hold"). After basic techniques are learned, students study freestyle defense against multiple opponents, and techniques with weapons.

### Fitness

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Aikido\&action=edit\&section=9 "Edit section: Fitness")]

Physical training goals pursued in conjunction with aikido include controlled [relaxation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relaxation_technique "Relaxation technique"), correct movement of joints such as hips and shoulders, [flexibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flexibility_\(anatomy\) "Flexibility (anatomy)"), and [endurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endurance "Endurance"), with less emphasis on [strength training](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strength_training "Strength training"). In aikido, pushing or extending movements are much more common than pulling or contracting movements. This distinction can be applied to general fitness goals for the aikido practitioner.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-ADS-4) In aikido, specific muscles or muscle groups are not isolated and worked to improve tone, mass, or power. Aikido-related training emphasizes the use of coordinated whole-body movement and balance similar to [yoga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoga_as_exercise "Yoga as exercise") or [pilates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilates "Pilates"). For example, many dōjōs begin each class with warm-up exercises (準備体操, *junbi taisō*), which may include [stretching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stretching "Stretching") and *[ukemi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uke_\(martial_arts\) "Uke (martial arts)")* (break falls).[\[31\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-31)

### Roles of *uke* and *tori*

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Aikido\&action=edit\&section=10 "Edit section: Roles of uke and tori")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/41/G_Blaize_Kokiu_Nague_2.JPG/220px-G_Blaize_Kokiu_Nague_2.JPG)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:G_Blaize_Kokiu_Nague_2.JPG)

"Shite" (Nage), in this case, is the person on the right. He is doing Kokyu-Nage (呼吸投げ).\
"Uke", in this case on the left, is doing rolling [Ukemi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukemi "Ukemi") (France, 2002)

Aikido training is based primarily on two partners practicing pre-arranged forms (*[kata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kata "Kata")*) rather than freestyle practice. The basic pattern is for the receiver of the technique (*[uke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uke_\(martial_arts\) "Uke (martial arts)")*) to initiate an attack against the person who applies the technique—the *[tori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tori_\(martial_arts\) "Tori (martial arts)")* (取り), or *shite* (仕手) (depending on aikido style), also referred to as *nage* (投げ) (when applying a throwing technique), who neutralises this attack with an aikido technique.[\[32\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-afl2-32)

Both halves of the technique, that of *uke* and that of *tori*, are considered essential to aikido training.[\[32\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-afl2-32) Both are studying aikido principles of blending and adaptation. *Tori* learns to blend with and control attacking energy, while *uke* learns to become calm and flexible in the disadvantageous, off-balance positions in which *tori* places them. This "receiving" of the technique is called *ukemi*.[\[32\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-afl2-32) *Uke* continuously seeks to regain balance and cover vulnerabilities (e.g., an exposed side), while *tori* uses position and timing to keep *uke* off-balance and vulnerable. In more advanced training, *uke* will sometimes apply reversal techniques (返し技, *kaeshi-waza*) to regain balance and pin or throw *tori*. *Ukemi* (受身) refers to the act of receiving a technique. Good *ukemi* involves attention to the technique, the partner, and the immediate environment—it is considered an active part of the process of learning aikido. The method of falling itself is also important, and is a way for the practitioner to receive an aikido technique safely and minimize risk of injury.

### Initial attacks

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Aikido\&action=edit\&section=11 "Edit section: Initial attacks")]

Aikido techniques are usually a defense against an attack, so students must learn to deliver various types of attacks to be able to practice aikido with a partner. Although attacks are not studied as thoroughly as in striking-based arts, attacks with intent (such as a strong strike or an immobilizing grab) are needed to study correct and effective application of technique.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-ADS-4) Many of the strikes (打ち, *uchi*) of aikido resemble cuts from a [sword](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sword "Sword") or other grasped object, which indicate its origins in techniques intended for [armed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weapon "Weapon") combat.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-ADS-4) Other techniques which explicitly appear to be punches (*[tsuki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsuki "Tsuki")*), are practiced as thrusts with a [knife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knife "Knife") or sword. [Kicks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kick "Kick") are generally reserved for upper-level variations; reasons cited include that falls from kicks are especially dangerous, and that kicks (high kicks in particular) were uncommon during the types of combat prevalent in feudal Japan.

Some basic strikes include:

- **Front-of-the-head strike** (正面打ち, *shōmen'uchi*) is a vertical [knifehand strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knifehand_strike "Knifehand strike") to the head. In training, this is usually directed at the forehead or the [crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_\(anatomy\) "Crown (anatomy)") for safety, but more dangerous versions of this attack target the bridge of the nose and the [maxillary sinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxillary_sinus "Maxillary sinus").
- **Side-of-the-head strike** (横面打ち, *yokomen'uchi*) is a diagonal knifehand strike to the side of the head or neck.
- **Chest thrust** (胸突き, *mune-tsuki*) is a [punch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punch_\(strike\) "Punch (strike)") to the [torso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torso "Torso"). Specific targets include the [chest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chest "Chest"), [abdomen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdomen "Abdomen"), and [solar plexus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_plexus "Solar plexus"), sometimes referred to as "middle-level thrust" (中段突き, *chūdan-tsuki*), or "direct thrust" (直突き, *[choku-tsuki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choku-tsuki "Choku-tsuki")*).
- **Face thrust** (顔面突き, *ganmen-tsuki*) is a punch to the [face](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face "Face"), sometimes referred to as "upper-level thrust" (上段突き, *jōdan-tsuki*).

Beginners in particular often practice techniques from grabs, both because they are safer and because it is easier to feel the energy and the direction of the movement of force of a hold than it is for a strike. Some grabs are historically derived from being held while trying to draw a [weapon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katana "Katana"), whereupon a technique could then be used to free oneself and immobilize or strike the attacker while they are grabbing the defender.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-ADS-4) Here are examples of some basic grabs:

- **Single-hand grab** (片手取り, *katate-dori*), when one hand grabs one wrist.
- **Both-hands grab** (諸手取り, *morote-dori*), when both hands grab one wrist; sometimes referred to as "single hand double-handed grab" (片手両手取り, *katateryōte-dori*)
- **Both-hands grab** (両手取り, *ryōte-dori*), when both hands grab both wrists; sometimes referred to as "double single-handed grab" (両片手取り, *ryōkatate-dori*).
- **Shoulder grab** (肩取り, *kata-dori*) when one shoulder is grabbed.
- **Both-shoulders-grab** (両肩取り, *ryōkata-dori*), when both shoulders are grabbed. It is sometimes combined with an overhead strike as **shoulder grab face strike** (肩取り面打ち, *kata-dori men-uchi*).
- **Chest grab** (胸取り, *mune-dori or muna-dori*), when the lapel is grabbed; sometimes referred to as "collar grab" (襟取り, *eri-dori*).

### Basic techniques

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Aikido\&action=edit\&section=12 "Edit section: Basic techniques")]

Main article: [Aikido techniques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aikido_techniques "Aikido techniques")

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e0/Aikido_ikkyo.svg/220px-Aikido_ikkyo.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Aikido_ikkyo.svg)

Diagram of *ikkyō*, or "first technique"

Here is a sample of the basic or widely practiced throws and pins. Many of the techniques derive from Daitō-ryū Aiki-jūjutsu, but some others were invented by Morihei Ueshiba. The precise terminology for some may vary between organisations and styles; the terms presented are the terms used by the Aikikai Foundation. Although the names of the first five techniques listed are in succession, they are not universally taught in numeric order.[\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-33)

- **First technique** (一教 ([教](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%95%99 "wikt:教")), *ikkyō*), a control technique using one hand on the elbow and one hand near the wrist which leverages *uke* to the ground.[\[34\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-34) This grip applies pressure into the [ulnar nerve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulnar_nerve "Ulnar nerve") at the wrist.
- **Second technique** (二教, *nikyō*) is a [pronating wristlock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kote_mawashi "Kote mawashi") that torques the arm and applies painful nerve pressure. (There is an [adductive wristlock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wristlock#Adductive_wristlock "Wristlock") or Z-lock in the *ura* version.)
- **Third technique** (三教, *sankyō*) is a [rotational wristlock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wristlock#Rotational_wristlock "Wristlock") that directs upward-spiraling tension throughout the arm, elbow and shoulder.
- **Fourth technique** (四教, *yonkyō*) is a shoulder control technique similar to *ikkyō*, but with both hands gripping the forearm. The knuckles (from the palm side) are applied to the recipient's [radial nerve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radial_nerve "Radial nerve") against the [periosteum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periosteum "Periosteum") of the forearm bone.[\[35\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-35)
- **Fifth technique** (五教, *gokyō*)is a technique that is visually similar to *ikkyō*, but with an inverted grip of the wrist, [medial rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medial_rotation "Medial rotation") of the arm and shoulder, and downward pressure on the elbow. Common in [knife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knife "Knife") and other weapon take-aways.
- '***Four-direction throw'* (四方投げ, *shihōnage*)** is a throw during which *uke*'s hand is folded back past the shoulder, locking the shoulder joint.
- **Forearm return** (小手返し, *kotegaeshi*) is a [supinating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kote_gaeshi "Kote gaeshi") wristlock-throw that stretches the [extensor digitorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extensor_digitorum "Extensor digitorum").
- **Breath throw** (呼吸投げ, *kokyūnage*) is a loosely used umbrella term for various types of mechanically unrelated techniques; *kokyūnage* generally do not use joint locks like other techniques.[\[36\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-36)
- **Entering throw** (入身投げ, *iriminage*), throws in which *tori* moves through the space occupied by *uke*. The classic form superficially resembles a "clothesline" technique.
- **Heaven-and-earth throw** (天地投げ, *tenchinage*), a throw in which, beginning with *ryōte-dori*, moving forward, *tori* sweeps one hand low ("earth") and the other high ("heaven"), which unbalances *uke* so that they easily topple over.
- **Hip throw** (腰投げ, *koshinage*), aikido's version of the [hip throw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Throw_\(grappling\)#Hip_throws "Throw (grappling)"); *tori* drops their hips lower than those of *uke*, then flips *uke* over the resultant [fulcrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lever "Lever").
- **Figure-ten throw** (十字投げ, *jūjinage*), a throw that locks the arms against each other (the [kanji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanji "Kanji") for "10" is a cross-shape: 十).[\[37\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-37)
- **Rotary throw** (回転投げ, *kaitennage*) is a throw in which *tori* sweeps *uke*'s arm back until it locks the shoulder joint, then uses forward pressure to throw them.[\[38\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-38)

### Implementations

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Aikido\&action=edit\&section=13 "Edit section: Implementations")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/98/Aikido_ikkyo_omote_ura.png/220px-Aikido_ikkyo_omote_ura.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Aikido_ikkyo_omote_ura.png)

Diagram showing two versions of the *ikkyō* technique: one moving forward (the *omote* version) and one moving backward (the *ura* version)

Aikido makes use of body movement (*tai sabaki* or 体捌き) to blend the movement of *tori* with the movement of *uke*. For example, an "entering" (*[irimi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irimi "Irimi")*) technique consists of movements inward towards *uke*, while a "turning" (転換, *tenkan*) technique uses a pivoting motion.[\[39\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-39) Additionally, an "inside" (内, *uchi*) technique takes place in front of *uke*, whereas an "outside" (外, *soto*) technique takes place to their side; a "front" (表, *omote*) technique is applied with motion to the front of *uke*, and a "rear" (裏, *ura*) version is applied with motion towards the rear of *uke*, usually by incorporating a turning or pivoting motion. Finally, most techniques can be performed while in a seated posture (*[seiza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seiza "Seiza")*). Techniques where both *uke* and *tori* are standing are called *tachi-waza*, techniques where both start off in *seiza* are called *suwari-waza*, and techniques performed with *uke* standing and *tori* sitting are called *hanmi handachi* (半身半立).[\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-dynamic-40) From these few basic techniques, there are numerous of possible implementations. For example, *ikkyō* can be applied to an opponent moving forward with a strike (perhaps with an *ura* type of movement to redirect the incoming force), or to an opponent who has already struck and is now moving back to reestablish distance (perhaps an *omote-waza* version). Specific aikido *kata* are typically referred to with the formula "attack-technique(-modifier)"; *katate-dori ikkyō*, for example, refers to any *ikkyō* technique executed when *uke* is holding one wrist. This could be further specified as *katate-dori ikkyō omote* (referring to any forward-moving *ikkyō* technique from that grab).

*[Atemi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atemi "Atemi")* (当て身) are strikes (or [feints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feint "Feint")) employed during an aikido technique. Some view *atemi* as attacks against "[vital points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_point "Pressure point")" meant to cause damage in and of themselves. For instance, [Gozo Shioda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gozo_Shioda "Gozo Shioda") described using *atemi* in a brawl to quickly down a gang's leader.[\[28\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Shugyo-28) Others consider *atemi*, especially to the face, to be methods of distraction meant to enable other techniques; a strike, even if it is blocked, can startle the target and break their concentration. Additionally, the target may also become unbalanced while attempting to avoid a strike (by jerking the head back, for example) which may allow for an easier throw.[\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-dynamic-40) Many sayings about *atemi* are attributed to Morihei Ueshiba, who considered them an essential element of technique.[\[41\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-41)

### Weapons

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Aikido\&action=edit\&section=14 "Edit section: Weapons")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/88/PRehse002-cropped.jpg/220px-PRehse002-cropped.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:PRehse002-cropped.jpg)

Disarming an attacker using a "sword taking" (太刀取り, *tachi-dori*) technique

Weapons training in aikido traditionally includes the short staff (*[jō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C5%8D "Jō")*) (these techniques closely resemble the use of the bayonet, or [Jūkendō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C5%ABkend%C5%8D "Jūkendō")), the wooden sword (*[bokken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bokken "Bokken")*), and the knife (*[tantō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tant%C5%8D "Tantō")*).[\[42\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-42) Some schools incorporate firearm-disarming techniques, where either weapon-taking and/or weapon-retention may be taught. Some schools, such as the [Iwama style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwama_style "Iwama style") of [Morihiro Saito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morihiro_Saito "Morihiro Saito"), usually spend substantial time practicing with both *bokken* and *jō*, under the names of *[aiki-ken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aiki-ken "Aiki-ken")*, and *[aiki-jō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aiki-j%C5%8D "Aiki-jō")*, respectively. The founder developed many of the empty-handed techniques from traditional sword, spear and bayonet movements. Consequently, the practice of the weapons arts gives insight into the origin of techniques and movements, and reinforces the concepts of distance, timing, foot movement, presence and connectedness with one's training partner(s).[\[43\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-SOS-43)

### Multiple attackers and *randori*

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Aikido\&action=edit\&section=15 "Edit section: Multiple attackers and randori")]

One feature of aikido is training to defend against multiple attackers, often called *taninzudori*, or *taninzugake*. Freestyle practice with multiple attackers called *[randori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randori "Randori")* (乱取) is a key part of most curricula and is required for the higher-level ranks.[\[44\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-best-44) *Randori* exercises a person's ability to intuitively perform techniques in an unstructured environment.[\[44\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-best-44) Strategic choice of techniques, based on how they reposition the student relative to other attackers, is important in *randori* training. For instance, an *ura* technique might be used to neutralise the current attacker while turning to face attackers approaching from behind.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-ADS-4)

In [Shodokan Aikido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shodokan_Aikido "Shodokan Aikido"), *randori* differs in that it is not performed with multiple persons with defined roles of defender and attacker, but between two people, where both participants attack, defend, and counter at will. In this respect it resembles judo *randori*.[\[26\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-ACE-26)[\[45\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-45)

### Injuries

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Aikido\&action=edit\&section=16 "Edit section: Injuries")]

In applying a technique during training, it is the responsibility of *tori* to prevent injury to *uke* by employing a speed and force of application that is appropriate with their partner's proficiency in *ukemi*.[\[32\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-afl2-32) When injuries (especially to the joints) occur, they are often the result of a *tori* misjudging the ability of *uke* to receive the throw or pin.[\[46\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Aikinjuries-46)[\[47\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Aikinjuries2-47) A study of injuries in the martial arts showed that the type of injuries varied considerably from one art to the other.[\[48\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-injurycomparison-48) Soft tissue injuries are one of the most common types of injuries found within aikido,[\[48\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-injurycomparison-48) as well as joint strain and stubbed fingers and toes.[\[47\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Aikinjuries2-47) Several deaths from head-and-neck injuries, caused by aggressive *shihōnage* in a *[senpai/kōhai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senpai_and_k%C5%8Dhai "Senpai and kōhai")* hazing context, have been reported.[\[46\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Aikinjuries-46)

### Mental training

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Aikido\&action=edit\&section=17 "Edit section: Mental training")]

Aikido training is mental as well as physical, emphasizing the ability to relax the mind and body even under the stress of dangerous situations.[\[49\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-ZENMA-49) This is necessary to enable the practitioner to perform the 'enter-and-blend' movements that underlie aikido techniques, wherein an attack is met with confidence and directness.[\[50\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-AFL_1-9-50) Morihei Ueshiba once remarked that one "must be willing to receive 99% of an opponent's attack and stare death in the face" in order to execute techniques without hesitation.[\[51\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-AOP-51) As a martial art concerned not only with fighting proficiency but with the betterment of daily life, this mental aspect is of key importance to aikido practitioners.[\[52\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-NewWarrior-52)

Aikido practitioners (commonly called *aikidōka* outside Japan) generally progress by promotion through a series of "grades" (*[kyū](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ky%C5%AB "Kyū")*), followed by a series of "degrees" (*[dan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_\(rank\) "Dan (rank)")*), pursuant to formal testing procedures. Some aikido organizations use belts to distinguish practitioners' grades, often simply white and [black belts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_belt_\(martial_arts\) "Black belt (martial arts)") to distinguish *kyu* and *dan* grades, although some use various belt colors. Testing requirements vary, so a particular rank in one organization is not comparable or interchangeable with the rank of another.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-ADS-4) Some dōjōs have an age requirement before students can take the *dan* rank exam.[\[53\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-53)

The uniform worn for practicing aikido (*[aikidōgi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aikidogi "Aikidogi")*) is similar to the training uniform (*[keikogi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keikogi "Keikogi")*) used in most other modern martial arts; simple trousers and a wraparound jacket, usually white. Both thick ("[judo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judo "Judo")-style"), and thin ("[karate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karate "Karate")-style") cotton tops are used.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-ADS-4) Aikido-specific tops are available with shorter sleeves which reach to just below the elbow.[\[54\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-seido-54)

Most aikido systems add a pair of wide pleated black or [indigo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigo "Indigo") trousers known as *[hakama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hakama "Hakama")* (used also in [Naginatajutsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naginatajutsu "Naginatajutsu"), [kendo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kendo "Kendo"), and [iaido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iaido "Iaido")). In many schools, the wearing of *hakama* is reserved for practitioners with *dan* ranks or for instructors, while others allow all practitioners to wear a *hakama* regardless of rank.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-ADS-4)

## Aikido styles

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Aikido\&action=edit\&section=19 "Edit section: Aikido styles")]

[Aikido styles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aikido_styles "Aikido styles") vary in their intention, due to its holistic nature. The most common differences noted in aikido, when observed externally, relate to the intensity and realism of training. [Stanley Pranin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Pranin "Stanley Pranin") has observed that some criticism may stem from weak attacks from *uke*, allowing for a conditioned response from *tori*, resulting in underdevelopment of the skills needed for the safe and effective practice of both partners.[\[55\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Pranin-aikido_today-55)

To counteract that some styles allow students to become less compliant over time, however in keeping with the core philosophies, that is after having demonstrated proficiency in being able to protect themselves and their training partners. [Shodokan Aikido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shodokan_Aikido "Shodokan Aikido") addresses the issue by practicing in a competitive format.[\[26\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-ACE-26) Such adaptations are debated between styles, with some maintaining that there is no need to adjust their methods because either the criticisms are unjustified, or that they are not training for self-defense or combat effectiveness, but spiritual, fitness or other reasons.[\[56\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-56) Reasons for the difference and diversity of teachings, intention, and forms of aikido can be traced to the shift in training focus after the end of Ueshiba's seclusion in Iwama from 1942 to the mid-1950s, as he increasingly emphasized the spiritual and philosophical aspects of aikido. As a result, strikes to vital points by *tori*, entering (*irimi*) and initiation of techniques by *tori*, the distinction between *omote* (front side) and *ura* (back side) techniques, and the use of weapons, were all de-emphasized or eliminated from practice.[\[57\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-57)

Conversely, some styles of aikido place less importance on the spiritual practices which were emphasized by Ueshiba. According to Minoru Shibata of *Aikido Journal*:

> O-Sensei's aikido was not a continuation and extension of the old and has a distinct discontinuity with past martial and philosophical concepts.[\[58\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Shibata-Dilemma-58)

In other words, aikido practitioners who focus on aikido's roots in traditional *[jujutsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jujutsu "Jujutsu")* or *[kenjutsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenjutsu "Kenjutsu")* are said to be diverging from what Ueshiba taught, as some critics urge practitioners:

> \[Ueshiba's] transcendence to the spiritual and universal reality were the fundamentals of the paradigm that he demonstrated.[\[58\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Shibata-Dilemma-58)
