# Atomic number

Number of protons found in the nucleus of an atom

Not to be confused with [Atomic mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_mass "Atomic mass"), [Mass number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_number "Mass number"), or [Atomic weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_weight "Atomic weight").

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f4/Atomic_number_depiction.svg/300px-Atomic_number_depiction.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Atomic_number_depiction.svg)

An explanation of the superscripts and subscripts seen in atomic number notation. Atomic number is the number of protons, and therefore also the total positive charge, in the atomic nucleus.

The **atomic number** or **nuclear charge number** (symbol ***Z***) of a [chemical element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_element "Chemical element") is the [charge number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_number "Charge number") of its [atomic nucleus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus "Atomic nucleus"). For ordinary nuclei composed of [protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton "Proton") and [neutrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron "Neutron"), this is equal to the **proton number** (***n*p**) or the number of protons found in the nucleus of every [atom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom "Atom") of that element. The atomic number can be used to uniquely identify ordinary [chemical elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_elements "Chemical elements"). In an ordinary [uncharged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charge "Electric charge") atom, the atomic number is also equal to the number of [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron "Electron").

For an ordinary atom which contains protons, neutrons and [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron "Electron"), the sum of the atomic number *Z* and the [neutron number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_number "Neutron number") *N* gives the atom's [atomic mass number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_mass_number "Atomic mass number") *A*. Since protons and neutrons have approximately the same mass (and the mass of the electrons is negligible for many purposes) and the [mass defect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binding_energy#Mass_change "Binding energy") of the [nucleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleon "Nucleon") binding is always small compared to the nucleon mass, the [atomic mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_mass "Atomic mass") of any atom, when expressed in [daltons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalton_\(unit\) "Dalton (unit)") (making a quantity called the "[relative isotopic mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_mass "Atomic mass")"), is within 1% of the whole number *A*.

Atoms with the same atomic number but different neutron numbers, and hence different mass numbers, are known as [isotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotope "Isotope"). A little more than three-quarters of naturally occurring elements exist as a mixture of isotopes (see [monoisotopic elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoisotopic_element "Monoisotopic element")), and the average isotopic mass of an isotopic mixture for an element (called the relative atomic mass) in a defined environment on Earth determines the element's standard [atomic weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_weight "Atomic weight"). Historically, it was these atomic weights of elements (in comparison to hydrogen) that were the quantities measurable by chemists in the 19th century.

The conventional symbol *Z* comes from the German word *Zahl* 'number', which, before the modern synthesis of ideas from chemistry and physics, merely denoted an element's numerical place in the [periodic table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_table "Periodic table"), whose order was then approximately, but not completely, consistent with the order of the elements by atomic weights. Only after 1915, with the suggestion and evidence that this *Z* number was also the nuclear charge and a physical characteristic of atoms, did the word *Atomzahl* (and its English equivalent *atomic number*) come into common use in this context.

The rules above do not always apply to [exotic atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exotic_atom "Exotic atom") which contain short-lived elementary particles other than protons, neutrons and electrons.

## History

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Atomic_number\&action=edit\&section=1 "Edit section: History")]

In the 19th century, the term "atomic number" typically meant the number of atoms in a given volume.[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-1)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-2) Modern chemists prefer to use the concept of [molar concentration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_concentration "Molar concentration").

In 1913, [Antonius van den Broek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonius_van_den_Broek "Antonius van den Broek") proposed that the [electric charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charge "Electric charge") of an atomic nucleus, expressed as a multiplier of the [elementary charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_charge "Elementary charge"), was equal to the element's sequential position on the [periodic table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_table "Periodic table"). [Ernest Rutherford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Rutherford "Ernest Rutherford"), in various articles in which he discussed van den Broek's idea, used the term "atomic number" to refer to an element's position on the periodic table.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-3)[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-4) No writer before Rutherford is known to have used the term "atomic number" in this way, so it was probably he who established this definition.[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-5)[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-6)

After Rutherford deduced the existence of the proton in 1920, "atomic number" customarily referred to the proton number of an atom. In 1921, the German Atomic Weight Commission based its new periodic table on the nuclear charge number and in 1923 the International Committee on Chemical Elements followed suit.[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-7)

### The periodic table and a natural number for each element

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Atomic_number\&action=edit\&section=2 "Edit section: The periodic table and a natural number for each element")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c8/DIMendeleevCab.jpg/170px-DIMendeleevCab.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:DIMendeleevCab.jpg)

Russian chemist [Dmitri Mendeleev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dmitri_Mendeleev "Dmitri Mendeleev"), creator of the periodic table.

The [periodic table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_table "Periodic table") of elements creates an ordering of the elements, and so they can be numbered in order.[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-PaisInward-8): 222  [Dmitri Mendeleev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dmitri_Mendeleev "Dmitri Mendeleev") arranged his first periodic tables (first published on March 6, 1869) in order of [atomic weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_weight "Atomic weight") ("Atomgewicht").[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-dm1869-9) However, in consideration of the elements' observed chemical properties, he changed the order slightly and placed [tellurium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tellurium "Tellurium") (atomic weight 127.6) ahead of [iodine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iodine "Iodine") (atomic weight 126.9).[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-dm1869-9)[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-10) This placement is consistent with the modern practice of ordering the elements by proton number, *Z*, but that number was not known or suspected at the time.

A simple numbering based on atomic weight position was never entirely satisfactory. In addition to the case of iodine and tellurium, several other pairs of elements (such as [argon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argon "Argon") and [potassium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium "Potassium"), [cobalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobalt "Cobalt") and [nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel "Nickel")) were later shown to have nearly identical or reversed atomic weights, thus requiring their placement in the periodic table to be determined by their chemical properties.[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-PaisInward-8): 222  However the gradual identification of more and more chemically similar [lanthanide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanthanide "Lanthanide") elements, whose atomic number was not obvious, led to inconsistency and uncertainty in the periodic numbering of elements at least from [lutetium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lutetium "Lutetium") (element 71) onward ([hafnium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafnium "Hafnium") was not known at this time).

### The Rutherford-Bohr model and van den Broek

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Atomic_number\&action=edit\&section=3 "Edit section: The Rutherford-Bohr model and van den Broek")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/93/Bohr_atom_model.svg/300px-Bohr_atom_model.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bohr_atom_model.svg)

The Rutherford–Bohr model of the [hydrogen atom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_atom "Hydrogen atom") (*Z* = 1) or a hydrogen-like ion (*Z* > 1). In this model, it is an essential feature that the photon energy (or frequency) of the electromagnetic radiation emitted (shown) when an electron jumps from one orbital to another be proportional to the mathematical square of atomic charge (*Z2*). Experimental measurements by [Henry Moseley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Moseley "Henry Moseley") of this radiation for many elements (from *Z* = 13 to 92) showed the results as predicted by Bohr. Both the concept of atomic number and the Bohr model were thereby given scientific credence.

In 1911, [Ernest Rutherford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Rutherford "Ernest Rutherford") gave a [model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rutherford_model "Rutherford model") of the atom in which a central nucleus held most of the atom's mass and a positive charge which, in units of the electron's charge, was to be approximately equal to half of the atom's atomic weight, expressed in numbers of hydrogen atoms. This central charge would thus be approximately half the atomic weight (though it was almost 25% different from the atomic number of gold (*Z* = 79, *A* = 197), the single element from which Rutherford made his guess). Nevertheless, in spite of Rutherford's estimation that gold had a central charge of about 100 (but was element *Z* = 79 on the periodic table), a month after Rutherford's paper appeared, [Antonius van den Broek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonius_van_den_Broek "Antonius van den Broek") first formally suggested that the central charge and number of electrons in an atom were *exactly* equal to its place in the periodic table (also known as element number, atomic number, and symbolized *Z*). This eventually proved to be the case.

### Moseley's 1913 experiment

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Atomic_number\&action=edit\&section=4 "Edit section: Moseley's 1913 experiment")]

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/d/dd/Henry_Moseley.jpg/170px-Henry_Moseley.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Henry_Moseley.jpg)

[Henry Moseley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Moseley "Henry Moseley") in his lab.

The experimental position improved dramatically after research by [Henry Moseley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Moseley "Henry Moseley") in 1913.[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-11) Moseley, after discussions with Bohr who was at the same lab (and who had used Van den Broek's hypothesis in his [Bohr model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohr_model "Bohr model") of the atom), decided to test Van den Broek's and Bohr's hypothesis directly, by seeing if [spectral lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_line "Spectral line") emitted from excited atoms fitted the Bohr theory's postulation that the frequency of the spectral lines be proportional to the square of *Z*.

To do this, Moseley measured the wavelengths of the innermost photon transitions (K and L lines) produced by the elements from [aluminium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium "Aluminium") (*Z* = 13) to gold (*Z* = 79) used as a series of movable anodic targets inside an [x-ray tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_tube "X-ray tube").[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-12) The square root of the frequency of these photons (x-rays) increased from one target to the next in an arithmetic progression. This led to the conclusion ([Moseley's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moseley%27s_law "Moseley's law")) that the atomic number does closely correspond (with an offset of one unit for K-lines, in Moseley's work) to the calculated [electric charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charge "Electric charge") of the nucleus, i.e. the element number *Z*. Among other things, Moseley demonstrated that the [lanthanide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanthanide "Lanthanide") series (from [lanthanum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanthanum "Lanthanum") to [lutetium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lutetium "Lutetium") inclusive) must have 15 members—no fewer and no more—which was far from obvious from known chemistry at that time.

### Missing elements

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Atomic_number\&action=edit\&section=5 "Edit section: Missing elements")]

After Moseley's death in 1915, the atomic numbers of all known elements from hydrogen to uranium (*Z* = 92) were examined by his method. There were seven elements (with *Z* < 92) which were not found and therefore identified as still undiscovered, corresponding to atomic numbers 43, 61, 72, 75, 85, 87 and 91.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-13) From 1918 to 1947, all seven of these missing elements were discovered.[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-14) By this time, the first four [transuranium elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transuranium_element "Transuranium element") had also been discovered, so that the periodic table was complete with no gaps as far as [curium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curium "Curium") (*Z* = 96).

### The proton and the idea of nuclear electrons

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Atomic_number\&action=edit\&section=6 "Edit section: The proton and the idea of nuclear electrons")]

In 1915, the reason for nuclear charge being quantized in units of *Z*, which were now recognized to be the same as the element number, was not understood. An old idea called [Prout's hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prout%27s_hypothesis "Prout's hypothesis") had postulated that the elements were all made of residues (or "protyles") of the lightest element hydrogen, which in the Bohr-Rutherford model had a single electron and a nuclear charge of one. However, as early as 1907, Rutherford and [Thomas Royds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Royds "Thomas Royds") had shown that alpha particles, which had a charge of +2, were the nuclei of helium atoms, which had a mass four times that of hydrogen, not two times. If Prout's hypothesis were true, something had to be neutralizing some of the charge of the hydrogen nuclei present in the nuclei of heavier atoms.

In 1917, Rutherford succeeded in generating hydrogen nuclei from a [nuclear reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_reaction "Nuclear reaction") between alpha particles and nitrogen gas,[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-15) and believed he had proven Prout's law. He called the new heavy nuclear particles protons in 1920 (alternate names being proutons and protyles). It had been immediately apparent from the work of Moseley that the nuclei of heavy atoms have more than twice as much mass as would be expected from their being made of [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen "Hydrogen") nuclei, and thus there was required a hypothesis for the neutralization of the extra [protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protons "Protons") presumed present in all heavy nuclei. A helium nucleus was presumed to have four protons plus two "nuclear electrons" (electrons bound inside the nucleus) to cancel two charges. At the other end of the periodic table, a nucleus of gold with a mass 197 times that of hydrogen was thought to contain 118 nuclear electrons in the nucleus to give it a residual charge of +79, consistent with its atomic number.

### Discovery of the neutron makes *Z* the proton number

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Atomic_number\&action=edit\&section=7 "Edit section: Discovery of the neutron makes Z the proton number")]

All consideration of nuclear electrons ended with [James Chadwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Chadwick "James Chadwick")'s [discovery of the neutron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_the_neutron "Discovery of the neutron") in 1932. An atom of [gold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold "Gold") now was seen as containing 118 neutrons rather than 118 nuclear electrons, and its positive nuclear charge now was realized to come entirely from a content of 79 protons. Since Moseley had previously shown that the atomic number *Z* of an element equals this positive charge, it was now clear that *Z* is identical to the number of protons of its nuclei.

## Chemical properties

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Atomic_number\&action=edit\&section=8 "Edit section: Chemical properties")]

Each element has a specific set of chemical properties as a consequence of the number of electrons present in the neutral atom, which is *Z* (the atomic number). The [configuration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_configuration "Electron configuration") of these electrons follows from the principles of [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics"). The number of electrons in each element's [electron shells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_shell "Electron shell"), particularly the outermost [valence shell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_shell "Valence shell"), is the primary factor in determining its [chemical bonding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_bonding "Chemical bonding") behavior. Hence, it is the atomic number alone that determines the chemical properties of an element; and it is for this reason that an element can be defined as consisting of *any* mixture of atoms with a given atomic number.

## New elements

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Atomic_number\&action=edit\&section=9 "Edit section: New elements")]

The quest for new elements is usually described using atomic numbers. As of 2025, all elements with atomic numbers 1 to 118 [have been observed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_chemical_elements "Discovery of chemical elements"). The most recent element discovered was number 117 ([tennessine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tennessine "Tennessine")) in 2009. Synthesis of new elements is accomplished by bombarding target atoms of heavy elements with ions, such that the sum of the atomic numbers of the target and ion elements equals the atomic number of the element being created. In general, the [half-life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-life "Half-life") of a [nuclide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclide "Nuclide") becomes shorter as atomic number increases,\[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] though [undiscovered nuclides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_periodic_table "Extended periodic table") with certain "[magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_number_\(physics\) "Magic number (physics)")" numbers of protons and neutrons may have relatively longer half-lives and comprise an [island of stability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Island_of_stability "Island of stability").

A hypothetical element composed only of neutrons, [neutronium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutronium "Neutronium"), has also been proposed and would have atomic number 0,[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Antropoff_1926-16) but has never been observed.
