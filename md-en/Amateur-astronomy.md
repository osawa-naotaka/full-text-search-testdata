# Amateur astronomy

Hobby of watching the sky and stars

"Amateur astronomer", "Popular astronomy", "Skygazing", and "Stargazing" redirect here. For the magazine, see [The Amateur Astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Amateur_Astronomer "The Amateur Astronomer"). For Buddhist practice, see [Sky gazing (Dzogchen)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_gazing_\(Dzogchen\) "Sky gazing (Dzogchen)"). For other uses, see [Stargazing (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stargazing_\(disambiguation\) "Stargazing (disambiguation)") and [Popular Astronomy (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_Astronomy_\(disambiguation\) "Popular Astronomy (disambiguation)").

**Amateur astronomy** is a [hobby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hobby "Hobby") where participants enjoy observing or imaging [celestial objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_objects "Celestial objects") in the [sky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky "Sky") using the [unaided eye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naked_eye "Naked eye"), [binoculars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binoculars "Binoculars"), or [telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescope "Telescope"). Even though scientific research may not be their primary goal, some amateur astronomers make contributions in doing [citizen science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizen_science "Citizen science"), such as by monitoring [variable stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variable_stars "Variable stars"),[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-AAVSO-1) [double stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_stars "Double stars"),[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Heintz1978-2) [sunspots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunspot "Sunspot"),[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-3) or [occultations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occultation "Occultation") of [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star "Star") by the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon "Moon")[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Iota2017-4) or [asteroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid "Asteroid"),[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Iota2017-4) or by discovering [transient astronomical events](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transient_astronomical_event "Transient astronomical event"), such as [comets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comet "Comet"),[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Clay1981-5) galactic [novae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nova "Nova")[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Marsden1988-6) or [supernovae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernova "Supernova") in other [galaxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy "Galaxy").[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Zuckerman1996-7)

Amateur astronomers do not use the field of [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy") as their primary source of income or support, and usually have no professional degree in astrophysics or advanced academic training in the subject. Most amateurs are hobbyists, while others have a high degree of experience in astronomy and may often assist and work alongside professional astronomers.[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Skytelescope-8) Many astronomers have studied the sky throughout history in an amateur framework; however, since the beginning of the twentieth century, professional astronomy has become an activity clearly distinguished from amateur astronomy and associated activities.[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Meadows1988-9)

Amateur astronomers typically view the sky at night, when most celestial objects and astronomical events are visible, but others observe during the daytime by viewing the Sun and [solar eclipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_eclipse "Solar eclipse"). Some just look at the sky using nothing more than their eyes or binoculars, but more dedicated amateurs often use portable [telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_telescope "Optical telescope") or telescopes situated in their private or club [observatories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observatory "Observatory"). Amateurs also join amateur [astronomical societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_society "Astronomical society"), which can advise, educate or guide them towards ways of finding and observing celestial objects. They also promote the science of astronomy among the general public.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Motta2006-10)

Collectively, amateur astronomers observe a variety of celestial objects and phenomena. Common targets of amateur astronomers include the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun "Sun"), the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon "Moon"), [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet "Planet"), [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star "Star"), [comets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comet "Comet"), [meteor showers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteor_shower "Meteor shower"), and a variety of [deep sky objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_sky_object "Deep sky object") such as [star clusters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_cluster "Star cluster"), [galaxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy "Galaxy"), and [nebulae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebula "Nebula"). Many amateurs like to specialise in observing particular objects, types of objects, or types of events which interest them. One branch of amateur astronomy, amateur [astrophotography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophotography "Astrophotography"), involves the taking of photos of the night sky. Astrophotography has become more popular with the introduction of far easier to use equipment including, digital cameras, DSLR cameras and relatively sophisticated purpose built high quality [CCD cameras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CCD_camera "CCD camera") and [CMOS cameras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CMOS_camera "CMOS camera").

Most amateur astronomers work at visible [wavelengths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_spectrum "Electromagnetic spectrum"), but a small minority experiment with wavelengths outside the [visible spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_spectrum "Visible spectrum"). An early pioneer of [radio astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_astronomy "Radio astronomy") was [Grote Reber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grote_Reber "Grote Reber"), an amateur astronomer who constructed the first purpose-built [radio telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_telescope "Radio telescope") in the late 1930s to follow up on the discovery of [radio wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_frequency "Radio frequency") emissions from space by [Karl Jansky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Guthe_Jansky "Karl Guthe Jansky").[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-11) Non-visual amateur astronomy includes the use of [infrared filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_filter "Infrared filter") on conventional telescopes, and also the use of [radio telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_telescope "Radio telescope"). Some amateur astronomers use home-made radio telescopes, while others use radio telescopes that were originally built for astronomical research but have since been made available for use by amateurs. The [One-Mile Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-Mile_Telescope "One-Mile Telescope") is one such example.

Amateur astronomers use a range of instruments to study the sky, depending on a combination of their interests and resources. Methods include simply looking at the night sky with the naked eye, using binoculars, and using a variety of [optical telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_telescope "Optical telescope") of varying power and quality, as well as additional sophisticated equipment, such as cameras, to study light from the sky in both the visual and non-visual parts of the spectrum. To further improve studying the visual and non-visual part of the spectrum, amateur astronomers go to rural areas[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-13) to get away from [light pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_pollution "Light pollution"). Commercial telescopes are available, new and used, but it is also common for amateur astronomers to build (or commission the building of) their own custom telescopes. Some people even focus on [amateur telescope making](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur_telescope_making "Amateur telescope making") as their primary interest within the hobby of amateur astronomy.

Although specialized and experienced amateur astronomers tend to acquire more specialized and more powerful equipment over time, relatively simple equipment is often preferred for certain tasks. Binoculars, for instance, although generally of lower power than the majority of telescopes, also tend to provide a wider [field of view](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_of_view "Field of view"), which is preferable for looking at some objects in the night sky. Recent models of iPhones have introduced a "night mode[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-14)" option when taking pictures as well, that allows you to increase exposure, which is a period of time the picture is being taken for. This optimizes focus on light in the frame which is why it is used primarily at night.

Amateur astronomers also use [star charts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_chart "Star chart") that, depending on experience and intentions, may range from simple [planispheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planisphere "Planisphere") through to [star atlases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_atlas "Star atlas") with detailed charts of the entire night sky. A range of astronomy software is also available and used by amateur astronomers, including software that generates maps of the sky, software to assist with astrophotography, observation scheduling software, and software to perform various calculations pertaining to astronomical phenomena.

Amateur astronomers often like to keep records of their observations, which usually takes the form of an observing log. Observing logs typically record details about which objects were observed and when, as well as describing the details that were seen. Sketching is sometimes used within logs, and photographic records of observations have also been used in recent times. The information gathered is used to help studies and interactions between amateur astronomers in yearly gatherings. Although not professional information or credible, it is a way for the hobby lovers to share their new sightings and experiences.

The popularity of imaging among amateurs has led to large numbers of web sites being written by individuals about their images and equipment. Much of the social interaction of amateur astronomy occurs on mailing lists or discussion groups. Discussion group servers host numerous astronomy lists. A great deal of the commerce of amateur astronomy, the buying and selling of equipment, occurs online. Many amateurs use online tools to plan their nightly observing sessions, using tools such as the [Clear Sky Chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clear_Sky_Chart "Clear Sky Chart").

While a number of interesting celestial objects are readily identified by the naked eye, sometimes with the aid of a star chart, many others are so faint or inconspicuous that technical means are necessary to locate them. Although many methods are used in amateur astronomy, most are variations of a few specific techniques.\[*[according to whom?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Words_to_watch#Unsupported_attributions "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Words to watch")*]

### Star hopping

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Amateur_astronomy\&action=edit\&section=4 "Edit section: Star hopping")]

Main article: [Star hopping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_hopping "Star hopping")

**Star hopping** is a method often used by amateur astronomers with low-tech equipment such as binoculars or a manually driven telescope. It involves the use of maps (or memory) to locate known landmark stars, and "hopping" between them, often with the aid of a [finderscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finderscope "Finderscope"). Because of its simplicity, star hopping is a very common method for finding objects that are close to naked-eye stars.

More advanced methods of locating objects in the sky include [telescope mounts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescope_mount "Telescope mount") with *setting circles*, which allow pointing to targets in the sky using [celestial coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_coordinates "Celestial coordinates"), and *GOTO telescopes*, which are fully automated telescopes that are capable of locating objects on demand (having first been calibrated).

### Mobile apps

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Amateur_astronomy\&action=edit\&section=5 "Edit section: Mobile apps")]

The advent of [mobile applications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_app "Mobile app") for use in smartphones has led to the creation of many dedicated apps.[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-15)[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-16) These apps allow any user to easily locate celestial objects of interest by simply pointing the smartphone device in that direction in the sky. These apps make use of the inbuilt hardware in the phone, such as [GPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPS "GPS") location and [gyroscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyroscope "Gyroscope"). Useful information about the pointed object like celestial coordinates, the name of the object, its constellation, etc. are provided for a quick reference. Some paid versions give more information. These apps are gradually getting into regular use during observing, for the alignment process of telescopes.[\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-17)

### Setting circles

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Amateur_astronomy\&action=edit\&section=6 "Edit section: Setting circles")]

Main article: [Setting circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Setting_circles "Setting circles")

**Setting circles** are [angular measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_measurement "Angular measurement") scales that can be placed on the two main [rotation axes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_around_a_fixed_axis "Rotation around a fixed axis") of some telescopes.[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-18)[\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-19) Since the widespread adoption of digital setting circles, any classical engraved setting circle is now specifically identified as an "analog setting circle" (ASC). By knowing the [coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate "Coordinate") of an object (usually given in [equatorial coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_coordinates "Equatorial coordinates")), the telescope user can use the setting circle to align (i.e., point) the telescope in the appropriate direction before looking through its [eyepiece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eyepiece "Eyepiece"). A computerized setting circle is called a "digital setting circle" (DSC). Although digital setting circles can be used to display a telescope's [RA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_ascension "Right ascension") and [Dec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declination "Declination") coordinates, they are not simply a digital read-out of what can be seen on the telescope's analog setting circles. As with go-to telescopes, digital setting circle computers (commercial names include Argo Navis, Sky Commander, and NGC Max) contain databases of tens of thousands of celestial objects and projections of planet positions.

To find a celestial object in a telescope equipped with a DSC computer, one does not need to look up the specific RA and Dec coordinates in a book or other resource, and then adjust the telescope to those numerical readings. Rather, the object is chosen from the electronic database, which causes distance values and arrow markers to appear in the display that indicate the distance and direction to move the telescope. The telescope is moved until the two angular distance values reach zero, indicating that the telescope is properly aligned. When both the RA and Dec axes are thus "zeroed out", the object should be in the eyepiece. Many DSCs, like go-to systems, can also work in conjunction with laptop sky programs.\[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Computerized systems provide the further advantage of computing coordinate precession. Traditional printed sources are subtitled by the [*epoch* year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epoch_\(astronomy\) "Epoch (astronomy)"), which refers to the positions of celestial objects at a given time to the nearest year (e.g., J2005, J2007). Most such printed sources have been updated for intervals of only about every fifty years (e.g., J1900, J1950, J2000). Computerized sources, on the other hand, are able to calculate the right ascension and declination of the "epoch of date" to the exact instant of observation.[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Argo2008-20)

### GoTo telescopes

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Amateur_astronomy\&action=edit\&section=7 "Edit section: GoTo telescopes")]

Main article: [GoTo (telescopes)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GoTo_\(telescopes\) "GoTo (telescopes)")

**GOTO telescopes** have become more popular since the 1980s as technology has improved and prices have been reduced. With these computer-driven telescopes, the user typically enters the name of the item of interest and the mechanics of the telescope point the telescope towards that item automatically. They have several notable advantages for amateur astronomers intent on research. For example, GOTO telescopes tend to be faster for locating items of interest than star hopping, allowing more time for studying of the object. GOTO also allows manufacturers to add equatorial tracking to mechanically simpler alt-azimuth telescope mounts, allowing them to produce an overall less expensive product. GOTO telescopes usually have to be calibrated using alignment stars to provide accurate tracking and positioning. However, several telescope manufacturers have recently developed telescope systems that are calibrated with the use of built-in GPS, decreasing the time it takes to set up a telescope at the start of an observing session.

### Remote-controlled telescopes

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Amateur_astronomy\&action=edit\&section=8 "Edit section: Remote-controlled telescopes")]

With the development of fast internet in the last part of the 20th century along with advances in computer controlled telescope mounts and CCD cameras, "remote telescope" astronomy is now a viable means for amateur astronomers not aligned with major telescope facilities to partake in research and deep sky imaging. This enables anyone to control a telescope a great distance away in a dark location. The observer can image through the telescope using CCD cameras. The digital data collected by the telescope is then transmitted and displayed to the user by means of the Internet. An example of a digital remote telescope operation for public use via the Internet is the [Bareket observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bareket_observatory "Bareket observatory"), and there are telescope farms in New Mexico,[\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-21) Australia and [Atacama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atacama_Desert#Astronomical_observatories "Atacama Desert") in Chile.[\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-22)

## Scientific research

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Amateur_astronomy\&action=edit\&section=10 "Edit section: Scientific research")]

Scientific research is most often not the *main* goal for many amateur astronomers, unlike professional astronomers. Work of scientific merit is possible, however, and many amateurs successfully contribute to the knowledge base of professional astronomers.[\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-23)[\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-24) Astronomy is sometimes promoted as one of the few remaining sciences for which amateurs can still contribute useful data. To recognize this, the [Astronomical Society of the Pacific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_Society_of_the_Pacific "Astronomical Society of the Pacific") annually gives [Amateur Achievement Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur_Achievement_Award_of_Astronomical_Society_of_the_Pacific "Amateur Achievement Award of Astronomical Society of the Pacific") for significant contributions to astronomy by amateurs.[\[25\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-25)

The majority of scientific contributions by amateur astronomers are in the area of data collection. In particular, this applies where large numbers of amateur astronomers with small telescopes are more effective than the relatively small number of large telescopes that are available to professional astronomers. Several organizations, such as the [American Association of Variable Star Observers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Association_of_Variable_Star_Observers "American Association of Variable Star Observers") and the [British Astronomical Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Astronomical_Association "British Astronomical Association"), exist to help coordinate these contributions.

Amateur astronomers often contribute toward activities such as monitoring the changes in brightness of [variable stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variable_star "Variable star") and [supernovae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernova "Supernova"), helping to track [asteroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid "Asteroid"), and observing [occultations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occultation "Occultation") to determine both the shape of asteroids and the shape of the terrain on the apparent edge of the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon "Moon") as seen from Earth. With more advanced equipment, but still cheap in comparison to professional setups, amateur astronomers can measure the light spectrum emitted from astronomical objects, which can yield high-quality scientific data if the measurements are performed with due care. A relatively recent role for amateur astronomers is searching for overlooked phenomena (e.g., [Kreutz Sungrazers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kreutz_Sungrazers "Kreutz Sungrazers")) in the vast libraries of digital images and other data captured by Earth and space based observatories, much of which is available over the Internet.

In the past and present, amateur astronomers have played a major role in discovering new [comets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comet "Comet"). Recently however, funding of projects such as the [Lincoln Near-Earth Asteroid Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LINEAR "LINEAR") and [Near Earth Asteroid Tracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_Earth_Asteroid_Tracking "Near Earth Asteroid Tracking") projects has meant that most comets are now discovered by automated systems long before it is possible for amateurs to see them.

## Societies

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Amateur_astronomy\&action=edit\&section=11 "Edit section: Societies")]

Main article: [List of astronomical societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_astronomical_societies "List of astronomical societies")

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/84/Outreach%2C_one_of_the_most_rewarding_jobs_%284798622790%29.jpg/220px-Outreach%2C_one_of_the_most_rewarding_jobs_%284798622790%29.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Outreach,_one_of_the_most_rewarding_jobs_\(4798622790\).jpg)

Amateur astronomy groups are often involved in outreach to introduce astronomy to the general public

There are a large number of amateur [astronomical societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_society "Astronomical society") around the world, that serve as a meeting point for those interested in amateur astronomy. Members range from active observers with their own equipment to "armchair astronomers" who are simply interested in the topic. Societies range widely in their goals and activities, which may depend on a variety of factors such as geographic spread, local circumstances, size, and membership. For example, a small local society located in dark countryside may focus on practical observing and [star parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_parties "Star parties"), whereas a large one based in a major city might have numerous members but be limited by light pollution and thus hold regular indoor meetings with guest speakers instead. Major national or international societies generally publish their own academic journal or newsletter, and some hold large multi-day meetings akin to a scientific conference or convention. They may also have sections devoted to particular topics, such as [lunar observation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_observation "Lunar observation") or [amateur telescope making](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur_telescope_making "Amateur telescope making").

## Notable contributions by amateur astronomers

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Amateur_astronomy\&action=edit\&section=12 "Edit section: Notable contributions by amateur astronomers")]

Further information: [Category:Amateur astronomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category:Amateur_astronomers "Category:Amateur astronomers")

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/89/Sir_Patrick_Moore_at_the_opening_of_the_South_Downs_Planetarium.jpg/170px-Sir_Patrick_Moore_at_the_opening_of_the_South_Downs_Planetarium.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Sir_Patrick_Moore_at_the_opening_of_the_South_Downs_Planetarium.jpg)

[Sir Patrick Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Patrick_Moore "Sir Patrick Moore") was one of the world's leading popularisers of astronomy.

There have been many significant scientific, technological, and cultural contributions made by amateur astronomers:

- [George Alcock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Alcock "George Alcock"), one of the most successful visual discoverers comets and novae.
- [John E. Bortle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_E._Bortle "John E. Bortle"), authored '"Comet Digest" in *[Sky and Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_and_Telescope "Sky and Telescope")* magazine and the monthly AAVSO circular for the [American Association of Variable Star Observers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Association_of_Variable_Star_Observers "American Association of Variable Star Observers"). Created the [Bortle scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bortle_scale "Bortle scale") to quantify the darkness of the night sky.
- [Robert Burnham Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Burnham_Jr. "Robert Burnham Jr.") (1931–1993), author of the *Celestial Handbook*.
- [Andrew Ainslie Common](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Ainslie_Common "Andrew Ainslie Common") (1841–1903), built his own very large reflecting telescopes and demonstrated that photography could record astronomical features invisible to the human eye.
- [Robert E. Cox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_E._Cox "Robert E. Cox") (1917–1989) who conducted the "Gleanings for ATMs" column in *[Sky & Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_%26_Telescope "Sky & Telescope")* magazine for 21 years.
- [John Dobson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dobson_\(amateur_astronomer\) "John Dobson (amateur astronomer)") (1915–2014), promoter of astronomy whose name is associated with the [Dobsonian telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dobsonian_telescope "Dobsonian telescope").
- [Robert Owen Evans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Evans_\(astronomer\) "Robert Evans (astronomer)") (1937–2022) was an amateur astronomer who currently holds the all-time record for visual discoveries of [supernovae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernova "Supernova").
- [Giuseppe Donatiello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Donatiello "Giuseppe Donatiello"), discovered eleven nearby [dwarf galaxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwarf_galaxy "Dwarf galaxy") in the [Local Volume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Volume "Local Volume") including the first galaxy to be named after its non-professional discoverer. .
- [Will Hay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_Hay "Will Hay"), the famous comedian and actor, who discovered a white spot on Saturn.
- [Walter Scott Houston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Scott_Houston "Walter Scott Houston") (1912–1993) who wrote the "Deep-Sky Wonders" column in *[Sky & Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_%26_Telescope "Sky & Telescope")* magazine for almost 50 years.
- [Albert G. Ingalls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Graham_Ingalls "Albert Graham Ingalls") (1888–1958), editor of *Amateur Telescope Making, Vols. 1–3* and "[The Amateur Scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Amateur_Scientist "The Amateur Scientist")".
- [David H. Levy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_H._Levy "David H. Levy") discovered or co-discovered 22 comets including [Comet Shoemaker-Levy 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comet_Shoemaker-Levy_9 "Comet Shoemaker-Levy 9"), the most for any individual.
- [Sir Patrick Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Moore "Patrick Moore") (1923–2012), presenter of the BBC's long-running *[The Sky at Night](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sky_at_Night "The Sky at Night")* and author of many books on astronomy.
- [Russell W. Porter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_W._Porter "Russell W. Porter") (1871–1949) founded [Stellafane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellafane "Stellafane") and has been referred to as a "founder" of [amateur telescope making](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur_telescope_making "Amateur telescope making").
- [Grote Reber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grote_Reber "Grote Reber") (1911–2002), pioneer of [radio astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_astronomy "Radio astronomy") constructing the first purpose-built [radio telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_telescope "Radio telescope") and conducted the first [sky survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_survey "Astronomical survey") in the radio frequency.
