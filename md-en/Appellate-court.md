An **appellate court**, commonly called a **court of appeal(s)**,[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Garner_Page_232-1) **appeal court**, **court of second instance** or **second instance court**, is any [court of law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_law "Court of law") that is empowered to [hear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hearing_\(law\) "Hearing (law)") a [case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_case "Legal case") upon [appeal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeal "Appeal") from a [trial court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trial_court "Trial court") or other lower [tribunal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribunal "Tribunal"). In much of the world, [court systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judiciary "Judiciary") are divided into at least three levels: the trial court, which initially hears cases and considers factual [evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence_\(law\) "Evidence (law)") and [testimony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testimony "Testimony") relevant to the case; at least one intermediate appellate court; and a [supreme court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_court "Supreme court") (or court of last resort) which primarily reviews the decisions of the intermediate courts, often on a [discretionary basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discretionary_review "Discretionary review"). A particular court system's supreme court is its highest appellate court.[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-2) Appellate courts nationwide can operate under varying rules.[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-3)

Under its [standard of review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_of_review "Standard of review"), an appellate court decides the extent of the deference it would give to the lower court's decision, based on whether the appeal were one of fact or of law. In reviewing an issue of fact, an appellate court ordinarily gives deference to the trial court's findings. It is the duty of trial judges or [juries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jury "Jury") to find facts, view the evidence firsthand, and observe [witness testimony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eyewitness_testimony "Eyewitness testimony"). When reviewing lower decisions on an issue of fact, courts of appeal generally look for clear error. The appellate court reviews issues of law *de novo* (anew, no deference) and may reverse or modify the lower court's decision if the appellate court believes the lower court misapplied the facts or the law. An appellate court may also review the lower judge's discretionary decisions, such as whether the judge properly granted a new trial or disallowed evidence. The lower court's decision is only changed in cases of an "[abuse of discretion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abuse_of_discretion "Abuse of discretion")". This standard tends to be even more deferential than the "clear error" standard.

Before hearing any case, the court must have [jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisdiction "Jurisdiction") to consider the appeal. The authority of appellate courts to review the decisions of lower courts varies widely from one jurisdiction to another. In some areas, the appellate court has limited powers of review. Generally, an appellate court's judgment provides the final directive of the appeals courts as to the matter appealed, setting out with specificity the court's determination that the action appealed from should be affirmed, reversed, remanded or modified.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-4) Depending on the type of case and the decision below, appellate review primarily consists of: an entirely new hearing (a non [trial de novo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trial_de_novo "Trial de novo")); a hearing where the appellate court gives deference to factual findings of the lower court; or review of particular legal rulings made by the lower court (an appeal on the record).

## Bifurcation of civil and criminal appeals

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Appellate_court\&action=edit\&section=1 "Edit section: Bifurcation of civil and criminal appeals")]

While many appellate courts have jurisdiction over all cases decided by lower courts, some systems have appellate courts divided by the type of jurisdiction they exercise. Some jurisdictions have specialized appellate courts, such as the [Texas Court of Criminal Appeals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Court_of_Criminal_Appeals "Texas Court of Criminal Appeals"), which only hears appeals raised in criminal cases, and the [U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Appeals_for_the_Federal_Circuit "United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit"), which has general jurisdiction but derives most of its caseload from patent cases, on one hand, and appeals from the [Court of Federal Claims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Federal_Claims "Court of Federal Claims") on the other. In the United States, Alabama, Tennessee, and Oklahoma also have separate courts of criminal appeals. Texas and [Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma "Oklahoma") have the final determination of criminal cases vested in their respective courts of criminal appeals,[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-5) while Alabama and Tennessee allow decisions of its court of criminal appeals to be finally appealed to the state supreme court.[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-6)[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-7)

### Australia

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Appellate_court\&action=edit\&section=5 "Edit section: Australia")]

Main articles: [High Court of Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Court_of_Australia#Appellate_jurisdiction "High Court of Australia") and [Australian courts of appeals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judiciary_of_Australia#Australian_court_hierarchy "Judiciary of Australia")

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/aa/Supreme_Court_of_Victoria_%285064240836%29.jpg/292px-Supreme_Court_of_Victoria_%285064240836%29.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Supreme_Court_of_Victoria_\(5064240836\).jpg)

The Victorian [Court of Appeal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Victoria "Supreme Court of Victoria")

The [High Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Court_of_Australia "High Court of Australia") has appellate jurisdiction over all other courts. Leave must be granted by the court, before the appeal matter is heard. The High Court is paramount to all federal courts. Further, it has an constitutionally entrenched general power of appeal from the Supreme Courts of the [States and Territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_and_territories_of_Australia "States and territories of Australia").[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-s73-8)[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-KirbyCentenary-9) Appeals to the High Court are by special leave only, which is generally only granted in cases of public importance, matters involving the interpretation of the Commonwealth Constitution, or where the law has been inconsistently applied across the States and Territories.\[19] Therefore, in the vast majority of cases, the appellate divisions of the Supreme Courts of each State and Territory and the Federal Court are the final courts of appeal.[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-10)

### New Zealand

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Appellate_court\&action=edit\&section=6 "Edit section: New Zealand")]

Main article: [Court of Appeal of New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Appeal_of_New_Zealand "Court of Appeal of New Zealand")

The Court of Appeal of New Zealand, located in [Wellington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington "Wellington"), is New Zealand's principal intermediate appellate court.[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-11) In practice, most appeals are resolved at this intermediate appellate level, rather than in the [Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_New_Zealand "Supreme Court of New Zealand").[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-12)

### Philippines

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Appellate_court\&action=edit\&section=7 "Edit section: Philippines")]

Main article: [Court of Appeals of the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Appeals_of_the_Philippines "Court of Appeals of the Philippines")

The Court of Appeals of the Philippines is the principal intermediate appellate court of that country. The Court of Appeals is primarily found in [Manila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manila "Manila"), with three divisions each in [Cebu City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cebu_City "Cebu City") and [Cagayan de Oro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cagayan_de_Oro "Cagayan de Oro"). Other appellate courts include the [Sandiganbayan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandiganbayan "Sandiganbayan") for cases involving graft and corruption, and the [Court of Tax Appeals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Tax_Appeals "Court of Tax Appeals") for cases involving tax. Appeals from all three appellate courts are to the [Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_Philippines "Supreme Court of the Philippines").

### United States

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Appellate_court\&action=edit\&section=10 "Edit section: United States")]

Main articles: [United States Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Supreme_Court "United States Supreme Court") and [United States courts of appeals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_courts_of_appeals "United States courts of appeals")

In the United States, both state and [federal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_courts_of_appeals "United States courts of appeals") appellate courts are usually restricted to examining whether the lower court made the correct legal determinations, rather than hearing direct evidence and determining what the facts of the case were.[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-13) Furthermore, U.S. appellate courts are usually restricted to hearing appeals based on matters that were originally brought up before the trial court. Hence, such an appellate court will not consider an appellant's argument if it is based on a theory that is raised for the first time in the appeal.[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-14)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4d/FL5DistrictCourtAppeal_0766.JPG/225px-FL5DistrictCourtAppeal_0766.JPG)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:FL5DistrictCourtAppeal_0766.JPG)

The [Fifth District Court of Appeal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Fifth_District_Court_of_Appeal "Florida Fifth District Court of Appeal") for Florida, an intermediate appellate court for the Florida judicial system

In most U.S. states, and in U.S. federal courts, parties before the court are allowed one appeal as of right. This means that a party who is unsatisfied with the outcome of a trial may bring an [appeal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeal "Appeal") to contest that outcome.[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-United_States_Department_of_State_2023_j033-15) However, appeals may be costly, and the appellate court must find an error on the part of the court below that justifies upsetting the verdict.[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Robertson_2013-16) Therefore, only a small proportion of trial court decisions result in appeals.[\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Eisenberg_2004-17) Some courts, particularly supreme courts, have the power of [discretionary review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discretionary_review "Discretionary review"), meaning that they can decide whether they will hear an appeal brought in a particular case. [\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-United_States_Department_of_State_2023_j033-15)[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Robertson_2013-16)

#### Nomenclature

\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Appellate_court\&action=edit\&section=11 "Edit section: Nomenclature")]

Many U.S. jurisdictions title their appellate court a ***court of appeal*** or ***court of appeals***.[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Garner_Page_232-1) Both terms are used in the United States, but the plural form is more common in [American English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_English "American English"), while in contrast, [British English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_English "British English") uses only the singular form.[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Garner_Page_232-1) The correct form is whichever is the statutorily prescribed or customary form for a particular court and particular jurisdiction; in other words, one should never write "court of appeal" when the court at issue clearly prefers to be called a "court of appeals", and vice versa.[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/#cite_note-Garner_Page_232-1)

Historically, certain jurisdictions have titled their appellate court a ***court of errors*** (or ***court of errors and appeals***), on the premise that it was intended to correct errors made by lower courts. Examples of such courts include the [New Jersey Court of Errors and Appeals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_Court_of_Errors_and_Appeals "New Jersey Court of Errors and Appeals") (which existed from 1844 to 1947), the Connecticut Supreme Court of Errors (which has been renamed the [Connecticut Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connecticut_Supreme_Court "Connecticut Supreme Court")), the Kentucky Court of Errors (renamed the [Kentucky Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kentucky_Supreme_Court "Kentucky Supreme Court")), and the Mississippi High Court of Errors and Appeals (since renamed the [Supreme Court of Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Mississippi "Supreme Court of Mississippi")). In some jurisdictions, a court able to hear appeals is known as an **appellate division**.

The phrase "court of appeals" most often refers to intermediate appellate courts. However, the [New York Court of Appeals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Court_of_Appeals "New York Court of Appeals") is the highest appellate court in New York. The [New York Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Supreme_Court "New York Supreme Court") is a trial court of general jurisdiction. The [Supreme Court of Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Maryland "Supreme Court of Maryland") was known as the Court of Appeals, and the [Appellate Court of Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellate_Court_of_Maryland "Appellate Court of Maryland") was known as the Court of Special Appeals, until a 2022 constitutional amendment changed their names. Depending on the system, certain courts may serve as both trial courts and appellate courts, hearing appeals of decisions made by courts with more limited jurisdiction.
